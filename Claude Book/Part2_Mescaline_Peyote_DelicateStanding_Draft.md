# A Delicate Standing

*Part 2: The Substances — Mescaline chapter, aside*
*Status: Draft, Critic-reviewed (Excellent)*
*Pays off the bridge piece's promise, "Peyote is in a delicate place right now, both culturally and ecologically, and out of respect for what's going on with it, I've stayed in my lane. There's a chapter coming up on that, and I want to tell that story carefully."*

---

I promised earlier in this chapter that I'd tell this story carefully, so let me start by being honest about what this piece is and isn't. It isn't a guide to peyote ceremony. It isn't a description of dose or preparation, the way I gave you for San Pedro. I work with Huachuma, not peyote, and I don't have the standing to write about the inside of a tradition I haven't been invited into. What I can do is tell you, as a physician looking at the situation from outside it, why that tradition deserves more caution than curiosity right now.

Start with the plant itself. A San Pedro arm can add real height in a single growing season, and when it falls, it roots again and starts climbing once more within a year or two. Peyote does nothing of the kind. A button the size of a tennis ball, the size I mentioned a few pages back, took roughly fifteen years to get there. Lophophora williamsii grows so slowly that a patch harvested carelessly doesn't recover on any timeline a person plans around. The ecological cushion that lets San Pedro absorb mistakes simply doesn't exist for peyote.

That slow growth is colliding with a fast-rising demand. Wild peyote habitat in South Texas, nearly the only place it grows naturally in the United States, has been shrinking for decades under ranching, oil and gas development, and the kind of land conversion that doesn't ask the cactus's permission first. Layered on top of habitat loss is straightforward over-harvesting, some of it legal, increasingly more of it not. Texas currently licenses only a handful of peyoteros, the harvesters legally permitted to supply the Native American Church. One of them has described filling entire trays of peyote in her father's day and struggling now to fill a single one. That isn't a metaphor about scarcity. That's one family watching the actual math of a vanishing resource play out across two generations.

The legal protection sitting on top of all this is narrower than most people assume, and the narrowness is the point. Peyote's exemption from federal drug scheduling doesn't cover peyote use generally. It covers bona fide ceremonial use by enrolled members of federally recognized tribes, specifically through the Native American Church, codified through the 1994 amendment to the American Indian Religious Freedom Act after a Supreme Court ruling had briefly stripped that protection away entirely. No other psychedelic in this book carries a protection built this specifically, or this fought-for. It exists because one community needed it to survive a legal system that came close to taking it from them once already, not because the substance itself earned a general exemption.

I'm writing this book in the middle of a real moment for psychedelic medicine, the same moment that's made it possible for me to write candidly about psilocybin and ayahuasca and San Pedro at all. That same momentum is part of what's now pressing on peyote from the outside, through rising interest from people with no relationship to the Native American Church and no path to the legal protection that community holds. A few conservation groups are buying and replanting habitat to push back against the shrinking supply, and that work matters. But replanting takes the same fifteen years the cactus always needed, and the demand isn't waiting for it.

None of this is a reason to look away from peyote. It's the reason I've chosen, in this book, to look at it from a respectful distance instead of standing inside it the way I can with the medicine I actually practice. Some chapters in a book like this one get written from experience. This one gets written from the outside, on purpose, because that's where I belong with this particular plant.
