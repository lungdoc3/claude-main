# Knowing your exact dosage is pretty much impossible when working with Ayahuasca. Why she is so different.

*Part 2: The Substances — Ayahuasca chapter*
*Status: Draft, Critic-reviewed (Excellent)*
*Pharmahuasca aside intentionally omitted, pending separate development*

---

I have served one man more times than I can count. He is not new to this medicine. He has sat with grandmother more nights than most people reading this book ever will.

And every single time, before he drinks, he hands me a spoon. Not a soup spoon, not a measuring spoon, just an ordinary kitchen spoon he has decided, with complete confidence, is the correct instrument for delivering precisely the right amount of medicine into his body. He walks me through the meniscus. Concave or convex changes the volume, he tells me, and the volume matters. He watches me pour and corrects me if the curve of the liquid isn't exactly what he specified, and only then does he drink.

And every single time, the medicine ignores him completely. Same man, same spoon, same meniscus measured to his exact specification, and a different experience every time, sometimes wildly different, for reasons that have nothing to do with anything he was tracking so carefully.

One night the medicine took him somewhere strong. The Shipibo call it koshi, a particular intensity with a flavor all its own. He sat bolt upright in the middle of it, looked straight at me, and asked what was happening. I told him gently that he had taken ayahuasca. He looked at me for a few seconds. "Oh, right," he said, and went straight back into the journey.

He has never once adjusted the ritual. I don't think he ever will, and I'm not sure he should. Obsessing over the dose is a desire to control the experience, and there is real comfort in that ritual even when it isn't doing what he thinks it's doing. The control is a liability. We'll come back to that. The meniscus was never the variable that mattered, and the one that did is what comes next.

Conventional medicine runs on standardization. Every 250 milligram tablet of ampicillin contains the same amount of the same compound, manufactured to the same specification, tested against the same standard. We know how it enters the body, what it does once it's there, and how the body clears it. That predictability is the entire foundation of modern dosing, and it is the first thing that disappears the moment you start working with this medicine.

What gets called ayahuasca is a tea made by boiling the Banisteriopsis caapi vine with a DMT-containing plant, almost always the leaves of Chacruna. Chacruna alone carries more than DMT. It carries N-methyltryptamine, several beta-carbolines, indole alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, and a handful of nitrogen-containing compounds nobody has fully characterized. None of those concentrations are fixed. They shift with the microclimate, the macroclimate, the soil, the age of the plant, and the hour the leaves were cut, and leaves harvested at dawn carry more DMT than leaves cut in the afternoon.

The vine is no more cooperative. Harmine, harmaline, and tetrahydroharmine do most of the pharmacological work in caapi, and total alkaloid content swings from under a tenth of a percent to more than one percent of dry weight depending on the chemotype. Vine from the Pucallpa region tends to run hotter than vine from elsewhere. Combine a variable vine with variable leaves in a ratio that has never been written down anywhere because no one ever standardized it, and you get a brew that is genuinely a different substance every time it's cooked. The same maestro working from the same lineage will still produce a batch that isn't identical to the last one. The tradition has always worked this way.

Then add the cook. Two to five days at the fire, deciding by feel how far to reduce it, checking the final concentration the way one of my teachers still does, with a notch cut into a stick. There is no instrument for this. There has never needed to be one, because nobody serving this medicine for the last several thousand years was trying to hit a number.

All of that lives upstream of the cup, in the plant and the pot, before the medicine ever reaches a mouth. The harder truth, the one I had to sit with as both a physician and a man who drinks this medicine on a regular basis, is that even a hypothetically perfect batch, lab verified down to the milligram, would still not behave the same way twice. The body receiving it isn't standardized either.

MAO-A activity varies meaningfully from person to person, and that single enzyme governs how quickly the beta-carbolines in the vine clear your system, which in turn governs how long and how strongly the DMT gets to act. Gut transit time changes how much of the brew gets absorbed before it moves through. Body composition changes distribution. A history of prior journeys changes a nervous system's relationship to what's coming in ways no lab value will ever capture. Two people drinking from the same pot are not getting the same dose, and the same person drinking from that pot two nights apart isn't either. The real variable was never the cup. It was everyone who ever held it.

This is why there's no floor button to push. Every variable above, the leaf, the vine, the cook, the body drinking it, is the reason the department store I described earlier holds up better here than anywhere else in this book. Ayahuasca doesn't just offer you a different floor depending on the dose. She rearranges the floors while you're still walking the halls of the last one.

A Maestro once told me, "We just open the door. The medicine does what she needs to do. Just a crack, just a crack." That has stayed with me longer than almost anything else anyone has said to me in this work. If we stick with the known and the predictable, we will be stuck with the known and the predictable. If we knew or could predict exactly what we needed to heal, I suspect we would have done it by now.

You can hand me the most carefully measured spoon in the Amazon. She was never going to read it anyway.
