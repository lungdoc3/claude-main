# Introducing Psychedelic Healing

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

Modern medicine was built on a foundation of Newtonian physics, and it has never fully left that foundation.

This is not a criticism. It is a description. When medicine was developing its core framework, the scientific model available was deterministic and mechanistic: identify the cause, apply the fix, observe the effect. The world, at that level of analysis, behaved predictably. A pathogen invades, you eliminate it. A bone breaks, you set it. A vessel occludes, you open it. Cause, intervention, outcome. The model worked extraordinarily well for a very large category of problems, and it built the institutions, the language, the training, and the entire epistemology of modern medicine around its assumptions.

The assumption at the center of it: the body is a machine, and broken machines can be repaired by identifying and correcting the broken part.

For physical breakdown, this model remains powerful and should be celebrated for what it is. When a leg is broken, the language stays precise and impersonal. A leg is broken. The patient is not. The person remains intact while the body is addressed.

The problem emerged when medicine tried to apply the same logic to the mind.

## Phineas Gage and a Very Inconvenient Skull

In 1848, a railroad foreman named Phineas Gage survived a tamping iron blasting through his frontal lobe. Physically, he recovered. The man who emerged, however, was someone his friends and family no longer recognized. Responsible, measured, socially competent Phineas was replaced by someone impulsive, profane, and unable to maintain commitments or relationships. His physician noted that the equilibrium between his intellectual faculties and his animal propensities seemed to have been destroyed.

It took a iron rod through a man's skull to force medicine to begin taking seriously what it should have been obvious: the brain does not merely process information. It generates personality, behavior, emotional life, and the entire subjective experience of being a person.

Before Gage, the prevailing instinct was to treat the mind as something separate from the body, beyond the reach of physical intervention. After Gage, that position became harder to defend. The brain was biological, and what it produced could be altered by physical events.

This was progress. But the Newtonian framework that absorbed this insight bent it in a particular direction: if the brain is biological, then brain problems are biological problems, and biological problems have biological fixes. More sophisticated machinery. Same logic.

## Physics Ran Into the Same Wall

In the early twentieth century, physicists hit a boundary that their existing models could not explain. At the subatomic level, matter stopped behaving the way matter was supposed to behave. Particles existed in superposition. Observation affected outcomes. Causality became probabilistic rather than deterministic. The equations that had described the macro world with extraordinary precision simply did not work at this scale.

The response of physics was not to insist that the subatomic world must conform to the existing model. The response was to build new models. New mathematics. New language. An entirely new conceptual framework capable of describing a domain that operated by different rules than the world Newton had mapped.

Medicine is standing at an equivalent crossroads and has not yet made the same move.

The domain of psychological suffering, of faulty brain output, of the vast territory of human distress that does not resolve into a broken part with a corresponding fix: this domain does not behave the way the Newtonian model predicts. Decades of pharmaceutical intervention have produced marginal improvements in outcomes for depression, anxiety, trauma, and addiction, while the prevalence of all of them continues to rise. The model is not accurately predicting the observations.

If medicine wants to continue identifying as a science, it needs to apply the scientific method to itself. When the model fails to predict the observations, the model must be updated or replaced. Insisting on the square peg is not scientific rigor. It is attachment to a framework past its expiration date.

## Two Different Problems

Before introducing what psychedelics actually do, a distinction needs to be established clearly, because the failure to make it is responsible for most of the confusion in this space.

There is a fundamental difference between physical body breakdown and faulty brain output.

A failing kidney is a broken part. The organ is not performing its biological function and the solution, dialysis, transplant, medication, is applied to the organ. The person's experience of themselves is not the therapeutic target.

Faulty brain output is different in kind, not just degree. The brain is not broken in the structural sense. It is producing output, thoughts, narratives, emotional responses, behavioral patterns, that are not serving the person and that the person experiences as themselves. Not as something happening to them. As what they are.

This is the collapse that changes everything. When the physical body is affected, the language stays impersonal. A lung is inflamed. A heart rhythm is unstable. In the ICU, when a patient becomes delirious, we do not say the patient has become the delirium. We understand that the brain is temporarily dysregulated. The person remains distinct from the malfunction.

But when psychological distress arises in ordinary waking life, we abandon this distinction quietly and completely. Anxious thoughts become I am anxious. Depressive narratives become I am broken. The experience stops being something occurring within the system and becomes the system itself. This collapse is rarely questioned. It profoundly shapes how healing is pursued. And it is precisely what makes the Newtonian fix-the-broken-part approach so ineffective in this domain: you cannot repair something that the patient has become.

## What Psychedelics Actually Address

Psychedelics address brain output issues. Not broken parts. Not chemistry that simply needs rebalancing. The patterns, the loops, the deeply embedded predictions the brain is running below the level of conscious access that are generating the suffering.

And they do this not by replacing the output with something better, the way an antidepressant attempts to, but by temporarily disrupting the mechanism that keeps the old output locked in place. Creating a window. In that window, the system can update itself in ways that are not available under ordinary conditions.

This is the insight that requires new models and new language, the same way subatomic physics required new models and new language. The therapeutic agent is not simply the compound. It is the compound plus the context plus the person plus what happens in the days and weeks after the window opens. You cannot run those variables through a Newtonian equation and get a meaningful result.

Psychedelics are not the cure. They are the conditions under which the system becomes capable of healing itself. That distinction is not semantic. It determines everything about how this work must be approached, held, and understood.

## The Crossroads

Physics built quantum mechanics because it had to. The observations demanded it. The old framework, however elegant, could not account for what was actually happening at the level where it mattered.

Medicine is being handed the same demand.

The territory we are entering, the domain of consciousness, of identity, of the stories the brain generates and mistakes for reality, operates by different rules than the macro world the Newtonian model was built to describe. Treating it with the same tools, the same language, the same epistemological assumptions, will continue to produce the same inadequate results.

What is required is not the abandonment of rigor. It is the expansion of the framework to include the domain it has been refusing to map.

The map and the territory are not the same thing. Where we go next is about learning to tell the difference.
