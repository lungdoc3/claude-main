# Phillip and Wachuma

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

My son Phillip was about to start Harvard.

I figured that was as good a time as any to shake things up.

He had watched me for years. The breathwork study, the retreats we held at the house, the apprenticeship with the Q'ero shamans, the Mesa, the ceremonies. He was always supportive in the way that a grounded, intelligent kid is supportive of his father's unusual pursuits: genuinely, from a distance, without any particular need to make it his own. None of that was real to him. Nor should it have been. It wasn't his time yet.

I arranged for him to travel to Peru with me just before he left. Two nights with Grandmother Ayahuasca in the Happy Valley, which deserves its own several stories and will get them. A few days to breathe and explore. Then back to Cusco to meet one of my Q'ero brothers for a Wachuma ceremony.

## The Plateau

We headed out early the next morning, cab through Cusco and up onto the high plateau that rings the city, a landscape scattered with the stone remnants of civilizations that predate the Inca. The altitude sits on your chest in a way that makes everything feel slightly more deliberate. We set up on a high vista with Apu Ausangate behind us, the mountain massive and unhurried in the way that things are when they have been present for millions of years and are not particularly concerned about your schedule.

I opened my Mesa and held the space. We lit a small fire, the Q'ero shaman's preferred instrument, and held a despacho ceremony: prayers and gratitude woven into an offering, the smoke carrying them upward in the way that smoke does in traditions that understood fire long before we did. The medicine warmed over the flame. We drank.

My Q'ero brother moved through the morning playing his ceremony flute, and the music did what ceremony music does: it oriented us, held the container, reminded our nervous systems what kind of day this was going to be.

The day progressed exactly as Wachuma days do when you stop trying to direct them: wandering, attending, following curiosity rather than agenda. The plateau has a particular quality under Wachuma's influence. The stone structures surface differently. The sky becomes more itself. You become more aware that you are standing on ground that has held human feet for an incomprehensibly long time, and that the feet before yours were doing something not entirely different from what you are doing now.

## The Sheep

After several hours our wandering became more focused, the way things under Wachuma sometimes do when something wants your attention. My Q'ero brother led us off the top of the vista and down the side of the hill along what might generously be called a trail.

Phillip was behind me when we came around a corner and stopped.

Just around the bend, where there had been nothing but high plateau scrub for the entire morning, was what I can only describe as an oasis. Lush green grass. Trees. A small herd of sheep. And watching over them, an ageless woman.

Ageless in the specific sense I mean when I use that word in this context: not from present time ageless. Not simply old. Belonging to a different temporal register entirely. When you have done this work long enough you learn to recognize these moments. They stand out, which is the whole point. They catch your attention the way a rent in fabric catches the light differently than the surrounding material. Call it a portal. Call it Maya. Call it a rent in the matrix. The name matters less than the recognition: this is not ordinary. Pay attention.

My brain, for reasons I will not overthink, immediately offered me Pan. Greek. Shepherd. Pipes.

I was briefly confused about when the Greeks had made it to the Peruvian altiplano, and I found this both deeply interesting and genuinely hilarious, and I turned to share it with Phillip.

He was completely frozen.

Not frightened. Frozen in the specific way that someone is frozen when they are receiving something they have no existing category for. His attention was locked on the woman, the sheep, the impossible green grass. Transfixed. Enamored. Completely and entirely hooked.

Which was, of course, precisely why Wachuma had arranged it.

This is how he works. He does not come at you directly. He does not announce himself or make demands. He finds the precise image, the exact moment, the specific arrangement of reality that your particular attention cannot move past, and he places it in front of you. And when your attention stops, he walks through the door that your stopping opened. The hook was for Phillip. It worked immediately and completely.

## The Afternoon

What followed was several hours that I can describe only from the outside, because Phillip was somewhere I did not have full visibility into and I was somewhere that kept me too occupied to watch him closely.

I was deeply at home. The Mesa, the Q'ero practices, the shaman moving through the landscape with his flute: these things tie me to something ancient in a way that feels less like memory and more like recognition. I was with my people, in my landscape, doing the work I have been doing for long enough that it stopped feeling like something I do and started feeling like something I am.

What I noticed of Phillip, in the intervals: he moved through the rest of the afternoon with a quality of attention that was different from how he had started the day. He would stop at one of the stone structures and study it the way you study something you are trying to understand rather than simply observe. Then he would look at me, then back to wherever the other part of his attention was. He was tracking two things simultaneously, and whatever the second thing was, it had his full respect.

We moved down the hillside slowly, following the trail the way you follow something you do not need to have followed for the first time to know how to follow. Same walk. Same thirst. Same dust. The kind of continuity that Wachuma specializes in making available: the sense that you have been doing this exact thing, in some form, for much longer than one lifetime accounts for.

## The Bus Stop

And then we came around the final corner.

One step. The trail ended and a residential street began. Pavement. A bus stop. And ten minutes later, a bus.

Just that fast. One world to another. Ancient to present. Then to now.

I have made that kind of transition enough times that I have developed a certain competence at it. The shift is real and it can be jarring and I have learned to move through it with something like grace. I checked Phillip.

He looked confused. Not distressed. Confused in the specific way of someone dealing with something for which they have no model, which is different from confusion about something that simply needs more information. This was structural. His framework for what was real had encountered something it was not built to process.

We rode the bus back into Cusco and he began, gradually, to find language for what had happened.

After the sheep, he said, he had almost immediately begun to feel the presence of another reality running just underneath this one. Not replacing it. Underneath it. And he had begun to shift between them without being able to control the direction, which had been disorienting until he remembered something Ayahuasca had shown him two nights before: a lesson about the cost of trying to control things. He had let go of the steering. Stopped trying to stay in one or the other and allowed himself to be in both. And at some point the seam between them disappeared.

He sat with that for a moment.

Then, playful in the way that people are playful when they are genuinely uncertain whether they are asking a serious question, he said:

*Was that real? Or is this real?*

I thought about it.

That is as good a place to start as anywhere, I told him.

---

Integration is not always a formal process. It does not always happen in a dedicated session with a trained therapist in a carefully held container. Sometimes it happens on a bus coming down from a plateau outside Cusco, when a young man who is about to start one of the best universities in the world is quietly working through the fact that the reality he arrived in Peru with is no longer the only one he has access to.

The container for that afternoon was as loose as containers get. No maloca. No ceremony songs. No careful preparation of the space. Just a high plateau, an ancient shaman with a flute, a Mesa, a fire, and Wachuma doing what Wachuma does when you stop trying to plan the day.

Phillip did not need a formal container. He needed a sheep. An ageless woman. A corner to come around.

And a father who recognized what was happening and knew enough to stay out of the way.

The question he asked on that bus is one I have been turning over for years in various forms. It is, in the end, the question this entire book is circling. Not which reality is real. But what we lose, and what becomes possible, when we stop assuming we already know the answer.
