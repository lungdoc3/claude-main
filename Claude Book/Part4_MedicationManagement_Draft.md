# Medication Management Before Ceremony
## Part 4 — Clinical Safety Section

*[Note: This section addresses one of the most common and most preventable safety failures in retreat and ceremony settings — the blanket instruction to stop all prescription medications before sitting. First-pass draft in Randy's voice. Randy POV placeholder at end.]*

---

**Medication Management Before Ceremony**

Here is what happens far too often.

A retreat is booked. It might be a single overnight ceremony or a week-long immersion with four sessions. At some point in the intake process, the guest is asked to complete a medical history. Except it is not really a medical history. It is closer to a getting-to-know-you form with a section that says something like "please list all medications you currently take."

Which sounds reasonable. Until you look at what actually gets written in that box.

The guest probably does not know the name of their prescriptions. They might know one of them is "the little blue one" and another "the one for sleep." They may not know the dose. They almost certainly do not know the mechanism — why this drug, at this dose, for this particular condition. And they probably do not think that some of what they take qualifies as a prescription drug in the sense the form is asking about. It is something they take occasionally. It helps them wind down. It is not a big deal.

That is how someone ends up not disclosing their trazodone.

Trazodone is not a big deal in the way most people think about prescription drugs. It is commonly prescribed for sleep, often in low doses, often mentioned almost as an afterthought at the end of an appointment. The guest does not think of themselves as someone who takes an antidepressant. They think of themselves as someone who uses something to help them sleep on occasion.

Trazodone is a serotonin antagonist and reuptake inhibitor with documented activity at the 5-HT2A receptor. That is the exact receptor the psychedelic medicine needs to do its work. No one in that intake process flagged it. The guest did not know it mattered. The form did not know what to do with the answer even if it had been given.

One more layer worth naming. A meaningful number of people take medications that are not technically theirs. A partner's leftover anxiety prescription used occasionally. A friend's sleep aid grabbed for a difficult stretch. A family member's blood pressure medication sampled when their own ran out. These people will answer "no" to questions about prescription medications and mean it. In their mind, they do not take prescription drugs. They are not lying. They are operating with a definition of "their medications" that does not match the clinical one. The form will not catch this. Only a direct, non-judgmental conversation with someone who knows what questions to ask has a chance of surfacing it.

Here is who knew what any of this meant. Their doctor.

---

**The Rule That Traveled Wrong**

The "no prescription medications" policy at many retreat centers did not arrive through clinical reasoning. It arrived through transmission. Indigenous healers and the lineages they carry have always worked with patients who were not on prescription medications, because in that cosmology and that era, prescription medications did not exist. The ideal ceremonial participant, from that frame, arrives unmedicated, unencumbered, and pharmacologically clean. That is a coherent framework for the world it was built in.

Marge from Ohio lives in a different world.

When a retreat center tells Marge to stop her medications before ceremony, they are applying a rule designed for a context that no longer fits. The intention is not malicious. The reasoning is often sincere. But intentions do not counteract pharmacology, and sincerity does not prevent rebound hypertension.

---

**When Stopping Is More Dangerous Than Staying**

When Marge stops her two blood pressure medications two weeks before ceremony, her blood pressure is no longer controlled. In many patients, abrupt discontinuation of antihypertensives does not simply return them to their pre-medication baseline. It produces a rebound effect. Blood pressure rises above where it was before treatment began. Marge arrives at ceremony already elevated and labile, then ingests a medicine with documented cardiovascular stimulation that will raise it further.

Why is Marge on those medications? If she has essential hypertension managed with an ACE inhibitor, that is one clinical picture. If she has paroxysmal atrial tachycardia managed with a beta-blocker, that is an entirely different one. A heart rate of 160 firing mid-ceremony, unmedicated, is not a manageable inconvenience. It is a medical emergency. The rule that told Marge to stop her medications did not ask which ones or why.

The principle holds across drug classes. Stopping medications without medical guidance introduces risks that are frequently more serious than the interaction risks the rule was designed to avoid.

---

**The SSRI Problem**

Antidepressants are where this issue becomes most acute. SSRIs are among the most commonly prescribed medications in the western world and among the most clinically relevant to psychedelic work. A participant on a long-term SSRI cannot sit in ceremony with that medication on board. The experience will be significantly blunted or absent. The pharmacological reasons are covered in the pharmaceutical section. The clinical question here is what to do about it.

Some retreat centers and facilitators tell the participant to stop the SSRI. They offer general guidance on tapering, or no guidance at all, with the assumption that the guest will work it out before they arrive.

This is not acceptable. It is not safe. And it is not the facilitator's clinical domain.

SSRI discontinuation requires a taper calibrated to the specific drug, the dose, how long the patient has been on it, and their clinical history. Discontinuation syndrome is real, documented, and distressing. It includes dizziness, electric shock sensations, severe mood instability, and in some cases worsening depression. The participant who develops discontinuation syndrome three weeks before their retreat has no one to call. Their prescribing physician does not know they are tapering. Their facilitator is not a physician. Their retreat center is not a medical facility.

When discontinuation-related depression worsens, or when the participant loses the stability the medication was providing, the question of who bears clinical responsibility is not abstract. Facilitators who guide participants through medication changes they are not clinically qualified to manage are not being helpful. They are assuming liability and risk that belongs in a physician's hands.

---

**The Only Appropriate Path**

Prescription medications should be discontinued only by the prescribing physician or under their direct guidance. This is not bureaucratic preference. It is basic clinical safety.

That means participants need to have the conversation with their physician. For many people, this feels impossible. They assume the doctor will dismiss them, judge them, or refuse to engage with anything involving psychedelics. That assumption is increasingly outdated as physician awareness of this field grows. And even in cases where it is accurate, the answer is not to avoid the conversation. The answer is to find a physician who can have it.

A physician who penalizes a patient for asking an honest question about their medications is not serving that patient's interests. Patients deserve physicians they can talk to. If the current one cannot provide that, finding one who can is not a radical act. It is reasonable self-advocacy.

A physician who understands the psychedelic space will look at a medication list and know what to do with it. They can determine which drugs require a taper, over what timeline, and which ones are non-negotiable because stopping them creates more risk than the ceremony does. That guidance exists. It is increasingly available. It is what every participant deserves.

The person who cannot have that conversation, stops their medication on their own, and ends up in crisis three weeks before ceremony, or in the middle of one, is not experiencing an unfortunate outcome. They are experiencing a preventable one. The information needed to prevent it was always available. It lived in their doctor's office.
