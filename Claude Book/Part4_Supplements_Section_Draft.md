### Natural MAOIs — The Hidden Category

This is the most underappreciated supplement danger in the psychedelic space, and it warrants its own subsection. Several common, widely available, entirely legal supplements contain beta-carboline alkaloids with documented monoamine oxidase inhibitory activity. Most users have no idea.

---

#### Passionflower (Passiflora incarnata)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Capsules, tinctures, teas — widely sold as a sleep and anxiety supplement |
| **Active compounds** | Harman, harmine, harmaline, harmol, harmalol — beta-carboline alkaloids |
| **Mechanism** | MAO-A inhibition. Harmine specifically inhibits MAO-A without inhibiting MAO-B — the same selectivity as some pharmaceutical MAOIs. Reduces serotonin degradation; slows psilocin clearance. |
| **Clinical effects** | Documented to prolong, deepen, and intensify psychedelic effects — to the point that it is deliberately used in "psiloflora" ceremonies. This is not benign amplification; it is pharmacological potentiation of unpredictable magnitude. Serotonin syndrome risk. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 2–3 (harmine content is chemically documented; MAO inhibition is pharmacologically established; clinical human data are anecdotal/community) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Hard contraindication. Discontinue minimum 5–7 days before. Often perceived as "gentle" and "natural" — this is precisely the danger.** |

---

#### Rhodiola Rosea (Arctic Root)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Capsules; sold as an adaptogen for stress and cognitive performance |
| **Active compounds** | Salidroside, rosavins |
| **Mechanism** | In vitro studies demonstrate >80% MAO-A and MAO-B inhibition at standard concentrations. Salidroside also inhibits serotonin reuptake and influences serotonin transport across the blood-brain barrier. Combined: elevated synaptic serotonin + reduced breakdown. |
| **Clinical effects** | Theoretical serotonin syndrome risk. Additive serotonergic load on top of psilocin's own weak serotonin reuptake inhibition. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 2–3 (in vitro MAO inhibition is well-documented; human clinical data on SS risk with psychedelics are absent) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid in the lead-up to ceremony. Particularly important given its wide adoption in the high-performance and wellness communities — exactly the population likely to sit with psilocybin.** |

### Serotonin Precursors and Modulators

---

#### L-Tryptophan

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Capsules; sold for sleep, mood, and protein support |
| **Mechanism** | Direct serotonin precursor — one step upstream from 5-HTP. The metabolic chain: L-tryptophan → 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) → serotonin. Taking L-tryptophan raises the serotonin synthesis floor. Combined with psilocin's 5-HT2A agonism and weak reuptake inhibition, the serotonergic burden increases. |
| **Clinical effects** | Theoretical serotonin excess. Risk compounds if combined with other serotonergic supplements or medications. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (metabolic pathway is established; specific psilocybin + L-tryptophan human data are absent) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid. Treat similarly to 5-HTP.** |

---

#### SAMe (S-Adenosylmethionine)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Capsules; sold for liver health, joint support, and mood — increasingly popular as a "natural antidepressant" |
| **Mechanism** | SAMe is a methyl donor involved in the synthesis of serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. It raises monoamine levels and has demonstrated antidepressant properties in clinical trials. Theoretically, combining SAMe's monoamine-raising effects with psilocin's 5-HT2A agonism elevates serotonin syndrome risk. SAMe has a short half-life (~100 minutes), so complete clearance before a session is achievable. |
| **Clinical effects** | Theoretical serotonin syndrome — particularly when combined with other serotonergic agents. Risk is amplified in polypharmacy contexts. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Discontinue 24 hours before. Short half-life means this is manageable with simple timing.** |

---

#### Methylene Blue

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Increasingly marketed as a "nootropic" and cognitive longevity supplement; available in drops and capsules |
| **Mechanism** | **This one requires emphasis.** Methylene blue is a potent MAO inhibitor — a fact buried under its nootropic marketing. It also inhibits nitric oxide synthase and has mitochondrial effects. The MAOI activity is well-documented and serious: methylene blue has been involved in documented serotonin syndrome cases in surgical patients who received it intraoperatively. The FDA issued a warning about methylene blue and serotonin syndrome in 2011. |
| **Clinical effects** | Serotonin syndrome risk. The interaction is not theoretical — there are case reports and a published systematic review (Stanford, PubMed 20484716). |
| **Evidence** | Tier 1–2 (FDA warning; systematic review; clinical case reports — albeit not from psychedelic-specific studies) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Hard contraindication. The nootropic marketing of methylene blue obscures a drug-level interaction risk. This is one of the most important supplements to screen for explicitly.** |

### Stimulant-Class Supplements

---

#### Kratom (Mitragyna speciosa)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Powder, capsules, shots; sold legally as a supplement in most US states |
| **Mechanism** | Complex: partial opioid receptor agonism + serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibition + significant CYP enzyme inhibition (CYP1A2, CYP2D6, CYP3A4). The CYP inhibition is particularly relevant: it could elevate psilocin plasma levels by slowing its metabolism. The serotonergic component adds pharmacodynamic risk. Serotonin syndrome cases with kratom + antidepressants are documented in the peer-reviewed literature (Eudaley et al., 2023, Therapeutic Advances in Drug Safety; reported in Critical Care Medicine 2024). |
| **Clinical effects** | Serotonin syndrome risk. Potential pharmacokinetic elevation of psilocin levels via CYP inhibition. CNS depression from opioid-receptor activity may also produce unpredictable combined effects. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 2–3 (SS with kratom + serotonergic drugs: Tier 2; specific psilocybin + kratom: Tier 3) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid. Kratom occupies a regulatory gray zone that gives it the appearance of safety — it is not.** |

---

#### Yohimbine / Yohimbe Bark Extract

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Capsules, commonly in "fat burner" and pre-workout supplements — often as an unlabeled component in proprietary blends |
| **Mechanism** | Alpha-2 adrenergic receptor antagonist. Blocks negative feedback on norepinephrine release, producing significant sympathetic activation: elevated heart rate, blood pressure, and anxiety. Psilocybin independently elevates BP and HR (~19 mmHg systolic). Combined cardiovascular stimulation may be clinically significant. Additionally, yohimbine has documented anxiogenic properties — it exacerbates anxiety in predisposed individuals — which is precisely the wrong addition to a psychedelic session. |
| **Clinical effects** | Amplified hypertension and tachycardia. Significantly worsened anxiety, panic, and paranoia during the experience. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (cardiovascular pharmacology is well-characterized; specific psilocybin combination is mechanistic extrapolation) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid — including scrutiny of any "fat burner" or pre-workout product for yohimbine content, which is frequently undisclosed.** |

---

### Nootropics and Cognitive Supplements

---

#### Ginkgo Biloba

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Capsules; widely used for cognitive support and circulation |
| **Mechanism** | Clarification from the research: despite early in vitro suggestions of MAO inhibition, a PET imaging study in humans found ginkgo extract does NOT significantly inhibit MAO-A or MAO-B in the living human brain. Chronic ginkgo use does appear to affect norepinephrine reuptake (mild). Additionally, ginkgo inhibits platelet aggregation — relevant because psilocybin causes mild vasoconstriction. |
| **Clinical effects** | MAO interaction is weaker than often claimed. Mild NE-modulating effects. Antiplatelet effect is the more clinically relevant concern in combination with psilocybin's vascular effects. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 2 (human PET imaging study on MAO; well-established antiplatelet effect) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Lower concern than often cited for MAO interaction; worth noting the antiplatelet effect. Not a hard contraindication.** |

---

#### Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Capsules, powder; trendy nootropic; frequently co-marketed with psilocybin in microdosing protocols |
| **Mechanism** | Stimulates Nerve Growth Factor (NGF) and BDNF. No significant serotonergic activity identified. No pharmacodynamic interaction with psilocin expected at the receptor level. |
| **Clinical effects** | No significant interaction with psilocin's mechanism identified. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (absence of expected interaction) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Low concern for direct pharmacological interaction. Its co-marketing in microdosing stacks with psilocybin is not evidence of safety in macrodose ceremony contexts, but the mechanism does not suggest danger.** |

---

#### Melatonin

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Form** | Capsules, gummies; ubiquitous sleep supplement |
| **Mechanism** | Melatonin receptor agonist (MT1/MT2); synthesized from serotonin via N-acetyltransferase. Some evidence that psilocybin and related compounds influence pineal melatonin release. No significant pharmacodynamic collision with psilocin at standard doses. |
| **Clinical effects** | No well-documented serious interaction. May alter experiential quality by influencing circadian and pineal signaling. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 4 |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Low concern at standard doses. Avoid on the day of and day after ceremony as a general precaution; not a meaningful contraindication.** |

**The Problem with "Natural"**

If the pharmaceutical interactions section made you anxious, this one is trickier. With prescription medications, there is at least a label. A physician wrote the prescription. A pharmacist dispensed it. The compound is what it says it is, in the dose it claims.

Supplements are a different animal entirely.

I am not hostile to supplements. Many of them have real effects, genuine biological activity, and legitimate uses. The problem is not the compounds themselves. The problem is the industry that delivers them — an industry operating under a regulatory framework so permissive that the burden of proof has essentially been reversed. Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994, manufacturers do not need to prove safety or efficacy before putting a product on the market. The FDA can intervene after the fact, after people have been harmed, if it can marshal the resources and evidence to do so. Pre-market approval is not required. This is not a conspiracy. It is the law.

What that means in practice: in a peer-reviewed study of 30 weight-loss dietary supplements, 83% had inaccurate labels. Twenty-four of those thirty were legally misbranded. Seven contained hidden components — substances present in the product not listed anywhere on the label. Some of those hidden substances were pharmaceutical drugs.

Read that again. Active pharmaceutical compounds, in supplements, unlisted.

This is not an isolated finding. It is a documented, recurring pattern across supplement categories. The FDA's own enforcement data shows a 46% increase in manufacturing compliance violations from 2023 to 2024. These are not edge cases from disreputable operators. They are industry-wide findings.

The category that deserves particular scrutiny is what the industry calls *proprietary blends* — formulations that list a mix of ingredients but are not required to disclose the amount of each individual component. The label might say "Adaptogenic Focus Blend — 800mg" and list eight ingredients underneath. You have no idea whether any of those ingredients are present in a therapeutically active dose, a negligible trace, or a dose that would concern a physician. The blend is proprietary. Transparency is optional.

For someone preparing for any psychedelic experience, this is not an academic problem.

---

**The Categories That Matter**

The classical psychedelics — psilocybin, ayahuasca, mescaline — all act primarily through the serotonin system. That shared pharmacology means several supplement categories create meaningful risk across the board, regardless of which medicine you are working with. Let me name them plainly.

*Serotonin precursors.* These are supplements that directly raise the amount of serotonin your body produces. 5-HTP is the most commonly used. L-tryptophan is one step further upstream in the same metabolic chain. SAMe is a methyl donor that participates in monoamine synthesis more broadly. All three raise the serotonergic floor before adding a serotonin-active psychedelic on top. The risk — particularly when multiple serotonergic inputs are present simultaneously — is serotonin syndrome: a potentially dangerous excess of serotonergic activity that produces agitation, rapid heart rate, elevated blood pressure, fever, and in severe cases, seizures and death. The theoretical risk from any one of these supplements alone is modest. The risk in combination is harder to bound. We will address the specific picture for each medicine in its own section.

*Natural MAO inhibitors.* This is the category most people have never considered and the one I find most clinically concerning. Monoamine oxidase (MAO) is an enzyme that breaks down serotonin and related compounds. Pharmaceutical MAO inhibitors are well-known to interact dangerously with psychedelics — we covered them in the previous section. What most people do not know is that several common, widely sold supplements contain compounds that inhibit MAO at pharmacologically meaningful levels.

Passionflower is sold as a sleep and anxiety supplement in every health food store and most pharmacies. It contains harmine and related beta-carboline alkaloids — the same class of compounds found in the ayahuasca vine — and these are documented MAO-A inhibitors. The fact that you can buy passionflower tea at Whole Foods does not change what it does in your nervous system.

Rhodiola rosea — the adaptogen beloved by the performance and biohacking communities, marketed for stress resilience and mental clarity — has demonstrated greater than 80% MAO-A and MAO-B inhibition in human nerve terminal studies. This is not a minor effect. This is potent enzyme inhibition from a supplement most people consider entirely benign.

Then there is methylene blue. If you follow the longevity and nootropics space, you have heard of it. Methylene blue is marketed as a mitochondrial enhancer and cognitive booster, and some of that marketing is grounded in real science. What the marketing reliably omits is that methylene blue is a documented MAO inhibitor — documented clearly enough that the FDA issued a safety warning about it in 2011 after serotonin syndrome cases emerged in surgical patients who received it intraoperatively. A published systematic review documented multiple cases of serotonin syndrome associated with methylene blue. This is not theoretical. The nootropic branding is doing significant work here to obscure a drug-level interaction risk.

These three represent a category of supplement that most facilitators have never heard of and most participants do not think to mention. They are the naturalistic equivalent of walking in on an MAOI.

*[CALL-OUT — Ayahuasca:] The natural MAOI story is considerably more serious in the context of ayahuasca, which already contains its own beta-carboline MAO inhibitors as a pharmacologically essential component. Combining an ayahuasca ceremony with additional botanical MAO inhibitors — passionflower, rhodiola, Syrian rue — adds unpredictable potentiation to a system that is already running hot. We address this in detail in the ayahuasca section.*

*Stimulant-class supplements.* Two deserve specific mention. Kratom — sold legally as a supplement in most states — combines partial opioid receptor activity with serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibition and significant inhibition of the CYP liver enzymes responsible for metabolizing many psychoactive compounds. Serotonin syndrome cases involving kratom and serotonergic medications are documented in peer-reviewed literature. Yohimbine — an alpha-2 adrenergic antagonist found in many fat-burner and pre-workout products, often without prominent labeling — drives significant sympathetic nervous system activation. All of the classical psychedelics elevate blood pressure and heart rate to some degree. Adding yohimbine to that picture amplifies the cardiovascular load and adds pronounced anxiogenic effects. The wrong addition to any psychedelic experience.

*[CALL-OUT — Mescaline / Peyote:] Cardiovascular stimulation is more pronounced with mescaline than with psilocybin, making stimulant-class supplements a higher priority concern in that context. See the mescaline section for specifics.]*

*St. John's Wort* deserves a mention here that it did not fully receive in the pharmaceutical section. It inhibits serotonin reuptake with SSRI-like activity and induces several liver enzymes. The combined serotonergic load with any classical psychedelic is the primary concern. Discontinuation at least five to seven days before any ceremony is warranted given its active compounds' half-lives.

**The Compounding Problem**

There is one more layer, and it is the one I see most often in the people who come to this work.

The people most likely to sit with psychedelics in a therapeutic or intentional context are, in my experience, also the people most likely to take a lot of supplements. This is the health-conscious, self-optimizing, high-functioning population. These are not people who take nothing. They take adaptogens and nootropics and sleep stacks and hormone support and pre-workout formulations and whatever their functional medicine doctor or biohacker podcast recommended last month.

Each of those supplements may be individually below the threshold of concern. The combination may not be.

Serotonergic load accumulates. L-tryptophan raises the serotonin synthesis floor. SAMe raises monoamine levels. Rhodiola inhibits MAO. St. John's Wort inhibits serotonin reuptake. None of these alone will necessarily cause a dangerous interaction with a classical psychedelic. Stack two or three of them, and you are no longer talking about theoretical concerns. You are talking about a cumulative serotonergic burden with no clear ceiling and no clinical trial that has studied it.

The honest answer to "is my supplement stack safe with this medicine?" is often: I don't know, because no one has studied it, because the combinations are effectively infinite, and because the supplement labeling laws make it impossible to know with certainty what you are actually taking.

---

**What to Actually Do**

This is not an argument to stop taking all supplements forever. That is neither realistic nor the point.

The point is this: in the weeks before any psychedelic experience, approach your supplement cabinet the same way you would approach it before surgery. You would not take your full stack the morning of an operation without disclosing it to your anesthesiologist. The reasons are identical — the compounds have biological effects, those effects interact with what is about to happen in your nervous system, and the combination is harder to manage than either component alone.

In practical terms: read labels carefully, and treat "proprietary blend" as a flag requiring investigation. If you cannot identify what you are taking and at what dose, you cannot assess the interaction risk.

The supplements carrying the most meaningful pre-ceremony concern are serotonin precursors (5-HTP, L-tryptophan, SAMe), MAO-inhibiting botanicals (passionflower, rhodiola — and Syrian rue if you encounter it, which we address in the ayahuasca section), and supplements sold under nootropic or performance branding that carry pharmacological weight most users do not appreciate (methylene blue, kratom, yohimbine). These warrant discontinuation well in advance — a minimum of several days to a week for compounds with longer half-lives.

Talk to your facilitator about everything you take. Not just prescription medications. Everything. A facilitator who does not ask about supplements is leaving a gap in the screening process that matters.

And if your supplement stack is complex enough that you genuinely cannot assess the interactions — bring it to a physician who understands both pharmacology and the psychedelic space. That intersection is exactly what this section of the book is for.

The plant medicines are old. The supplement industry is new. Their combination has not been studied. They deserve the same respect.
