# Maybe Wrong, but Never in Doubt

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

I trained at Harvard. If you want to find the largest collection of outsized egos on the planet, that is a reasonable place to start looking.

I have worked alongside physicians who were, by any ordinary social standard, absolute assholes. Arrogant, imperious, constitutionally incapable of admitting uncertainty. The kind of men and women you would not choose to have dinner with under any circumstances.

I referred my patients to them anyway. I told my patients plainly: I am not sending you to find a new friend. I am sending you to the person most likely to get you out of that operating room alive.

I meant it. I still mean it.

There is something in the outsized medical ego that is not just tolerable but genuinely useful. Think about what the job actually requires. To go toe to toe with death, you have to believe, on some level, that you can win. That belief is not rational. Death has an undefeated record. The physician who has looked honestly at the odds and retained the conviction that they are going to beat them anyway is operating from a productive delusion. The ego is what makes that possible. The ego that says: not today. Not this patient. Not on my watch.

Does it lead you astray? Of course it does. Ego distorts risk estimation. It can make you attempt what should not be attempted and keep going when you should have stopped. These are real costs and they produce real harm. But I will put my money on the big ego to get you out of trouble in the OR. I have seen what the alternative looks like and it is not better.

The medical ego does have one redeeming quality. It does not pretend to be something it is not.

The asshole surgeon is identifiable as an asshole. The ego is visible, loud, and honest about what it is. Everyone in the room knows who the ego belongs to and roughly how large it is. The patient may not like it. The nurses definitely do not like it. But no one mistakes it for humility. No one is confused about what they are dealing with.

The ego and the patient also want the same thing. The surgeon's need to win and the patient's need to survive are pointed in the same direction. The ego's hunger for victory is, in that context, the patient's best asset.

Medicine has also spent a century building structures designed to manage the ego's known pathologies. Informed consent, peer review, morbidity and mortality conferences, licensing boards, liability: these systems exist because we know that ego distorts judgment and we have decided, as a field, to build external checks. They are imperfect. They fail. But they exist. They are the institutional acknowledgment that ego left unchecked causes harm, and that the person doing the harm is often the last one to see it.

The psychedelic space has almost none of this. The practitioner meets you in your most open and impressionable state, often in legal gray zones that make formal oversight impossible, with no standardized credentialing and no external accountability. What you have is the guide's own character and the culture of the communities they come from.

The credentialing and accountability structures are coming. They are being built right now, imperfectly and with great difficulty, by people who understand what is at stake. But they are not here yet, and the people entering this work today are navigating a space that medicine would consider dangerously underregulated.

That gap matters because of what lives in it.

The most dangerous animal in the jungle does not announce itself. It does not look dangerous. It looks, in fact, like exactly what you were hoping to find: warm, wise, experienced, and devoted to your healing. We will meet it properly later in this book. For now, understand that it thrives precisely in the absence of the structures that medicine spent a century building to contain the ego's known appetites.

The question of who is sitting across from you in that ceremony is not a secondary concern. Given everything we have just established about how this medicine works, about why the container is load-bearing and the relationship is mechanism, it is the primary one.
