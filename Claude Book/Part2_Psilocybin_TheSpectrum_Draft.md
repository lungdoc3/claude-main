# Safe Enough to Be Dangerous

*Part 2: The Substances — Psilocybin chapter, aside*
*Status: Draft, merged from "Safe Enough to Be Dangerous" + "In No Way Inferior", Critic-reviewed (Excellent after revision)*

---

Psilocybin is the only medicine in this book that genuinely supports the full range of how a person can approach it. On one end, a backyard, friends, no stated intention, nothing prepared beforehand and nothing processed afterward. On the other, a fully held ceremonial structure, screening and prep on one side, integration on the other, a guide who actually knows what they're doing in the room. Both ends are real. Both are legitimate uses of the same medicine. That range is not the problem. It might be the best argument for why this medicine belongs in as many hands as it's in.

Ayahuasca doesn't give you that range. The supply chain itself won't let you skip the container. There's no buying spores online and growing your own batch of grandmother. A maestro has to cook the brew. A lineage has to vouch for the maestro. Years of apprenticeship stand between a person and the medicine before they're ever allowed to serve someone else with it. I mean that as a real compliment to psilocybin, not a knock against it, but it does mean that with psilocybin, the question of who's actually qualified to be in the room with you depends entirely on the person, not the plant. Anyone can buy spores, grow a batch in a closet, and call themselves a guide by the time the next flush comes in. A friend of a friend can do it. Plenty of people just find it growing in a field.

So the range exists, and almost nobody is required to choose their spot on it deliberately. People drift to whichever end is easiest to reach, and the easiest end to reach is also the one with the least structure built into it.

Here's where the danger actually lives, and it isn't at either end of that range. Low-dose psilocybin with no container at all is genuinely fine. That's the fun-and-giggles use case, light visual shimmer, a loosened sense of humor, music that suddenly sounds better than it did an hour ago. Low stakes, low structure, no mismatch between the two. The trouble starts when the dose climbs and the container doesn't climb with it. Push the dose up and the same medicine turns into something else entirely. It becomes reality-shattering and ego-dissolving, in no way inferior to the most intense night of ayahuasca ceremony. Same mushroom, same active compound, a completely different category of experience depending entirely on how much of it you take. Someone can end up taking an aya-equivalent dose with none of the structure aya forces by default, because the culture they learned this medicine from never told them the structure was optional only up to a point.

Calling mushrooms a trickster, or niños santos, the holy children, was always more accurate than the label gets credit for. Tricksters in every mythology earn that name by being underestimated right up until the moment it costs someone something. The same playful reputation that makes psilocybin approachable at a low dose is exactly what makes people walk into a high dose without the respect it actually requires.

From where I stand as a physician, the part of this that's genuinely safe is the part most people worry about. Compared to ayahuasca, psilocybin is far more forgiving of a person's existing medication list. Someone on an SSRI can usually take psilocybin without the dangerous interaction risk that comes with the MAOIs in ayahuasca. The risk on that side is different. The SSRI can blunt psilocybin's effect so thoroughly that the person feels almost nothing at all. That pharmacological safety reputation is mostly earned, and it stays roughly constant whether someone is at the no-container end of the range or the fully-held end.

That's exactly what makes it dangerous. Pharmacological safety holding steady across the whole range is what convinces people the entire range is equally safe. It isn't. The axis that actually predicts what happens to someone is relational, set, setting, the container a person builds or fails to build around the experience, the preparation beforehand and the integration after. That axis swings as wide as anything in this book, from nothing at all to fully held, and it swings independently of whether the dose climbing right alongside it gets noticed.

The push toward medicalization and legalization makes this more pressing, not less. A licensed therapy setting might turn out to be the best possible container for this medicine, structured, supervised, integrated into actual healthcare. But that kind of access costs money and requires living somewhere it's offered, and most people don't have both of those things at once. The gap between how many people want this experience and how many properly trained centers exist to provide it keeps widening, and it has to go somewhere. It goes underground, into exactly the same low-barrier landscape where a friend of a friend with a grow tent is already calling himself a guide, handing out the kind of dose that deserves a maloca and a maestro to someone who showed up expecting a carnival ride.

Here's a piece of chemistry most people never learn, and I remember being genuinely startled by it myself the first time I did. Psilocin, the compound your body actually converts psilocybin into, has a real name, 4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine. Strip away the prefix and what's left is DMT, the same molecule doing the visionary work in ayahuasca. Psilocin is DMT with one hydroxyl group attached, not a distant pharmacological cousin. DMT, psilocin, and serotonin all belong to the same tryptamine family. What gets handed to your nervous system at a high dose is a close relative of a molecule it already produces every day, not a stranger arriving from outside the system.

That's the whole argument in one fact. The dose can take you exactly as far as ayahuasca takes people, by way of a molecule one hydroxyl group removed from the one she uses. Culture tells you the container is optional because psilocybin is the easy one, the fun one, the one you don't need a maestro for. Your nervous system never got that memo.

The range itself isn't the problem. It's actually part of what makes psilocybin valuable. The problem is landing somewhere on it by accident instead of choosing your spot on purpose.
