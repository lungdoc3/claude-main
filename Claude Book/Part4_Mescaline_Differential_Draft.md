**Before We Start: The Source Question**

Mescaline is not a single experience. It arrives in three primary forms — peyote cactus, San Pedro (Huachuma), and synthetic mescaline — and that distinction matters before any pharmacological discussion can begin.

Peyote and San Pedro are not pure mescaline. They contain dozens of additional alkaloids whose pharmacological effects are incompletely understood and whose interactions with western medications have not been studied at all. The safety data that exists for mescaline is largely data on the isolated compound. When someone is drinking San Pedro tea or chewing peyote buttons, they are working with a pharmacologically complex plant preparation, and the honest clinical answer to many interaction questions is: we know what mescaline does, and we do not fully know what the rest of it does.

This does not make these traditions less valuable. It means the uncertainty band around interaction risk is wider than it is for an isolated compound, and should be acknowledged as such. What follows applies most precisely to mescaline specifically, and applies to peyote and San Pedro with the added caveat that the full alkaloid picture adds variables that the current evidence cannot fully account for.

---

**The Pharmacological Foundation**

Mescaline is a phenethylamine — a structural class that includes dopamine, norepinephrine, and, at greater pharmacological distance, the amphetamines. This structural ancestry does not make mescaline an amphetamine. It does not cause monoamine release the way amphetamines do. It does not drive the dopaminergic stimulation that characterizes stimulant drugs. The structural kinship is real; the clinical equivalence is not.

What mescaline does: it acts as a partial agonist at 5-HT2A receptors — the same primary mechanism as psilocybin. This is the pharmacological core of its psychedelic effects, and it is why the serotonin-system interactions described in the general pharmaceutical discussion apply here. It also has documented activity at 5-HT1A receptors and adrenergic α2A receptors — a receptor profile psilocybin does not share.

What mescaline does not do: it does not inhibit the serotonin transporter (SERT). Psilocybin's active metabolite, psilocin, has weak SERT inhibition — a mild reuptake-inhibiting effect on top of the receptor agonism. Mescaline lacks this component. This is a pharmacological distinction with a potential clinical implication: the cumulative serotonergic load from mescaline, while still meaningful, may be marginally lower than from psilocybin in the specific context of serotonin syndrome risk from reuptake inhibition.

---

**What Stays the Same**

The foundational interaction categories from the general discussion apply to mescaline without modification.

Serotonergic antidepressants will attenuate the experience through 5-HT2A downregulation, and carry serotonin syndrome risk when stacked with an active 5-HT2A agonist. MAO inhibitors remain contraindicated, though mescaline's relationship to MAO metabolism is less clear than psilocybin's — more on that below. Antipsychotics block the experience through 5-HT2A antagonism. Lithium carries seizure risk. Benzodiazepines blunt the experience and serve as a potential emergency tool. The natural MAOI supplements — passionflower, rhodiola, methylene blue — carry the same categories of concern.

All of that holds. What follows is what changes.

---

**What Changes: Duration**

This is the most important clinical differentiator, and it changes the entire risk calculation.

In a randomized, placebo-controlled comparative trial — the only head-to-head study of mescaline, LSD, and psilocybin in humans — the effect duration of a psychoactive-equivalent dose of mescaline averaged 11 hours, with a range extending to 22 hours. Psilocybin in the same study averaged 5 hours. That is roughly double the duration, and the difference was statistically significant.

What that means clinically: every interaction concern in the general discussion now applies across a window that is twice as long. A cardiovascular effect that lasts five hours with psilocybin lasts ten or eleven with mescaline. An elevated heart rate, a raised blood pressure, an increased demand on cardiac workload — these are transient with psilocybin; with mescaline they are sustained. Not more severe at peak, based on current data. Just longer.

For a healthy person with no cardiovascular risk factors, this distinction is probably not clinically significant. For someone with hypertension, coronary disease, arrhythmia, or heart failure — someone on cardiac medications whose interaction profile is uncertain to begin with — duration compounds whatever concern already existed. Twice the exposure window is not a minor footnote. It is a fundamental feature of the risk calculation.

The same logic applies to psychological risk. A difficult experience that resolves in five hours is a different clinical situation than one that persists for eleven. If someone needs support, if the medicine needs to be countered, if an emergency intervention becomes necessary — the window in which that is relevant is roughly double. This does not make mescaline more dangerous. It makes the stakes of good preparation higher and the value of medical oversight greater.

The subacute period matters too. The comparative trial found that only mescaline showed significant adverse effects in the 12–24 hour window after dosing — fatigue, headache, and residual effects that the other substances did not produce. This is almost certainly a function of duration rather than a distinct toxicity. But it means the day after a mescaline ceremony is not a clean slate. Plan accordingly. This is not a day to drive, to make high-stakes decisions, or to find out what happens when your beta-blocker meets a nervous system that is still settling.

---

**What Changes: The Cardiovascular Picture**

The intuitive assumption — that mescaline's adrenergic receptor activity would produce more cardiovascular stimulation than psilocybin — was tested directly in the comparative trial and was not confirmed. Heart rate, blood pressure, and the combined cardiovascular stimulation index were comparable across mescaline, LSD, and psilocybin at psychoactive-equivalent doses. The adrenergic α2A binding that distinguishes mescaline's receptor profile did not translate into measurably greater cardiovascular stress in healthy volunteers.

This is clinically reassuring, but it comes with a caveat: these were healthy volunteers in a controlled research setting. The population most likely to have cardiovascular interactions with any psychedelic is precisely the population excluded from these trials. What we can say is that at equivalent psychoactive doses, mescaline does not appear to be uniquely cardiotoxic compared to its chemical relatives.

What we cannot say is that the sustained duration does not matter for people with existing cardiovascular vulnerability. It does. The interaction with cardiac medications — antihypertensives, antiarrhythmics, rate-controlling drugs — deserves real clinical attention in any mescaline context, not because the peak effect is higher, but because the sustained demand is longer.

Yohimbine and stimulant-class supplements, flagged in the general supplement discussion as a concern across all classical psychedelics, deserve particular emphasis here for the same reason. Adding sympathetic nervous system activation on top of an 11-hour psychedelic window is a longer-duration risk, not just a peak-intensity one.

---

**What Changes: MAO Metabolism — An Honest Uncertainty**

Psilocybin's relationship to monoamine oxidase is reasonably well-characterized: MAO-A participates in psilocin's metabolic clearance, which is why MAO inhibitors warrant caution.

Mescaline's relationship to MAO is genuinely uncertain. In vitro studies suggest mescaline may be a poor substrate of purified human MAO — meaning MAO may not play a significant role in its breakdown. The enzymes primarily responsible for mescaline metabolism appear to include MAO, diamine oxidase, and possibly others, but the relative contribution of each has not been definitively established. No clinical studies of mescaline combined with pharmaceutical MAO inhibitors have been published.

What this means in practice: the contraindication against MAO inhibitors cannot be stated with the same mechanistic certainty for mescaline as it can for psilocybin or for ayahuasca. The concern is real — any combination of a serotonergic psychedelic with a drug that broadly elevates serotonin warrants caution — but the specific mechanism by which MAO inhibitors might potentiate or interact with mescaline is not established. The precautionary principle applies. The honest framing is caution based on pharmacological class and incomplete evidence, not a well-characterized mechanism.

**What Changes: Nausea and Antiemetic Considerations**

Nausea is more prominent with mescaline — and considerably more prominent with peyote — than with psilocybin. With peyote in particular, the purging often experienced in ceremony appears to involve additional plant alkaloids beyond mescaline itself, though this has not been rigorously studied. San Pedro tends to produce less nausea than peyote in community experience, likely reflecting its different alkaloid profile.

The antiemetic choice rules from the general discussion apply here with slightly higher clinical relevance given the greater likelihood of nausea: ondansetron remains the acceptable option, acting through 5-HT3 receptor antagonism without significant interaction with the psychedelic mechanism. Metoclopramide, with its antipsychotic-class dopamine antagonism, remains the one to avoid.

---

**The Phenethylamine Question**

Mescaline belongs to the same structural class as dopamine, norepinephrine, and the amphetamines. In clinical practice, this generates two questions worth addressing.

The first is whether mescaline should be thought of as stimulant-like from an interaction standpoint. The answer is: not primarily. Mescaline does not cause the monoamine release that drives amphetamine pharmacology. It does not substantially inhibit dopamine or norepinephrine reuptake. Its psychedelic effects are 5-HT2A mediated. The phenethylamine backbone is structural, not a reliable predictor of amphetamine-class interactions.

The second is whether medications that interact with phenethylamine-class drugs in general — certain MAO inhibitors, for instance, which are specifically contraindicated with amphetamines due to hypertensive crisis risk — carry the same concern with mescaline. This is genuinely unclear. The mechanism by which MAOIs create hypertensive crisis with amphetamines involves massive norepinephrine and dopamine release. Mescaline does not trigger that release mechanism. The interaction, if it exists, would operate through different pathways. The precautionary principle again suggests caution, while honesty requires noting that the specific risk is not well-characterized.

**Summary of Differential Recommendations**

| Element | Psilocybin (Reference) | Mescaline (What Changes) |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | ~5 hours | ~11 hours — interaction window doubles |
| Cardiovascular peak | Moderate | Comparable; concern shifts to sustained duration |
| SERT inhibition | Present (weak) | Absent — marginally lower SS risk from this pathway |
| MAO metabolism | Established | Uncertain — precaution warranted, mechanism less clear |
| Adrenergic activity | None | α2A binding — in vivo effect comparable at clinical doses |
| Nausea | Mild–moderate | More prominent; significant with peyote |
| Subacute (12–24h) effects | Minimal | Documented — plan for full next-day recovery |
| Plant source variability | Less relevant (isolated compound typical) | Significant — peyote and San Pedro contain additional alkaloids |
| Antiemetic choice | Ondansetron yes; metoclopramide no | Same; higher clinical relevance given nausea profile |
