# Whose Life Is This?

Watch how kittens play. They stalk, they pounce, they practice hunting. Grown cats supervise. Puppies play dog — chase, wrestle, mouth. Human kids play make believe.

Every costume worn nonstop for a week, every game of pretend, every cardboard box turned into a spaceship is practice. And our job as parents is to support that play while loading in everything we believe they'll need: the values, the concepts, the beliefs, the direction. We do it because we love them. We do it because it's what was done to us. We do it because we genuinely think we know what a good life looks like.

At some point, we send them out dressed in all of it.

For some of those up-and-coming adult versions, something eventually cracks. A question arrives that doesn't have a good answer: why am I doing what I'm doing? How did I get here? Whose idea was this?

As my Q'ero brothers say: we are all trying to find the answers to the questions our ancestors posed.

The uncomfortable truth that follows is this: the life you've been living may have been assembled from other people's blueprints. Your family's idea of success. Your culture's definition of a good person. The expectations of people who loved you and had no idea what your actual life was supposed to look like. You've been playing make believe. You just stopped noticing the costume.

Most people bury this moment quickly. A new purchase, a new relationship, more of whatever recreational substance does the job. The question goes back underground. Life continues.

Psychedelic experiences have a way of making that burial harder. The experience itself can surface the realization that the choices you made weren't entirely yours. And then the experience creates the conditions to actually do something about it.

Which is where it gets complicated. Because people reach for the word "crisis." Mid-life crisis. Existential crisis. The language minimizes what's actually happening and points toward a fix: ride it out, make some changes, get back to normal. As though normal were the goal.

I became a doctor because of everything my family, my culture, and my society taught me about what being a doctor meant. Respect. Security. Autonomy. Good income. I added my own embellishments without any firsthand knowledge of what the job actually involved. For a while, it tracked perfectly against the script. Then it didn't. And I had to reckon with the fact that the gap between the brochure and the reality wasn't a bug. It was the information.

I was a single father at the time. Quitting was not on the table. What was on the table was changing my relationship to the job, which turned out to be a more interesting project than leaving would have been. I found that I genuinely enjoyed the work itself, separate from the identity I'd built around it. Letting go of the belief that I was responsible for my patients' healing, rather than responsible for the quality of my presence with them, released a performance anxiety I hadn't even known I was carrying. Same job. Different terms. Chosen, this time, by me.

That's the work that becomes possible after the realization lands. The question isn't what do I blow up. The question is: having seen the programming, what do I actually want to keep?

The psychedelic experience opens that door. Integration is how you walk through it.
