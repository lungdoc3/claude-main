# The Mystical Experience: What People Report, and Why It Matters

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

---

## Three Things Worth Knowing First

Mystical experiences that occur during psychedelic sessions have, across a growing body of clinical research, been measured, analyzed, and found to carry significant therapeutic weight. Before exploring what these experiences actually look like from the inside, three orienting principles deserve clear statement.

Mystical experiences can be peak human experiences.

Research from Johns Hopkins, where Roland Griffiths and colleagues followed participants for 14 months after a psilocybin session, found that 58% rated the experience among the five most personally meaningful of their lives, and 67% among the five most spiritually significant. No volunteer rated the session as having decreased their well-being. These aren't post-hoc rationalizations; the ratings held steady from 2 months to 14 months with no significant erosion. An 8-hour session in a research unit produced something that participants placed alongside the birth of a child or the death of a parent. William James would not have been surprised. Abraham Maslow would have recognized it immediately: a peak experience, a moment of self-transcendence that reorganizes what a person cares about and why.

Mystical experiences can contribute to healing.

A 2022 systematic review of 12 clinical studies across psilocybin, ayahuasca, and ketamine found that 10 of 12 established a significant association between the intensity of mystical experience and symptom reduction, across conditions including treatment-resistant depression, alcohol use disorder, tobacco dependence, and cancer-related existential distress. In several studies, the mystical quality of the experience was found to mediate the therapeutic effect: not merely correlated with it, but operating as the mechanism through which the medicine worked.

Mystical experiences are not a necessary condition for healing. 

This is as important as the second point. Some individuals who showed meaningful clinical improvement reported no mystical experience by any psychometric measure. Other mechanisms operate in parallel: neuroplasticity, emotional processing, increased psychological flexibility, a loosening of rigid cognitive patterns. Healing occurs across a spectrum of experience. No patient should be made to feel that a more ordinary session was a failure. The mystical experience, when it occurs, may be the most potent version of what the medicine makes possible. It is not the only version.

With those principles established, we can turn to what people actually experience. Across thousands of session reports, clinical interviews, and qualitative studies, a set of recurring themes emerges. They are not universal, and not every person encounters all of them. But they appear with enough consistency, and carry enough clinical and human significance, to be worth understanding.



Theme One: Cosmic Womb, Death, and Rebirth

Something dies in many psychedelic sessions. Not the person. But the self as the person knew it.

The sequence tends to follow a recognizable arc. First, the identity begins to dissolve. The ordinary sense of being a bounded, named person with a history and a future starts to come apart. "I'm not a person anymore." "I'm pure awareness." "I'm dissolving into nothing." The internal narrator, the one who maintains the ongoing story of who you are, goes quiet or disappears entirely. For people whose sense of self has been a source of relentless suffering, built around trauma, shame, or years of self-criticism, this dissolution can arrive as an unexpected relief. For others, the initial moments are terrifying. Because from inside the experience, it can feel exactly like dying.

This is where the experience earns its name: ego death. From a neurological perspective, what is happening maps closely onto what we described in the DMN chapter. The self-referential network that constructs and maintains the narrative self is temporarily suppressed. The story stops. What remains, for many people, is not nothing. It is something closer to pure awareness: warmth, presence, an experience of being held rather than lost.

Many people describe what follows as a womb-space. Darkness, warmth, a quality of suspension or fluidity. Sometimes a rhythmic sound, music or a perceived heartbeat. A felt sense of being held by something that has no personal form but feels unambiguously benevolent. Time disappears. The urgency of ordinary life disappears. What remains is a quality of deep, unearned, fundamental okayness.

And then there is a return. A breathing back into form. Light returning. People describe it as being born. Sometimes it carries a felt shift in identity: not a dramatic transformation of facts, but a reorientation of role. "I'm a protector." "I'm here to create." "I'm a father now, not just technically." "I'm a student again." The person who walks out of the session is, in some meaningful sense, not the same person who walked in. Not because they have acquired new beliefs, but because something reorganized beneath the beliefs.

Anthropologists and historians of religion will recognize this structure immediately. It is the initiatory sequence that appears across human cultures and across millennia: the descent, the ordeal, the transformation, the return. The hero's journey. The shamanic dismemberment. The dark night of the soul. What psychedelics appear to offer is a controlled encounter with this ancient arc, without requiring a tribe, a ceremony, or a theology.

The clinical relevance is concrete. People who move through an ego death experience, particularly the full arc from dissolution through return, consistently report reduced death anxiety at follow-up. What had frightened them was losing the self. Having lost it, and returned, the fear changes in quality. They know something they did not know before, something that arrived not as argument but as experience. Less fear of dying. More gratitude for being alive. A clearer, quieter sense of what actually matters.


Theme Two: Union with the Living Earth

Something else happens with striking regularity in psychedelic sessions: the boundary between self and nature dissolves, and the planet becomes personal.

This does not arrive as a metaphor. It arrives sensorially, with the force of direct perception. The earth is experienced as alive. Genuinely, indisputably alive. Plants feel intelligent. Animals feel like family. The air feels like a shared substance, not a resource but a relationship. People describe looking at a tree and recognizing it, in some felt sense, as a relative. They describe the soil as a living organism, breathing, responsive, present.

When self-boundaries soften, the category of "self" expands. If the ordinary sense of "me" encompasses my body, and my body is made of the same elements as everything around me, the dissolution of the self-boundary does not leave nothing. It leaves everything. The self becomes the world, at least temporarily. And what follows from that is not an idea about environmentalism. It is grief. Because when you love something and you see it suffering, you grieve it. The ecological crisis does not look like a policy problem from inside this experience. It looks like a family member being harmed.

This is why psychedelic experiences consistently appear in research as significant predictors of what investigators call nature relatedness, a measured construct assessing felt connection to the natural world. Multiple studies have found that a single psychedelic session produces lasting increases in nature relatedness, and that those increases predict more pro-environmental attitudes and behavior at follow-up. Not because someone was persuaded by an argument. Because they felt something they had not felt before.

The phrase that seems to capture it best: heart-based earth care. Not moral superiority. Not performative environmentalism or environmental guilt. Something closer to expanded belonging: a recognition that caring for the planet is not a sacrifice but a natural extension of caring for oneself, because the boundary between the two has become permeable.

This may be one of the least discussed but most consequential dimensions of the psychedelic experience. The individual healing and the collective healing are not as separate as we typically assume. What reorganizes a person's relationship to their own suffering can, in the same session, reorganize their relationship to the living systems they depend on and belong to.


Theme Three: The Underworld, the Honest Mirror, and What Gets Released

Not all psychedelic experiences are luminous. Some are not. And this is not a failure of the medicine.

The psychedelic state can open doors that have been locked for decades, sometimes deliberately, sometimes not even consciously. Material that was too threatening, too painful, or too shame-soaked to be integrated through ordinary means becomes accessible. The clinical literature calls this shadow work. People in the middle of it use different language: the dark room, the hard part, the terrifying hours. Call it what it is.

What the medicine does, under the right conditions, is not force this material into consciousness to overwhelm or re-traumatize the person. It is something more precise than that. It shows you the room. It lets you see what has been stored there, sometimes for a lifetime, without the defenses that have been keeping you from looking. Not to shame you with it. Not to punish you. To help you recognize it, acknowledge it, and begin to release it.

The distinction between reliving and releasing is everything. People who have accessed traumatic material in the psychedelic state frequently describe something that clinical practitioners will recognize as profoundly different from intrusive re-experiencing. They saw it. All of it. They did not fragment. "It's not a reliving. It's a release." Material that has been generating symptoms from behind a locked door loses some of its grip when it finally gets seen. The body completes something that was interrupted.

People report retrieving memories of abuse they had kept completely sealed. Grief that never found expression. Shame that had calcified into identity. Patterns of behavior whose origin they had genuinely never examined. The experience does not demand that they fix it in the session. It asks only that they look at it. And looking, it turns out, is often most of what was needed.

Woven through this underworld material, two other experiences frequently appear.

The first is what might be called a life review with moral clarity. People report seeing their own actions, including the harmful ones, with an unusual combination of honesty and compassion. They feel the pain they have caused others. They understand, sometimes for the first time, the pattern beneath the behavior. And they do this without being destroyed by the seeing. "I finally understood why I do it." "I felt what I had done to the people I love, and I didn't collapse." This is the mechanism behind some of the most striking addiction treatment outcomes in the clinical literature. It is not the acquisition of new information. It is felt truth. The denial that has been maintaining the pattern cannot survive the experience of feeling its actual consequences in the body. Something updates at a deeper level than insight can reach.

The second experience is ancestral contact: visions or felt presences of relatives, sometimes deceased, sometimes representing patterns carried across generations. The specific content varies enormously. The common clinical outcome is more consistent: grief release, forgiveness, a reframing of family identity. The shift from "I am cursed" to "I am carrying pain that can end with me" is not a small one. It reorganizes a person's relationship to their own history, and with it, their sense of what is possible going forward.

The underworld is real. It is often the most important terrain a person moves through. And the return from it, when the conditions are right, carries medicine.



Theme Four: Love Is the Source of Everything

Here is where we have to address the Hallmark slogan problem.

People who undergo profound mystical experiences, and try to describe what they found at the bottom of everything, reach for almost universally the same language: love. God is love. The universe is love. Everything, at its foundation, is love. And the moment those words appear in print, every reasonable, evidence-respecting reader feels the familiar internal cringe. We know. We have heard it before. It is printed on greeting cards and embroidered on pillows.

Does the familiarity of the language invalidate the experience?

The evidence suggests it does not. What makes this report compelling is not the content of the statement but the epistemological quality with which it arrives. People do not come back from these experiences believing that the universe is love in the way they believe a comforting idea. They come back knowing it in the same register that they know that fire is hot. It does not feel like an interpretation. It feels like a discovery. Philosophers of religion, following William James, call this the noetic quality of mystical experience: direct apprehension, felt as certainty, not reasoned toward. The person is not persuaded. They encounter.

What that produces, practically, is often indistinguishable from the outcomes a clinician would spend months working toward in therapy. The self-hatred softens. Shame loses its grip. Self-forgiveness, which can be among the most therapeutically stubborn challenges, becomes suddenly available. Because if love is the fundamental condition of existence, then the suffering person is not excluded from it. They have always been included in it. They simply did not have access to that knowledge until now.

Love experienced as substrate rather than sentiment also functions as emotional glue. The changes people make after a profound psychedelic experience tend to hold at higher rates than changes made through other means. Part of the explanation lies in the neuroplasticity window and the integration work that follows. But part of it may be fundamentally motivational. The person is not sustaining change through discipline, willpower, or fear. They are sustaining it because something became clear to them that was simply, profoundly, self-evidently true. When what you have experienced feels more real than the habits you are trying to break, the habits lose their authority.

It is worth acknowledging that this experience is not only beautiful. People who access unconditional love, especially for the first time, sometimes also encounter profound grief: for the years they spent without it, for others they love who remain cut off from it, for the distance between how the world actually is and how it feels when the filter comes down. The opening is real. So is what the opening reveals about how far we have drifted from what we need.

But as a clinical phenomenon, it may be the most fundamentally transformative thing the medicine can offer. Not because love is a nice thought. Because, for some people, experiencing it as an atomic fact rather than a hope rearranges everything that follows.
