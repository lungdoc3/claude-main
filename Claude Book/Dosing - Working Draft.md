# Dosing: The Wrong Question and the Right One

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

---

## The Question Everyone Asks

What is the right dose?

It is almost always the first thing people want to know. Which makes sense. We live in a medical culture built on the assumption that dosing is the central variable, the one that separates therapeutic from toxic, effective from inert. Get the dose right and the medicine works. Get it wrong and things go badly. Every drug a physician prescribes comes with a number attached. Milligrams per kilogram. Therapeutic window. Dose-response curve. The assumption underneath all of it is that the compound's effect is primarily a function of how much of it you take.

That framework works reasonably well for antibiotics. It does not work for psychedelics.

Not because dosing is irrelevant. It matters. But the question "what is the right dose?" assumes there is a fixed answer. With psychedelics, there isn't. And understanding why is one of the most important things you can know before entering this work.

---

## Why the Conventional Framework Breaks Down

Start with the compound itself.

When a pharmaceutical company produces a drug, every batch is required to be chemically identical to every other batch. Standardization is not optional. It is the foundation of how modern medicine controls for dosing variables. If two people take 50mg of the same medication, they are taking 50mg of the same molecule, produced to the same specification, tested to the same standard.

Plant medicines do not work this way. Plants are individuals. Two psilocybin mushrooms from the same species, the same patch of soil, the same harvest can carry meaningfully different concentrations of active compound. There is no manufacturing specification. There is no batch certificate. What looks like the same dose is, in a chemically real sense, not the same dose.

That is problem one. Problem two is larger.

Even if you could standardize the compound perfectly, the relationship between dose and effect is not primarily driven by the compound. Set and setting, the psychological state and the physical environment a person brings into the experience, have been shown to be as determinative of outcome as the pharmacology itself. Two people taking identical doses in identical physiological states can have entirely different experiences based on their intention, their history, their level of preparation, and the quality of support around them. The dose is one variable in a system of variables. It is not the master variable.

And then there is individual response. Some people are exquisitely sensitive. A dose that barely registers for one person will produce a full, floor-shifting experience for another. Age, body composition, prior experience, neurological architecture, liver enzymes, medication interactions, current psychological state: all of it modulates the experience. There is no universal therapeutic window because there is no universal user.

The conventional dosing framework assumes standardized compound, predictable response, and a fixed therapeutic target. Psychedelics offer none of those. The framework does not fit.

## Every Dose Is the Right Dose

One of my teachers framed it this way, and I have never found a better way to say it.

Think of a department store. Multiple floors, each one its own world. Men's wear up here. Outdoor and garden down there. Housewares somewhere in between. Each floor has its own inventory, its own atmosphere, its own particular rewards. The person shopping for work clothes and the person looking for a sleeping bag are not going to the same floor. Neither of them is wrong. They are simply visiting different floors for different reasons.

Each dose of a psychedelic is a different floor.

A low, sub-threshold dose is the ground floor. Sensory sharpening. Colors a bit more vivid. A mild shift in mood. The architecture is the same building, but you are barely inside. There is value here, particularly for someone new to the work, someone who needs to feel safe, someone whose nervous system needs to be introduced slowly to the experience of things being slightly different.

A moderate dose takes you up a few floors. Deeper perceptual shifts. Emotional material becomes more available. The filter loosens. What you might normally push aside or defend against begins to surface. This is where a lot of productive therapeutic work happens, close enough to ordinary consciousness that the person can engage and reflect, distant enough that the usual defenses have softened.

A high dose is a different floor entirely. The narrative self quiets or goes completely offline. The boundary between self and experience becomes permeable. What is available at that depth, both in terms of insight and in terms of difficulty, bears little resemblance to what the lower floors offer. This is where mystical experiences live. It is also where the work gets hardest and where the stakes for support are highest.

None of these floors is more correct than another. They each contain things worth knowing. The question is never "what is the right dose?" The question is "what floor do I want to visit, and am I prepared for what I will find there?"

## The River

My first teacher gave me an image I still use.

Accessing the knowledge available in psychedelic work is like approaching a wide, fast, deep river. The river is not hostile. It does not want to hurt anyone. But it is powerful, and it does not yield to people who underestimate it. People have gotten lost in that river. Some have not come back.

The teacher's job is to teach you how to navigate the water safely.

You start by dipping your toes in. You feel the current. You notice the temperature. You get a sense of what you are dealing with before committing any weight to it. Then you wade in to your ankles. Then your knees. You learn the feel of the riverbed. You learn how the current moves. You return to shore. You go back. You go a little deeper. You learn to tread water. Then to swim. Then to navigate. Then, eventually, to find what you came for and find your way back.

Every one of those steps is a lesson. Every level of depth teaches something that the previous level could not. And every step is taken while the teacher is present, watching, able to intervene, able to call you back if the current shifts or you get turned around.

That progression is what responsible introduction to this work looks like.

The same teacher who taught me this was insistent about something else: you do not skip floors. You do not start with the penthouse because someone told you that is where the real view is. You learn the building from the bottom up. Low doses first. Stay at that level until you are comfortable navigating those waters. Until the terrain is familiar. Until you can find your footing without effort. Then, and only then, do you move up. Not by much. And you repeat. And repeat. Until you can walk into any floor in the building and know where you are.

This is not timidity. It is exactly the opposite. It is what allows you to eventually go anywhere.

## The Rule That Does Not Bend

Here is the principle that everything else in this section is pointing toward.

The further you push your comfort level, the stronger the support needs to be.

Dipping your toes in at a low dose in a safe environment with a trusted person nearby? The support requirements are relatively modest. Exploring high-dose territory, approaching the floor where the self goes quiet and ordinary landmarks disappear? That requires serious support. An experienced guide. A well-prepared container. A person who has themselves been to that floor, more than once, under skilled supervision, and who knows what they are looking at when they get there.

Because here is the part people do not always want to hear.

You cannot hold space for an experience you have never had. A guide who has not been in deep water cannot teach you to swim in it. The lifeguard who has never entered the river cannot pull you back when the current takes you. Sitting with someone at a depth you have not personally navigated is not holding space. It is abandoning them to figure it out alone while someone watches from the bank.

This has implications for anyone considering this work without support. Arriving at a high-dose experience without preparation, without a skilled guide, without a container that was built for what is about to happen, is not brave or independent. It is something closer to jumping into that deep, fast river having never seen water before, with no idea what the current does, and deciding you will figure it out on the way down.

That is a special kind of uncomfortable. And sometimes it is a special kind of dangerous.

The medicine is not the risk. Unpreparedness is the risk. Unsupported depth is the risk.

Every floor of that department store has something worth finding. The question is whether you know how to navigate the one you are on, and whether you have someone with you who does too.
