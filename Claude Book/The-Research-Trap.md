# The Research Trap

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

The clinical trials are real. The results are remarkable. Treatment-resistant depression. PTSD. Addiction. OCD. Anxiety in the face of terminal illness. Study after study showing effect sizes that make the pharmaceutical industry's standard offerings look like rounding errors. Johns Hopkins. NYU. Imperial College London. The data is not in question.

Neither is the framing. And the framing is a problem.

Every one of those trials was designed around a diagnostic category. A DSM code. A billable condition with a measurable symptom cluster and a corresponding treatment target. This is how clinical research works. You identify the condition, define the endpoints, randomize the subjects, measure the delta. The infrastructure of evidence-based medicine was built for this, and it is genuinely good at it.

It was also built for a different kind of medicine.

The diagnostic category was designed to match pharmaceutical interventions: you have this condition, we have this molecule, the molecule acts on this mechanism, the condition improves. Cause, intervention, outcome. The Newtonian logic we have already discussed, now encoded into research methodology. When the question is whether a drug reduces a symptom, this framework is appropriate and useful.

When the question is whether a person's fundamental relationship to their own experience has changed, the framework is measuring the wrong thing with a ruler that was built for something else.

Here is the sharper version of that point.

The research focus on specific diagnostic categories is not a neutral scientific choice. It is a funding choice, a regulatory choice, and an institutional survival choice dressed up as science. Research requires funding. Funding flows toward conditions that insurance pays for. Insurance pays for conditions defined by the DSM. The DSM was built around what pharmaceuticals can address. The whole system is a closed loop, and psychedelic research, in its eagerness for legitimacy, has agreed to spin inside it.

The cost of that agreement is significant.

The population that could benefit from this work is not the population that shows up in clinical trial inclusion criteria. The person who is functional but suffering, not diagnosable, not in crisis, not meeting threshold for any particular category, but genuinely not living the life they are capable of living: they do not exist in the research framework. They are not depressed enough. They do not qualify. But they are, in my clinical experience, exactly the population this medicine is most consistently and most profoundly useful for. We are not studying them. We will not have data on them. And the absence of data will be used, inevitably, to argue that there is no indication for treating them.

The deeper problem is what happens when FDA approval arrives.

It will arrive. The data will demand it, eventually. And when it does, it will arrive with a label, an indication, a standardized protocol, a billing code, and a clinical infrastructure designed to deliver it in a way that the approval process can certify as safe and consistent. What that infrastructure will not be able to certify, because it has no mechanism for doing so, is the relational container. The preparation. The integration. The quality of presence of the person in the room. The music. The darkness. The permission to go wherever the medicine wants to take you.

Those things are not variables in the trials. They are controlled for. Held constant. Standardized. Because the trial needs to know what the molecule does, not what the molecule plus everything else does.

But the molecule plus everything else is precisely what produces the outcomes. Strip out everything else and you have an expensive hallucinogen administered in a clinical setting by someone with a certificate. Some of those sessions will still be remarkable. The medicine is too powerful to be entirely neutralized by a bad container. But the effect size will be smaller, the integration will be thinner, and the people who needed something more than symptom reduction will not get it.

They will conclude the medicine failed.

The medicine will not have failed. The frame will have failed. But the headline will say psychedelics, and the next funding cycle will reflect it, and the window will be a little narrower than it was.

This is not a hypothetical concern. It is the predictable consequence of forcing a non-Newtonian intervention into a Newtonian approval pathway. The pathway was not designed to accommodate what makes this work work. It will not expand to accommodate it. The work will be expected to shrink to fit.

The researchers doing this work are not the problem. Many of them understand exactly what I am describing and are doing what they can within a system that gives them limited options. The problem is the system, and our willingness as a field to accept its terms as the only legitimate terms.

The data we are generating answers the question the system is willing to ask. It does not answer the question that matters: what does this work do for human beings when it is done well, in the right container, with the preparation and integration it requires, for the full range of people it could serve?

That study has never been funded. It would not survive peer review as currently designed. It would not produce an approvable label.

It would, however, tell the truth.
