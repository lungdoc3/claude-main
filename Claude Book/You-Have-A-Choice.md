# You Have a Choice

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

What you discover, if you stay awake long enough during the integration window, is that the choice was always there.

Not just in the large, dramatic moments. In all of it. The small irritations, the habitual reactions, the stories you have been telling yourself about the people in your life and the circumstances you find yourself in. Every single one of them contains a choice that most people have stopped noticing they are making.

Most people have forgotten they are in charge of their experience. This is not a character flaw. It is, as we have established, the predictable result of a brain that is extraordinarily good at automating decisions and presenting the results as reality. But the forgetting has consequences. It shows up as complaining without acting. As the persistent sense that things are happening to you rather than being shaped by you. As the victim posture that is always more comfortable than the alternative, right up until it isn't.

## The Smallest Version First

Take the most mundane possible example.

*I don't want to go to work.*

Almost everyone has said this. It feels like a true statement about a desire you are not in control of. But look at it more carefully. You are going to work. Which means, at some level, you have decided that going to work is preferable to whatever the consequences of not going to work would be. You are not a prisoner of your schedule. You are making a continuous cost-benefit calculation and choosing the option that makes the most sense to you, and then describing that choice as something being done to you.

This is not a small distinction. The person who says I don't want to go to work and goes anyway is telling themselves a story about their own powerlessness that is simply inaccurate. The person who says I am choosing to go to work because the alternative costs more than I want to pay is in a different relationship with their own life entirely. Same job. Same commute. Completely different experience of themselves as an agent in the world.

## The Medicine Axiom

There is an axiom in clinical medicine that most physicians know and many forget: do not practice medicine based on your last patient. The meaning is specific and practical. If a case went badly, do not let that outcome distort your approach to the next patient. The next patient is not the last patient. Carry the lesson forward, not the fear.

The same distortion happens in personal life, and it operates largely below the level of awareness.

Your experience of your spouse, to use the example I return to most often with clients, is not a neutral perception of a person. It is a lens you have assembled from the accumulated history of every interaction you have had with them, weighted heavily toward the most recent ones. If you went to bed angry, or woke up nursing an irritation from yesterday, or have been running a low-grade resentment for the past week: that is the version of your spouse who is going to walk through the door this evening. Not because that is who they are. Because that is the lens you are looking through.

And here is the part that matters: that lens is yours. You assembled it. You are maintaining it. You are choosing, in each moment, to look through it rather than setting it down.

Which means you can choose differently.

Not easily. Not without effort. But the option exists, and pretending it does not is a choice in itself.

## The Uncomfortable Part

This is where most people push back, and I understand why.

If you genuinely have a choice about how you greet your spouse at the end of the day, about how you respond to your child in the moment when they are testing every gram of your patience, about how you carry a conflict into the next morning or set it down before you sleep: then the story about the other person being responsible for your experience starts to fall apart.

And that story is comfortable. It is familiar. It has been doing useful work for a long time, distributing the weight of your experience onto other people and circumstances and getting you off the hook for how you feel and what you do in response.

Accepting that you have choices does not do that. It hands the weight back to you. Every bit of it. It requires that you take ownership not just of your actions but of the quality of attention you bring to the people in your life, the assumptions you carry into each interaction, the version of reality you are generating through the lens you have chosen to look through.

This is not always comfortable.

It is always accurate.

## What the Medicine Revealed

The ceremony did not give you choices you did not have before. You had them. What the medicine did, if the work was real and the integration is honest, was show you the room where they live. Made visible what had been running below the surface, below the reach of ordinary awareness. Interrupted the autopilot long enough for you to see, perhaps for the first time, that there was an autopilot.

The work of integration is not to maintain the altered state. It is to bring that visibility back into ordinary life and act on what you saw.

You are in charge of your experience. Not of everything that happens to you. But of how you meet it, what you make of it, and who you choose to be in response to it.

That is not a small thing. That is, in fact, everything.
