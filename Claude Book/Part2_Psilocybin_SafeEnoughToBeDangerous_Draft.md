# Safe Enough to Be Dangerous

*Part 2: The Substances — Psilocybin chapter, aside*
*Status: Draft, Critic-reviewed (Good → revised)*

---

Psilocybin is the substance most likely to get ripped out of its safety context entirely, and the reason has nothing to do with the mushroom itself. It has to do with how easy the mushroom is to get. Anyone can buy spores online, grow a batch in a closet, and call themselves a guide by the time the next flush comes in. A friend of a friend can do it. Plenty of people just find it growing in a field. There's no lineage standing between a person and the medicine the way there is with ayahuasca, no maestro's permission required, no years of apprenticeship before you're allowed to serve someone else. I mean that as a real compliment to psilocybin. But it also means the question of relational safety, who's actually qualified to be in the room with you, gets more urgent here than with any other medicine in this book, precisely because almost nobody is required to answer it.

Culturally, psilocybin gets treated like a carnival ride. People take it for fun, at festivals, with friends, the same way they'd take a tab of MDMA on a Saturday night. Nobody takes a shot of ayahuasca and then goes looking for a dance floor. I'm an exception to my own rule here, I dance hard during the second half of most of my own ceremonies, but that comes out of a specific, decades-long relationship with grandmother that took years to build. Most people's relationship with psilocybin starts on a much lighter footing than that, and the culture around it reflects it.

That lightness shows up in how people describe the medicine itself. Mushrooms get called childlike, playful, a trickster, which is fair as far as it goes, the niños santos framing from Mazatec tradition captures something real about its character. The trouble is what happens when people do decide to take it seriously. The conversation almost always narrows straight down to depression, as if relieving major depressive disorder were the only legitimate reason an adult could have for sitting with this medicine.

From where I stand as a physician, the pharmacological safety reputation is mostly earned. Compared to ayahuasca, psilocybin is far more forgiving of a person's existing medication list. Someone on an SSRI can usually take psilocybin without the dangerous interaction risk that comes with the MAOIs in ayahuasca. The risk on that side is different: the SSRI can blunt psilocybin's effect so thoroughly that the person feels almost nothing at all.

None of that pharmacological math is actually the safety question that matters most. Pharmacological safety is real, and it is also the wrong place to look if you want to know whether someone is going to be safe taking this medicine. The axis that actually predicts what happens is relational: set, setting, the container a person builds or fails to build around the experience, the preparation beforehand and the integration after. That's the spine of this entire book, and it's exactly the layer that gets skipped when psilocybin gets treated as the safe one.

The push toward medicalization and legalization makes this more pressing, not less. A licensed therapy setting might turn out to be the best possible container for this medicine, structured, supervised, integrated into actual healthcare. But that kind of access costs money and requires living somewhere it's offered, and most people don't have both of those things at once. The gap between how many people want this experience and how many properly trained centers exist to provide it keeps widening, and it has to go somewhere, and it goes underground, into exactly the same low-barrier landscape where a friend of a friend with a grow tent is already calling himself a guide.

High demand and the lowest barrier to entry of any medicine in this book is exactly the combination that produces unqualified guides. The barrier itself was always going to be the real danger.
