\# Foundation 2: Your Programming  
Firmware, Software, and the Code You Didn't Write

\*Working Draft | **Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective**\*

You didn't write your own code. You couldn't have. The most important programming you carry was written before you had the cognitive capacity to evaluate what was being programmed into you. Before you had language. Before you had memory in the way adults understand it. Before you had any mechanism for consent or refusal.

Your brain arrived in this world extraordinarily unfinished. That's not a design flaw. It's actually the whole point. The human brain's extended period of development is what allows it to be shaped by its specific environment, its specific caregivers, its specific culture. It's what makes us adaptable. It's also what makes early experience so consequential, and so hard to undo.

What got written in those early years is still running. Right now. In the background, below awareness, shaping everything.

Let's use a distinction that makes this concrete: firmware versus software.

Software is the kind of code you can update. Habits you've built as an adult. Beliefs you've acquired and can examine. Coping patterns you've developed and, with enough work, can change. Therapy tends to work here. Reflection works here. Education, coaching, conscious effort: these are all software-level interventions. This layer is real, it matters, and a lot of good work happens here.

Firmware is different. Firmware is the code written directly into the hardware during the windows of time when the brain was most plastic, most open, most capable of being shaped by experience. It is not conscious. It does not respond well to argument or insight. It runs below the reach of most conventional interventions, not because it's permanently fixed, but because the window that allowed it to be written in the first place has closed.

Those windows are called critical periods.

During certain developmental phases, the brain opens itself to specific types of learning in a way it never will again. The most well-documented examples are early: the windows for language acquisition, for visual processing, for attachment and social bonding, for the foundational architecture of emotional regulation. During these periods, experience doesn't just influence the brain. It literally shapes its structure. The connections that form, the patterns that get reinforced, the emotional rules that get encoded: all of it becomes part of the hardware.

And then the window closes.

This is the part that explains so much of human suffering. The child who grows up in an environment of chronic unpredictability doesn't just learn that the world is unsafe. That knowledge gets written into the nervous system during a critical period, at the level of firmware. The child who receives consistent, attuned care doesn't just feel loved. The neural architecture of secure attachment gets built in. For better or worse, what happens during these windows doesn't get stored the way later experiences do. It becomes the lens through which all later experience is processed.

Here is the second piece: programming doesn't only live in the mind. This is something conventional talk-based approaches consistently underestimate. The body is a storage medium. Fear encoded during early experience lives in the nervous system, in the gut, in the musculature, in the posture, the body language, in the baseline activation of the stress response. A person can develop genuine intellectual insight into why they react the way they do, can understand the origin of the pattern clearly and articulately, and still find the old response firing in the body before the insight has a chance to intervene. The firmware runs faster than the software can read it.

This is not a personal failure. This is architecture.

**When It Breaks**

The clearest sign that firmware is running the show is the gap between what someone knows and how they live.

The person who understands, with full intellectual clarity, that they are worthy of love, and still makes themselves small in every relationship they enter. The person who can trace their addiction to its origins with impressive precision, and cannot stop. The person who knows the critical voice in their head is distorted, exaggerated, and not to be trusted, and believes every word it says anyway. Insight without access to the firmware layer produces exactly this: people who understand their patterns completely and remain trapped in them anyway.

Trauma is perhaps the starkest example. When a threatening or overwhelming experience occurs during a critical period, it doesn't get filed as a memory in the ordinary sense. It gets written into the system as a rule about how reality works. The world is dangerous. People hurt you. You are not safe. These aren't thoughts. They are operating parameters. And because they were written into firmware rather than software, they tend to activate faster than conscious processing can intercept them, and they resist revision in ways that puzzle both the person carrying them and the clinicians trying to help.

The body keeps track in ways the mind can't always read. Chronic tension held in specific muscle groups. A digestive system calibrated for a threat level that no longer exists. A nervous system that never fully down regulates because at some point, full down regulation wasn't safe. These are not psychosomatic complaints in the dismissive sense. These are the physical artifacts of programming that was written into the body during a window that has long since closed.

**Where Psychedelics Enter**

In 2023, neuroscientist Gül Dölen and her team at Johns Hopkins published a finding that stopped a lot of people in the field.

They demonstrated that psychedelics reopen critical periods of learning in the brain. Not metaphorically. Measurably, mechanistically, at the level of the cellular and molecular markers that scientists use to track brain plasticity. In their research, compounds including psilocybin, LSD, MDMA, ibogaine, and ketamine all restored the conditions of a critical period window in adult brains, specifically the critical period for social reward learning. A window that had closed was reopened.

The implications are significant. If critical periods are when firmware gets written, and psychedelics can reopen those windows, then what becomes available in the hours and days following a session is not just a software update. It is access to a layer of the system that is ordinarily out of reach. The deeply embedded patterns, the early attachment wounds, the body-held rules about safety and worth and what is possible in this life, these become, temporarily, available for revision in a way they simply are not under ordinary conditions.

(beyond the studies, in my PERSONAL experience I believe this to be true. I witnessed this first hand)

This is why people in well-supported psychedelic sessions sometimes access material they have never been able to reach in years of conventional therapy. Not because the medicine manufactured the content. Because it reopened the level where the content lives.

The duration of the reopened window varies by compound. The research suggests weeks rather than months, which is one of the reasons what happens in the integration period following a session matters so much. The window opens. It does not stay open indefinitely. What you do inside that window, the new experiences, the therapeutic work, the relationships, the practices you bring into contact with the reopened system, all of that has an opportunity to reach a layer of programming that has not been accessible since childhood.

This is not a small thing. It is arguably the most mechanistically important development in understanding why these compounds work, and it reframes the entire conversation about what psychedelic therapy is actually doing.

It is not just an experience. It is a reopened window.

What we choose to let in through that window is everything.

Foundation 3 is what that programming produces and delivers to your awareness. That's where we go next.
