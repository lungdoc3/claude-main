# The Research Trap

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

The clinical trials are real. The results are remarkable. Treatment-resistant depression. PTSD. Addiction. OCD. Anxiety in the face of terminal illness. Study after study showing effect sizes that put the standard pharmaceutical offerings in this space to shame. Johns Hopkins. NYU. Imperial College London. This is not fringe science. The data is serious and it is accumulating.

What is also worth examining is the frame the data is being collected inside.

Every one of those trials is organized around a diagnostic category. A DSM code. A defined condition with a measurable symptom cluster and a treatment target that the existing research infrastructure knows how to evaluate. This is how clinical research works, and it works this way for reasons that are structural rather than conspiratorial. Research requires funding. Funding follows conditions that the existing system recognizes, reimburses, and cares about addressing. The diagnostic category is the unit of currency.

This produces rigorous science inside a particular set of boundaries. What it does not produce is a complete picture of what this medicine can do or who it can serve.

The population that shows up in clinical trial inclusion criteria is not the population that could benefit from this work. The person who does not meet threshold for any particular diagnosis — not depressed enough, not traumatized enough to qualify, carrying no DSM code but also not living the life they are capable of living — does not exist in this research framework. In my clinical experience, this is a large population. It is also exactly the population this medicine is often most consistently useful for. We are not generating data on them. And the absence of data will shape what becomes accessible, to whom, and under what conditions.

There is a longer consequence worth thinking about.

When FDA approval arrives — and based on the current trajectory of the evidence, it will arrive — it will come with an indication, a label, a standardized protocol, and a clinical infrastructure designed to deliver the treatment in a form the approval process can evaluate and certify. What that infrastructure can certify is safety and reproducibility. What it cannot easily certify is the relational container. The quality of preparation. The depth of integration support. The music, the setting, the permission to go wherever the medicine needs to go. Those variables cannot be standardized without being lost.

In the trials, they are controlled for. Held constant. The point is to isolate what the molecule does. The medicine that gets approved will be the medicine the trials measured — which is the molecule, delivered in a defined protocol, by a trained clinician, in a setting designed for reproducibility rather than depth.

Some of those sessions will still be extraordinary. The medicine is too potent to be entirely contained by a thin protocol. But the outcomes will be smaller than what becomes possible when the container is right, and the people who needed more than symptom relief will not always get it.

They will conclude the medicine failed.

The medicine will not have failed. The frame will have failed. But the headline will say psychedelics, and the next funding cycle will reflect it, and the opening we are in right now will be a little narrower than it was before.

The researchers doing this work understand these tensions. Many of them are working as carefully as the system allows. The constraints are not of their making. The system that funds and approves medical treatments was not designed to evaluate what makes psychedelic healing work, and it will not expand to accommodate it without pressure and time and probably some instructive failures.

What we can do, in the meantime, is be honest about what the research is measuring and what it is not. The trials answer the question the infrastructure is set up to ask. They do not answer the question that matters most: what does this medicine do for human beings when it is held well, prepared for seriously, integrated thoroughly, and offered to the full range of people it could serve?

That study has not been funded. It would be difficult to design within current methodological norms. It would not produce an approvable label in its current form.

It would, however, tell the truth.
