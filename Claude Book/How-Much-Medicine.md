# How Much Medicine

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

Let me be direct about something that does not get said often enough in this space.

The goal is not to take more medicine.

In my practice, success looks like a patient who no longer needs to see me. The treatment worked. They are better. They go live their life. That is the outcome we are working toward. The same principle applies here. A ceremony that leads to genuine, integrated change — such that you go live a different life and have no particular reason to return — is the best possible outcome. It is not a failure of the process. It is the process working.

For many people, a single well-supported experience coupled with serious integration work is enough. The door opened. They walked through it. They did not need to go back through the door again to make the most of what was on the other side.

Others will need more than one experience. The work is layered, or the first experience opened something that requires a second to complete, or life brings them back to the medicine at a later stage with a different question. This is legitimate. It is also not something you can determine from inside the glow of a recent ceremony. Give the system time to settle before you start making that assessment.

And for a small minority, the call to continue on this path of discovery is real. Not performed, not compensating for something, not a sophisticated form of avoidance. Real. Those people tend to know the difference, eventually, between that and what I am about to describe.

The Enlightenment Junkie.

Another ceremony. Another substance. A different facilitator, a different country, a different tradition. They have a thousand stories to tell, all told from the same place.

That last part is the tell. The stories accumulate. The experiences stack up. The vocabulary grows richer and the itinerary longer. And the person telling you about all of it is, somehow, still the same person they were before the first one. The experiences have not been integrated. They have been collected. And collecting is not the same as changing.

Chasing the experience is chasing the dragon. The high of the ceremony, the openness, the love, the sense of cosmic significance — it is real while it is happening. Integration is the unglamorous process of making it real in ordinary life. It is slower. It is less dramatic. It does not make for good stories at dinner parties. But it is the only part that actually matters.

Wait. This is not a small instruction. Give the system genuine time to stabilize before you entertain the idea of more. Weeks at minimum. Often months. The question of whether you need another experience cannot be answered honestly from inside the experience or from the tender period that follows it.

If you have been working with an integration specialist, a coach, or a therapist familiar with this territory, they are well positioned to help you navigate this question. Not to answer it for you. To help you find your own answer, which is a meaningfully different thing.

The medicine will be there. The question is whether you are ready to actually use it, or whether you are still processing the last time.
