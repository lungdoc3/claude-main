# Something Happened. Will This Help?
## FAQ — Grief, Loss, and Psychedelic Readiness

---

*[Note: This FAQ response addresses the most common version of the grief question — "something life-changing happened to me, and I think / wonder / hope / know psychedelics will help." The answer is organized around the questions underneath the question. Randy's voice. First-pass draft.]*

---

The question arrives in a lot of different forms. A parent died. A marriage ended. Someone I loved left, or was taken. It happened recently or it happened years ago. I wonder if this medicine would help, or I think it would, or I hope it would, or I know it would.

I get some version of this more than almost any other question. And it is a much more complicated question than most people realize they are asking.

Let me start with the part that almost nobody thinks about first.

---

**When did this happen?**

There is a meaningful difference between grief that is searing and immediate and grief that has been sitting in the body for years. Both are real. Both can be debilitating. They are not the same clinical picture, and the medicine does not meet them the same way.

If you are in it right now — if the loss is recent and the wound is still open and your nervous system is already overwhelmed — that is not the moment to add something that will amplify whatever is present. Psychedelics do not calm acute distress. They intensify what is there. A person in acute grief who sits in ceremony is not going to be gently guided toward healing. They are going to be submerged. That is not therapeutic. That is destabilizing. The timing of this matters more than most people want to hear.

The low-frequency, slow-moving grief that has been living in you for years is a different conversation. Something that settled in and quietly reorganized the architecture of your daily life without you fully noticing. That is where this work has something real to offer. But it requires you to be stable enough to go into it and come back. Stability is not the same as being okay. It is having enough ground under you to stand on.

---

**What happened, and how are you responding to it?**

The nature of the loss matters. So does something most people do not think to examine: whether your response fits the event.

Some losses are expected. A parent dying at the end of a long life is a real grief, and it deserves to be honored as one. Some losses are early or sudden or wrong in a way that leaves a different kind of mark. Some losses should not be losses at all. None of those are the same.

But here is what I actually pay attention to. Not what happened. How you are responding to it. Specifically, whether the size of your response matches the event. A person who is not functioning two years after a loss that most people navigate is telling me something important — not about the loss, but about what was already there before it. A person who is surprisingly fine three months after something that should have leveled them is also telling me something. The event is context. The response is the data.

---

**How certain are you that this will help?**

Pay attention to the word you chose when you asked the question.

I wonder sits in a different place than I think, which sits in a different place than I hope, which is very far from I know.

I wonder is genuine inquiry. It suggests you are open, you do not have the answer, and you are actually asking. That is a good place to be.

I hope is often the most honest answer in the room. Hope means you understand that nothing is guaranteed. That you need something to happen, not just something to be confirmed. People who hope are often ready in a way they do not give themselves credit for.

I know is where things get complicated. In my experience, the person who knows that this medicine will help almost never actually knows. What they have is a feeling of certainty that is doing work for them — protecting them from the real question, which is harder than whether psychedelics help. If you knew, you would not need to ask. The certainty is a signal, not an answer.

---

**What makes you think that?**

This is the question I actually ask people. Not to be difficult. Because the answer tells me almost everything.

Someone who has genuinely reflected on this will have something to say. A real observation about themselves. Something they noticed, something they have been sitting with, some honest accounting of where they are and what they think might be missing. They do not have to be certain. They just have to have actually thought about it.

A lot of people have not thought about it. They have absorbed an answer from somewhere else. Their teacher said it. A podcast said it. Someone in their community went through something similar and came back transformed and it looked like what they want. That is not thinking. That is borrowing someone else's conclusion.

I am not dismissing those paths to the question. People find their way here from all kinds of directions. But I need to know which one you came from, because the work requires you to be present with your own experience, not someone else's.

---

**Is this inquiry or validation?**

This is the harder version of that same question, and I will say it plainly because the indirect version never lands.

Most people who ask whether psychedelics will help with their grief are not actually asking. They have already decided the answer is yes. They are looking for a physician to confirm what they have already concluded, so they can proceed with less uncertainty.

That is a deeply human thing to do. I do not say it to shame anyone. But it matters enormously, because the medicine will not give you what you came looking for if what you came looking for is confirmation. It will give you what is actually there.

The person who arrives in ceremony seeking validation is going to encounter the thing underneath the question. The medicine will surface what is actually present. Whether they are ready to meet it is a different question from whether they believe they are.

---

**Or is this something else entirely?**

There are two versions of the medicine-will-take-care-of-it assumption that I have learned to listen for.

The first is a kind of spiritual overconfidence. I understand how this works. I know what the medicine does. It will take me where I need to go and I will be fine when I get back. Maybe it will. But the person who has constructed a sophisticated framework for how their healing is going to unfold is often describing how they hope it will unfold, not what is actually going to happen. The medicine is not obligated to follow anyone's script. People with very confident maps of their journey often discover that the territory has other ideas.

The second is simpler and in some ways more honest. I cannot stand this reality right now. Any other reality is better than the one I am in. That is not a healing intention. That is escapism, and the medicine knows the difference even when the person sitting with it does not. You cannot use a psychedelic to leave your grief behind. You can only use it to go further into it. The people who discover that in ceremony, unprepared, do not always come back in a better place than they left.

---

**So. Will it help?**

It might. Under the right conditions, approached with genuine honesty about what is actually happening in you, this work has helped people carry grief differently. Not less — differently. The loss does not go away. The relationship to it can change. That is not nothing. It is sometimes everything.

But here is what the question really requires from you, before I can answer it.

Honest accounting of the timing. Honest accounting of your response, separate from the event itself. Real examination of how certain you are, and what that certainty is doing. A genuine answer to what makes you think this will help, in your own words, from your own reflection. And a clear look at whether you are seeking something real or seeking an exit from a reality you would rather not be in.

If you can sit with those questions and answer them honestly, the answer to whether this work is right for you right now will usually become clear. That clarity is not always what people came looking for. It is always more useful than the alternative.
