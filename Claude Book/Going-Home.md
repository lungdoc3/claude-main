# Going Home for the Holidays

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

Everyone loves the idea of going home for the holidays.

And then they go home for the holidays.

Within twenty minutes of walking through the door, the version of you that has done years of work, that has sat in ceremony, that has made genuine and hard-won changes to how you move through your life, has quietly left the building. Your sibling says the thing they always say. Your parent does the thing they always do. Someone brings up the topic that has always been a flashpoint, in exactly the tone it has always been raised in, and you respond exactly as you always have, and somewhere in the back of your mind a small voice notes that this is not who you are anymore.

Except right now it is.

This is not a failure of the work you have done. It is one of the most reliable dynamics in human psychology, and understanding it is essential for anyone who has done a deep ceremony and is trying to carry the changes back into their ordinary life.

## The System Has a Role for You

Every system you belong to, your family, your friend group, your workplace, your marriage, has achieved a kind of equilibrium. Each person in the system occupies a role, plays a character, fulfills a function that the system has come to depend on. The peacemaker. The difficult one. The responsible one. The one who always deflects with humor. The one who carries the emotional weight. These roles were not formally assigned. They emerged over years of interaction until they became load-bearing: the system is structured around them, and the system will work, with considerable force and zero malice, to keep everyone in their designated place.

When one part of a system changes, the system experiences it as a threat to its stability. Not consciously. Not with intention. Systemically. The pressure to restore equilibrium is automatic and powerful, and it comes from every direction simultaneously. Your mother's tone of voice. Your brother's familiar joke at your expense. The old argument that surfaces the same way it always has. None of these people have gathered to conspire against your growth. They are simply running their own autopilots, and their autopilots have you cast in a very specific role, and the pull to step into that role is nearly irresistible because it is woven into every cue in the environment.

This is why going home for the holidays is so reliably humbling. The setting itself is a trigger. The smells, the rooms, the seating arrangements, the rhythms of the household: all of it is associated with the old character at a level below conscious awareness. The firmware recognizes the environment and loads the corresponding behavioral package before you have had a chance to decide whether you want it running.

## The Casting Problem at Scale

The insight from the previous chapter about refusing the role your wife casts you in applies here too, but the degree of difficulty is different. Declining a role when one person is casting you is challenging. Declining a role when an entire system is casting you simultaneously, when every person in the room is running a version of you that matches the old character, when the physical environment itself is priming the old responses: this is substantially harder.

And the same dynamic operates outside the family. You go back to work after a ceremony that genuinely changed something. Your colleagues are friendly, familiar, and absolutely certain they know who you are. The office has a role for you. Your friend group has a role for you. The conversations find their familiar grooves. The old jokes land. The old complaints get aired. And without any conscious decision to abandon what opened in ceremony, you find yourself back inside the old character because the social environment rebuilt it around you while you were just trying to get through the day.

This is one of the most underappreciated challenges in integration work, and it is the reason the weeks after ceremony deserve as much intention as the ceremony itself.

## What Is Actually Happening

The people in your life are not trying to undermine your growth. Most of them are not even aware they are doing anything. They are carrying a version of you that was accurate for a long time, that helped the system function, and that they have no particular reason to update. The casting is not hostile. It is habitual.

But habitual casting is still casting. And a character that the whole room is performing at you is a powerful thing to resist, especially when part of you remembers how comfortable it was to play that role, how little it required, how well it fit.

The work here is the same work, scaled up. Recognize the casting before you step into the role. Notice the environmental cues that are loading the old behavioral package. Choose, in each specific moment, who you are going to be rather than accepting the version the system is offering.

This does not mean announcing your transformation to your family at the dinner table. It does not mean refusing to engage with the people who matter to you. It means something quieter and more continuous: simply declining, again and again, to be the character they are expecting, not through confrontation but through the steady presence of someone who has become someone slightly different and is not going to pretend otherwise.

Some systems will update. When you stop playing your role reliably, the system has to adjust. Other members recalibrate. New dynamics become possible. The whole cast begins, gradually, to play a different scene. This is often how the most meaningful relational changes happen after ceremony: not through direct confrontation but through the persistent refusal to perform the old story.

Some systems cannot accommodate the new character. This is the harder truth. There are friend groups built around a shared dynamic that requires everyone to stay in their role. There are family systems so rigidly structured that any genuine change by one member is experienced as an existential threat to the whole. When the new character keeps showing up and the system cannot adapt, something has to give. Sometimes what gives is the relationship.

This is a real loss and deserves to be named as one. The grief of outgrowing a friendship, of watching a relationship fail not because of conflict but because of growth, is genuine. The work does not promise that everything stays intact. It promises that what remains will be real.

## Before You Go Home

The practical wisdom is this: the holidays will come. The office will be there Monday morning. The friend group will want to get together. These are not threats to your integration. They are its proving ground. The ceremony opened something. The question is whether you can stay open inside the environments that were built around the closed version of you.

You will not always manage it. There will be dinners where the old character shows up despite everything, where the firmware runs faster than the awareness can catch it, where you find yourself in the middle of the old dynamic before you knew it was happening. This is not failure. It is information about where the work continues.

What you are building, slowly and imperfectly, across every return to every system that has you cast: the capacity to be yourself in rooms that were designed for someone else.

That is not a small thing. That is the whole project.
