# The Diagnostic Dilemma

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

When we label an experience, we stabilize it. We give it boundaries. We convert a dynamic process into a noun and proceed as though that noun represents a discrete, enduring entity.

This has practical advantages. Without shared labels, communication becomes difficult. A diagnosis allows a physician in Boston and a specialist in Houston to discuss the same patient without starting from scratch. It allows research to accumulate. It allows insurance to pay. Concepts allow us to point toward experience, even when they cannot fully capture it.

But naming also carries a cost. Once a state is labeled, it tends to persist. It begins to organize future experience around itself, with all of the associated expectations and behaviors quietly falling into step. The label, which was supposed to describe the experience, starts to produce it.

I learned this slowly, across years of clinical practice. I became cautious about handing out a diagnosis. Diagnosis is often necessary and genuinely useful. My caution was about what I watched happen after.

"You know I can't do that, Dr. Evans. I have COPD."

I heard some version of this regularly. The patient sitting across from me had pulmonary function that was, on paper, near normal. The condition was real, the history was real, but the physiological limitation had improved significantly. What had not improved was the story. The diagnosis had become the primary organizing principle of a life. It constrained behavior that the body no longer needed to constrain. The belief was more powerful than the biology, and medicine had handed the belief over with the best of intentions and a prescription pad.

## The First Invitation

A diagnosis is an invitation. Several invitations, actually. The first is the most seductive: an invitation to offload ownership.

Suffering is heavy. The question of why you feel the way you feel, why your life looks the way it looks, why nothing seems to shift no matter what you try: that question is exhausting to carry. A diagnosis hands the weight to a third party. The condition did this. The chemistry did this. Something outside the self, with a name and a mechanism and a literature, is responsible. You are not failing. You have something.

This is not dishonest, exactly. The condition is real. The relief of finally having an explanation is real. But the relief comes with a cost that rarely gets named in the consultation room: the same move that transfers the blame transfers the agency. If the diagnosis caused the problem, the diagnosis controls the solution. You become a passenger in your own recovery, waiting for medicine to fix the thing medicine told you was broken.

It is a very convenient arrangement. For everyone.

The physician gets a tractable problem. You can treat a label. Prescribe to it, monitor it, adjust the protocol. Treating a person (their history, their patterns, their relationship to their own suffering, the upstream causes of the downstream symptoms) is considerably messier and does not fit neatly into a fifteen-minute appointment. The diagnostic category makes the transaction manageable. Both parties leave having done what was expected of them. The system is satisfied.

The patient's suffering often is not. But that is a problem for the next visit.

## The Second Invitation

The second invitation is subtler, and the ego is the one being invited.

A diagnosis, particularly the right diagnosis, offers something the ego finds deeply appealing: identity. Community. An explanation that converts private confusion into a recognized condition. Reduced expectations from a world that was demanding too much. A language for describing yourself that other people already understand.

The ego is doing what egos do in the presence of genuine suffering: reaching for solid ground. People find their diagnosis and feel, for the first time, that they are not crazy, not weak, not uniquely broken. They find others who share the experience. That recognition is real and it matters.

And the ego, which is always looking for a stable platform to stand on, sometimes does not stop there.

Medical fashions are real. Mitral valve prolapse had an extended run: fatigue, palpitations, anxiety, a constellation of symptoms that were genuinely distressing and genuinely difficult to pin down. Fibromyalgia. Chronic Lyme. Each had their moment. Each offered an identity scaffold that explained suffering without asking the sufferer to examine the life the suffering was embedded in.

The Spectrum is currently surging in ways that deserve honest attention. Autism spectrum disorder is real. The expansion of diagnostic criteria, the improved recognition, the reduction in stigma: these are real developments that have helped real people. And the ego, which reaches for whatever scaffolding is available, has noticed that this particular diagnosis comes with a coherent identity, a vibrant community, a compelling explanation for social difficulty, and a significantly reduced set of social obligations. That is attractive scaffolding. The ego does not need to be cynical to reach for it. It just needs to be looking for somewhere to stand.

The diagnosis becomes the person. The person becomes the diagnosis. Inquiry stops. Curiosity fades. Medicine believes it knows what it is dealing with and, in doing so, risks missing it entirely.

## The Dilemma

This is the bind that clinicians rarely discuss with each other, let alone with patients.

The diagnosis is necessary. Without it, insurance does not pay, research does not accumulate, and the patient cannot communicate their experience to the next physician who sees them. The label is a practical requirement of the system we are operating inside.

The diagnosis is also, in ways that are difficult to quantify and easy to overlook, potentially disabling. The name of the condition. The story that forms around the name. The identity that crystallizes. The agency that quietly exits the room.

To diagnose or not to diagnose. The question sounds simple and is not. Most clinicians resolve it by not asking it, defaulting to diagnosis because the system requires it and moving on. The downstream consequences belong to someone else's appointment.

There is more to this than most clinicians realize, and more than most patients are ever told. The label is not neutral. It is an invitation, and the person accepting it deserves to know what they are accepting.

---

*This matters for the work this book is about. Psychedelic healing does not require a diagnosis. What it requires is a person willing to examine the experience beneath the label: the actual texture of the suffering, its origins, its function, what it has been protecting and what it has been costing. The diagnostic category may point in the right direction. It is rarely the destination.*
