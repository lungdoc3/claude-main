# Choosing Your Guide

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

I want to say something plainly, because it does not get said plainly enough in this space: the training undergone by a traditional ayahuascero is, in many respects, as demanding as any western medical training program. In some respects, it is more demanding. The intellectual commitment is serious. The physical requirements are serious. Years of restriction, isolation, extended dieta, and accumulated time in ceremony are not undertaken casually. And the spiritual dimension of that training, which western medicine does not have a category for and therefore tends to dismiss, is where the hardest work lives.

I say this not to romanticize. I say it because when western-trained physicians encounter indigenous healing traditions, the default assumption is often that one system is rigorous and accountable and the other is folk medicine dressed in ceremony. That assumption is wrong, and it matters that it is wrong, because it shapes how people approach the choice of who will hold their ceremony.

A traditional healer who has completed a genuine apprenticeship has done something serious. It deserves to be treated as such.

Here is where the analogy with western medicine breaks down, and where the problem begins.

## The Information Problem

If I want to know something about a physician, I can find it. Medical licensing is public. Disciplinary actions are searchable. Board certifications are verifiable. Patient reviews exist, with all their limitations. There is a bad doctor list. There is a paper trail.

If I want to know something about the ayahuascero who will be pouring my medicine next month, I have almost nothing. The credentials that matter in that world (lineage, apprenticeship, years of practice) are not centrally recorded anywhere accessible to an outsider. They are known within communities that are not easy to access, passed along through networks of trust that take years to develop, and almost entirely invisible to someone approaching this work for the first time.

Commercial retreat centers add another layer of complexity. The center may have a legitimate reputation. The guide may have been trained well. Or may not. The guest has limited means to distinguish between these situations, and the stakes are not low.

This is not an argument against the work. It is an argument for approaching the choice of guide with the same care you would bring to choosing a surgeon. More, actually, because the information is harder to find and the accountability structures that exist in medicine do not exist here.

## What Lineage Actually Means

The ayahuasca world talks extensively about lineage. It is worth understanding what that conversation is and is not about.

Lineage matters because it represents a chain of transmission. A healer with lineage was taught by someone, who was taught by someone, who was taught by someone, in a tradition that has been developed and refined over generations. What that means practically is that there is a body of knowledge, a set of practices, a context for understanding what the medicine does and how to work with it responsibly. There is something to be accountable to beyond one's own judgment.

What lineage does not mean is a hierarchy of traditions. There is no better lineage and no worse lineage. The question is whether there is one. A guide with genuine lineage in any established tradition has demonstrated a commitment to the work and a willingness to submit to a process of learning. That matters.

Some of the most skilled and powerful healers I have sat with do not come from a single lineage. They trained across multiple traditions, integrated the teachings, and built something that draws from many wells. This is not a disqualification. It is often a sign of serious engagement with the work. What I am looking for is not the specific lineage but evidence that one exists, meaning that someone, somewhere, was accountable for this person's training.

## What a Good Guide Actually Does

There is a misunderstanding, particularly common among people new to this space, that serving the medicine means pouring the medicine. It does not. Pouring the medicine is the beginning of the job description.

The minimum that a competent guide brings to ceremony begins with their own experience of the medicine. They have been where you are going. Not a single brief excursion. Substantial, repeated experience with the specific medicine they are serving. This matters for reasons that are both practical and relational. Practically, they will not panic at what they witness, because they have been there. Relationally, there is something in the room when a guide has genuine familiarity with the territory. It registers below the level of conscious awareness, and it steadies the space.

The capacity to create and hold a therapeutic relationship is a separate skill from medicine knowledge, and it is undervalued. What happens in a ceremony is not purely pharmacological. How safe you feel, how seen, how steady the presence is that holds the space: all of it shapes the experience in ways that go well beyond the compound in the cup. A guide who is knowledgeable about the medicine but cannot hold a therapeutic relationship is like a surgeon who knows the anatomy and cannot read the room.

A good guide understands the arc of the specific experience they are facilitating. The timing of onset. The quality of the peak. When intensity is normal and when it is a signal that something needs attention. When to move toward a participant and when to leave them completely alone, which is just as important. Reading the room is not intuition. It is a skill built from experience, and it is the difference between a ceremony that is held well and one that is not.

And beneath all of it sits a genuine and deep understanding of what these medicines are doing to the human mind and nervous system. Not necessarily a western clinical understanding, though that is useful. Some form of knowledge about what is happening and why. This is what allows a guide to be with difficulty without being afraid of it, to recognize what they are seeing, to know what the experience is asking for.

## The Hollow Bone

In the shaman training I underwent, I was introduced to a concept that has stayed with me longer than almost anything else from that period. The hollow bone.

The idea is this: as a healer, your function is to open and maintain the portal between worlds. What passes through that portal is the healing and the wisdom. Your job is to be the channel through which those things move. And for that to work, for the energy to pass through cleanly and arrive intact, the channel must be clean. Dirty pipes corrupt what flows through them. Your unresolved material, your unexamined wounds, your needs and fears and projections: these are the contaminants. If they are present in the channel, they are present in the ceremony.

A more colloquial translation: know what is your shit and keep it off the dance floor.

This is what distinguishes the healers who consistently do transformative work from those who do not. Not the volume of their training. Not the prestige of their lineage. Not the number of ceremonies they have sat. Whether they have done, and continue to do, the work on themselves that keeps their own material from becoming part of your ceremony.

A guide who has not done that work is not holding a space for your healing. They are having an experience at your expense, in a setting where you are wide open and have no defenses and cannot easily tell the difference.

## Who Goes Wrong, and How

I want to be clear before naming what can go wrong: most facilitators, in my experience, are good people doing this work with genuine skill and intention. The field is full of remarkable healers. What follows is not a counsel of despair. It is an honest map of the terrain.

The first category of concern is the guide who is good-hearted but undertrained. These are people who have had a genuine experience, sometimes a series of them, and from those experiences have drawn the conclusion that they are called to serve. The experiences were real. The calling feels real. The problem is that feeling called to serve the medicine is not the same as being prepared to serve it, any more than surviving a difficult surgery makes you qualified to perform one.

What this represents, when you look at it clearly, is a misunderstanding of what is at stake. Or, more charitably, a misunderstanding of how much there is to learn. These medicines are not toys. The experience of a person in ceremony can be one of the most profound and vulnerable states a human being ever enters. To hold that requires preparation that goes significantly beyond one's own experience with the medicine.

I will say something that needs to be said: the "medicine called me" framing concerns me when it is used as a substitute for training rather than a beginning of it. The genuine call to this work, in my observation, is accompanied by humility about one's own preparation and a deep respect for the traditions and the teachers who carry them. A person who bypasses serious training because the spirit told them to go straight to serving is not honoring those traditions. They are doing something else.

The second category is the guide who was once competent and has lost their way. This is the harder one to talk about and the harder one to see from the outside. Something changed. The practice drifted. The ego reassembled in a form the person cannot recognize. The accountability structures that once existed have fallen away, the teacher, the community, the peer group, and without them the drift goes unchecked. These practitioners often have the language of the work and the history of the work, and neither of those things tells you where they are now.

The third category I will address at length in the chapter that follows. It is the most insidious and the most dangerous.

## The Psilocybin Question

Across all of these medicines, the risk of encountering an undertrained or compromised guide is real. But it is not evenly distributed.

The ayahuasca tradition, peyote, San Pedro: these have higher barriers to entry. The medicine is not easy to access. The traditions that carry these practices are established and, within their communities, relatively transparent about training and standards. The barriers are not perfect protection. But they filter for commitment.

Psilocybin is a different situation. The barrier to entry is as low as it has ever been for any psychedelic in modern western history. The access is near-ubiquitous. The established protocols and agreed-upon practices that govern the traditional medicine world are largely absent. And in that vacuum, a significant number of people have had a powerful experience with psilocybin and concluded that the next step is to guide others through one.

The mushroom called them. They are certain of it.

Some of these people will do good work. Some will learn, over time, and become genuinely skilled. But the current landscape contains a large number of practitioners offering psilocybin facilitation with preparation that ranges from serious to minimal, and limited means for a first-time participant to tell the difference. This is a real public health consideration, and it will not be resolved until the field develops the credentialing and accountability structures that do not yet exist.

## What This Means for You

The choice of guide is not a logistical decision. It is a clinical one. You are entrusting your nervous system to another person during one of the most vulnerable states a human being can enter. The vetting deserves proportional care.

The good news is that the field is full of remarkable, dedicated, skilled practitioners who take the responsibility seriously. Finding one is not impossible. It requires asking questions that many people in this space are not used to being asked, and paying careful attention to how those questions are received.

A guide who welcomes your scrutiny has earned the first point of trust.

A guide who frames your questions as resistance, or who is vague about their lineage and training, or who cannot tell you who holds them accountable: these are not reasons to walk away automatically. They are reasons to ask more questions.

The medicine deserves a worthy vessel. So do you.
