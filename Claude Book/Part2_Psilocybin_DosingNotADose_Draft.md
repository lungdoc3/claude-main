# A Dose Is Not a Dose

*Part 2: The Substances — Psilocybin chapter, aside*
*Status: Draft, Critic-reviewed (Excellent after revision)*
*Pairs with the spoon piece in the Ayahuasca chapter — same question, different answer*

---

People ask me how many grams the right dose is more often than they ask me almost anything else about this medicine. I understand why. It sounds like a question with a number for an answer, the way a prescription does. It isn't.

Working with psilocybin well requires someone who actually understands the variables sitting underneath that number, and there are more of them than most people expect. Some live in the mushroom. Some live in the person eating it. None of them show up on the bag.

Start with the mushroom itself. Potency shifts with species and strain, with the point in the grow cycle it was picked, with how it was dried, with whether it gets eaten raw, steeped into a tea, or soaked in lemon juice before it's taken. Two caps that look identical sitting next to each other on a plate can carry meaningfully different amounts of active compound. Someone with real experience growing and serving this medicine can estimate potency reasonably well once they know the strain and the harvest, the way an experienced cook can tell you roughly how much heat a pepper is going to bring without measuring it. It isn't an exact science. There is still something real there, a working knowledge built from doing it enough times to recognize the patterns. But it is an estimate, not a measurement, and anyone who tells you otherwise hasn't grown enough mushrooms.

Then there's the person eating them. Bodyweight, personal metabolism, tolerance built or not built from past experience, all of it changes what a given dose actually does once it's in the body. A good guide interviews someone about their history before they ever serve them, asking what they've taken before, how it went, whether they're familiar with what practitioners call the shift, that specific moment a dose tips from felt to total. Someone who has sat with this medicine many times develops a working sense of what to expect from a given amount, the same way the man with the spoon in the ayahuasca chapter has built a working sense of his own, even though the variable he's tracking was never the one that mattered most.

Here's a fact most people never hear, and it's the clearest proof that a dose is not a dose. Clinical trials studying psilocybin for depression do not use mushrooms. They use synthetic psilocybin, manufactured in a lab to pharmaceutical standard, the same way every tablet of a conventional drug is manufactured to the same standard. Science needs a known, reproducible dose before it can draw any honest conclusion about what that dose did. Without that, a study isn't measuring the effect of psilocybin. It's measuring the combined effect of a dozen variables nobody controlled for, species, strain, harvest, drying, preparation, body, history, and calling the result a dose-response curve when it never was one.

That standard cannot be replicated outside a lab. Wanting the exact dose used in the Johns Hopkins depression trial isn't a real request, because that dose only ever existed as a manufactured compound under controlled conditions. The moment you're holding an actual mushroom, grown somewhere, dried somehow, by someone, you've already left the world where that number applies. It is genuinely impossible, not merely difficult, to know the exact dose in a natural preparation.

This is also why clinical research doesn't really work with the natural substance at all. A study needs to isolate one variable and hold everything else still. Mushrooms refuse to hold still. Different species carry meaningfully different strengths to begin with, which is part of why the legal psilocybin therapy centers in Oregon are restricted by law to a single species, Psilocybe cubensis, the one most people who've ever taken mushrooms have actually eaten. Even that restriction only gets you so far. Cubensis itself varies widely from one grow to the next, which means even the most carefully regulated legal framework available today is still working with an estimate dressed up as a standard.

None of that makes the estimate worthless. It makes the person serving the medicine the actual instrument, not the scale. A guide who has grown this particular batch, who knows its strain and its harvest and how it's been prepared, who has sat with enough people to recognize what a given range tends to do, carries something a label never will. The mushroom won't tell you what it is. The server has to already know.

That's true of psilocybin. It's even more true of grandmother. Neither of them was ever going to read the measurement anyway. They both need someone in the room who has learned to read them instead.
