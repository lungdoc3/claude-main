# When the World Changes
## Part 4 — Emergency Management

---

*[Note: This section addresses what facilitators should do when a medical emergency occurs in ceremony. It explicitly declines to teach clinical intervention — that framing is the argument. First-pass draft in Randy's voice.]*

---

**When the World Changes**

This section will not teach you to manage a hypertensive crisis. It will not walk you through the clinical signs of serotonin syndrome or instruct you on how to interpret what you are seeing and respond accordingly. The reason is not that those things are unimportant. It is that giving you that information would create exactly the wrong outcome. A facilitator who has read enough to believe they can identify and manage a medical emergency is more dangerous than one who knows clearly that they cannot. False competence in high-stakes situations does not split the difference between trained and untrained. It delays the call that needed to be made ten minutes ago.

This is not the world for "I think I can."

---

**The Lumbar Puncture Lesson**

In medical training there is a procedure called a lumbar puncture, a spinal tap in common language, that junior physicians approach with considerable anxiety. It is invasive. It can be painful. There is real skill involved, and the possibility of failure is real. And so the intern, when confronted with a patient who might need one, spends an extraordinary amount of mental energy finding reasons why it is probably not necessary. The anxiety is real. The hope that waiting will spare everyone the difficulty is real. The clinical reasoning constructed in service of that hope can be surprisingly sophisticated.

The teaching that eventually lands, usually delivered by a Fellow who has been through it, is this: if it crossed your mind, do it. There is a reason it crossed your mind. The clinical instinct that raised the question is carrying information your conscious reasoning is working to suppress. Waiting will not be the right answer.

The parallel to ceremony emergency management is exact. If the thought of calling for help crosses your mind, make the call. Not when you are certain. Not after you have monitored the situation for another twenty minutes and tried everything you can think of. The moment the thought arrives. Because there is a reason it arrived. And whatever is happening is not going to resolve itself because you willed it to.

---

**The Shift**

When a medical emergency occurs in ceremony, something changes. The container does not disappear. The other participants in the room still need you, still need the space held, still need someone present and calm at the center of whatever is unfolding. That does not stop. But a part of your responsibility has shifted into entirely different territory, and the most important thing you can do is recognize that shift clearly and without hesitation.

You are no longer operating in the spiritual realm. Not fully. Not in that moment. You are the person responsible for another human being's physical safety, and the tools of that responsibility are not ceremony tools. They are a phone and an honest voice and the willingness to step outside the container long enough to get qualified help moving toward you.

The facilitator who spends precious minutes trying to manage this within the ceremony, trying to hold it inside the space, trying to avoid the disruption of outside intervention, is making a choice. Sometimes it will be fine. Sometimes it will not. The calculus is not complicated: the only thing worse than an ambulance arriving at your ceremony is a guest who needed one and did not get it.

---

**What Preparation Actually Looks Like**

Get certified in CPR. Not because you will run a resuscitation. Because doing it changes who you are in a crisis. The training puts your hands on a chest, puts the physical reality of emergency care in your body rather than your imagination. You breathe differently when you have done it. You are less likely to freeze.

Have an AED on site. Know how to use it.

Know where the nearest hospital is. Know the address of your location and have it written down somewhere you can find it fast, not somewhere you will have to search for it while your hands are shaking.

---

**The Call**

Call 911 the moment the thought crosses your mind that you might need to. That threshold is the entire teaching.

When you call, be honest. Tell the operator that you have a guest who is having a reaction to a psychedelic substance. Tell them which substance if you know it. Do not soften this, do not rephrase it, do not construct a version of events that protects the ceremony at the expense of the information the responding team needs. As a physician who spent a career on the receiving end of these calls, I can tell you with certainty: when we ask what someone took, that is all we care about. We are not surprised. We are not calling law enforcement based on your answer. We need to know what we are walking into so we can help. What you do not tell us will cost us time we may not have.

Tell the operator that you have other people on site who are in a vulnerable state and that you would like the team to approach without lights and sirens if at all possible. That is a reasonable request. Make it calmly and early in the call. Be prepared to explain why. You have a group in a healing context and an unexpected arrival will cause significant distress to people who are already in altered states. Dispatchers are human. A clear, calm explanation goes further than you expect.

Have someone meet them outside. Decide now, before you ever need it, who that person will be. They should know the address, know the guest's name, know the basic facts of what happened. They should be able to walk the team in calmly without requiring direction from you in the moment.

Use your helpers. The instinct in a crisis is to try to manage everything yourself, especially if you are the one who holds the container. Resist it. The people you trust in that room can hold the group while you step into this other responsibility. Let them.

---

**Call Because You Can**

There will be resistance to making that call. Some of it will be fear of legal consequences. Some of it will be the trained instinct to protect the container from intrusion. Some of it will be the hope, gathering force with every passing minute, that the situation will resolve and the call will turn out to have been unnecessary.

It is far easier to make that call because you can than because you have to.

The facilitator who calls early, explains clearly, and gets the team there before the situation becomes critical has done their job. If it turns out the guest was fine and the response was precautionary, that is not a failure. That is exactly what responsible stewardship looks like. Nobody gets a second chance at the alternative.
