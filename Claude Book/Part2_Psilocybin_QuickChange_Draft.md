# The Quick Change

*Part 2: The Substances — Psilocybin chapter, aside*
*Status: Draft, Critic-reviewed (Excellent), revised to correct horse/gate mapping (psilocybin is the horse, the diagnosis is the gate)*
*Reuses the Trojan Horse analogy established earlier in the book, applied to the individual patient rather than the institution*

---

The Trojan horse got psychedelic research back through the gates of medicine. The same trick runs again, in miniature, on almost every person who walks through the door specifically looking for depression relief.

Here's how it works. Someone arrives with a diagnosis, a referral, a treatment plan that says major depressive disorder, approved and billed and documented. That paperwork is the gate, small and specific, exactly the shape the front door was built to let something through. The thing that actually walks through it is psilocybin itself, dressed up as the antidepressant the diagnosis called for, looking as tame and single-purpose as the paperwork needs it to look. That disguise does double duty. It's also the reason this particular person takes the whole thing seriously enough to do it properly, screening, prep, a guide who knows what they're doing, integration on the other side, instead of eating a baggie of mushrooms at a festival because a friend said it might help. The diagnosis buys the container. People who come in through the depression door tend to build the kind of structure the medicine actually deserves, because the door made them think it mattered enough to.

Then the costume comes off, and the medicine does what it was never advertised as doing. It treats the depression, often. It also opens something considerably larger than the paperwork said it would, a sense of the divine, a dissolving of the boundary that usually separates a person from everything else, an experience that for most people lands somewhere in the top three or five most meaningful things that have ever happened to them, filed right next to the birth of a child or the death of someone they loved. Nobody wrote that on the intake form. The horse was never just carrying an antidepressant.

This is the trickster again, the same one from a few pages back, working both sides of her reputation at once. She took the invitation meant for treating one specific condition and used it to walk in carrying something else entirely. Cabaret and vaudeville have a word for this move, the quick change, a performer who steps behind a screen in one costume and reappears a heartbeat later in a completely different one, the transformation happening too fast for the audience to ever catch the seam. The most famous quick change artist in history was Italian, Leopoldo Fregoli, so good at vanishing into someone else entirely that his own name became the word psychiatry now uses for the delusion of seeing one person hiding inside another. Psilocybin pulls the same act on a diagnosis. It walks onstage dressed as an antidepressant. By the time the lights come back up, it's wearing something else completely, and nobody in the room saw the change happen.

It's a Trojan horse that delivers more than it promised. The healing on the other side of that door is bigger than what depression treatment, on its own, was ever built to deliver.
