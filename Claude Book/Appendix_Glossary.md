# Glossary
## Appendix

---

*Terms are defined as they are used in this book. Where a clinical term has a precise technical meaning and a common colloquial meaning, both are noted. Cross-references appear in italics.*

---

**5-HT2A Receptor**
A specific serotonin receptor subtype concentrated in the prefrontal cortex. The primary target of all classical psychedelics — psilocybin, mescaline, LSD, DMT. When activated, it loosens the ordinary dominance of the default mode network and produces the perceptual and psychological shifts characteristic of the psychedelic experience. Blocked by most antipsychotic medications and mirtazapine. See also: *Agonist, Antagonist, Downregulation.*

**AED (Automated External Defibrillator)**
A portable device that analyzes heart rhythm and delivers an electrical shock if a life-threatening arrhythmia is detected. Designed for use by non-medical personnel. The machine performs the diagnostic work; the operator attaches the pads and follows audio instructions. Recommended equipment for any ceremony setting.

**Agonist**
A molecule that binds a receptor and activates it, producing the same downstream effect as the receptor's natural chemical signal. The classical psychedelics are agonists at the *5-HT2A receptor*. Contrast with *Antagonist.*

**Antagonist**
A molecule that binds a receptor and blocks it, preventing the natural signal from doing its work. Antipsychotic medications are antagonists at the *5-HT2A receptor* — they occupy the receptor without activating it, which is why they block psychedelic effects and reduce psychotic symptoms. Contrast with *Agonist.*

**Arrhythmia**
An abnormal heart rhythm. Can range from benign to life-threatening. Relevant in the psychedelic context because classical psychedelics produce temporary increases in heart rate. Participants with a known arrhythmia should discuss with a cardiologist before ceremony. See also: *The Cardiologist Question.*

**Ayahuasca**
A plant medicine preparation originating in the Amazon basin, traditionally combining *Banisteriopsis caapi* (the vine, which contains *harmine*, *harmaline*, and *tetrahydroharmine*) with a *DMT*-containing plant such as *Psychotria viridis* (chacruna) or *Diplopterys cabrerana* (chaliponga). The combination is pharmacologically intentional: the *MAOI* component of the vine prevents the gut from breaking down DMT, allowing it to reach the brain. Neither plant produces the full experience alone. Also called "the medicine," "Grandmother," or by various indigenous names.

**Banisteriopsis caapi**
The ayahuasca vine, one of two core plants in the traditional ayahuasca preparation. Contains the *beta-carboline* alkaloids *harmine*, *harmaline*, and *tetrahydroharmine* (THH). Provides the *MAOI* mechanism that makes oral *DMT* bioavailable. The vine alone does not produce a classic ayahuasca experience, but is used medicinally in some traditions on its own.

**Benzodiazepine**
A class of medications that enhance the effect of GABA, the brain's primary inhibitory neurotransmitter, producing sedation, anxiety reduction, and muscle relaxation. Common examples include diazepam (Valium), lorazepam (Ativan), and alprazolam (Xanax). Blunt the psychedelic experience through general CNS sedation. Not recommended for routine pre-ceremony use; available as an emergency tool for overwhelming experiences.

**Beta-blocker**
A class of medications that block adrenergic receptors, reducing heart rate and blood pressure. Commonly prescribed for hypertension, heart conditions, and anxiety. Generally do not block the psychedelic experience. Exception: carvedilol has documented *5-HT2A* activity and may attenuate effects through that mechanism.

**Beta-carboline**
A class of naturally occurring alkaloids found in *Banisteriopsis caapi* and various other plants. The primary beta-carbolines in ayahuasca — *harmine*, *harmaline*, and *tetrahydroharmine* — are responsible for the *MAOI* activity that makes the preparation work. Also found in smaller quantities in passionflower, Syrian rue, and tobacco.

**Bipolar Disorder**
A psychiatric condition characterized by cycling between manic and depressive episodes, sometimes with periods of stability between. Not an absolute contraindication for psychedelic work, but a significant risk factor for destabilization. Each mood episode can lower the threshold for future episodes. Requires case-by-case evaluation by a mental health professional familiar with both the patient and the medicine.

**Blood-Brain Barrier**
A selective membrane that separates the brain's blood supply from the brain itself, filtering out many substances while allowing others to pass. Relevant because *DMT* must cross it to produce psychedelic effects — which it cannot do in meaningful quantities when given orally without *MAOI* inhibition of gut metabolism.

**Ceremony**
The structured, intentional context in which plant medicine is administered and held. Led by a facilitator or traditional practitioner, ceremony provides the *container* — physical, relational, and in many traditions cosmological — within which the experience unfolds. The structure is not decoration around the medicine. The intention, the relationships, the held space, and the sequence of the work are part of how the medicine does what it does. Used throughout this book to mean any facilitated psychedelic experience held within a deliberate framework, whether derived from indigenous tradition, contemporary therapeutic practice, or some integration of the two. See also: *Container, Set and Setting.*

**Chaliponga (Diplopterys cabrerana)**
An admixture plant used in some ayahuasca traditions as an alternative or addition to *Psychotria viridis*. Contains *DMT* and, notably, 5-MeO-DMT, producing a somewhat different experience profile than chacruna-based preparations.

**Chacruna (Psychotria viridis)**
The most commonly used *DMT*-containing admixture plant in traditional ayahuasca preparations. The leaves provide the primary psychoactive compound. Alone, oral consumption produces no significant effect due to rapid metabolism of DMT by gut MAO. Combined with *Banisteriopsis caapi*, the experience becomes fully active.

**Classical Psychedelics**
A pharmacological category including psilocybin, LSD, mescaline, DMT, and related compounds. Defined by their primary mechanism: *agonism* at the *5-HT2A receptor*. Distinguished from other psychoactive substances (ketamine, MDMA, ibogaine) that produce altered states through different mechanisms. The term "classical" reflects this shared receptor pharmacology, not a cultural or legal classification.

**Container**
In ceremony contexts, the held space — physical, relational, and energetic — within which the experience unfolds. Created and maintained by the facilitator. A strong container provides the safety and structure that allows participants to move through difficult experiences without fragmentation. Not a clinical term; the concept of a held therapeutic frame has functional equivalents in psychotherapy.

**CPR (Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation)**
An emergency procedure combining chest compressions and rescue breathing to maintain circulation and oxygenation when the heart has stopped. Certification is recommended for all facilitators — not because facilitators are expected to run a resuscitation, but because the training changes how the body responds to a crisis. Less freezing. More capacity to act.

**Critical Period**
A developmental window during which the brain is unusually plastic — open to certain types of learning and experience in a way that is not possible before or after. Early childhood is the most consequential critical period for attachment, emotional regulation, and social learning. Relevant to psychedelic pharmacology because research by Gül Dölen and others has demonstrated that psychedelics can reopen critical periods in adult brains, temporarily restoring the plasticity of those early windows.

**Curandero / Ayahuascero**
A traditional healer in Amazonian traditions, trained in the use of plant medicines through an apprenticeship that may span many years. The terms are used somewhat interchangeably, though ayahuascero specifically refers to a practitioner whose primary medicine is ayahuasca. These practitioners work within a cosmological framework that predates western pharmacology and was developed for populations who were not taking prescription medications.

**Default Mode Network (DMN)**
A network of brain regions that is most active during self-referential thinking — mind-wandering, rumination, narrative self-reflection, and the construction of the sense of a continuous self across time. Overactive DMN function is associated with depression, anxiety, and rigid thought patterns. Classical psychedelics reliably reduce DMN activity, which is associated with loosening of habitual mental patterns and the opening of new perspectives.

**Dieta**
The traditional period of dietary and behavioral restriction observed before and sometimes after plant medicine ceremony. Varies by tradition. Core elements typically include avoiding pork, alcohol, fermented foods, sex, salt, and sugar. Some restrictions have clear pharmacological rationale (tyramine-rich foods in the context of *MAOI* activity); others are rooted in traditional practice and carry ceremonial rather than pharmacological meaning. See also: *Tyramine.*

**Discontinuation Syndrome**
A cluster of symptoms that can occur when certain medications — particularly *SSRIs* — are stopped abruptly or tapered too quickly. Symptoms include dizziness, electric shock sensations ("brain zaps"), irritability, severe mood instability, nausea, and in some cases worsening depression. Not a sign of addiction in the conventional sense; a physiological response to sudden changes in serotonin availability. Requires medical supervision to manage safely.

**DMT (N,N-Dimethyltryptamine)**
The primary psychoactive compound in ayahuasca, contributed by the admixture plant (*Psychotria viridis* or *Diplopterys cabrerana*). A potent *5-HT2A agonist*. Orally inactive on its own due to rapid breakdown by gut *MAO*. Becomes orally bioavailable when combined with an *MAOI*. Also found endogenously in trace amounts in the human body; present in the smoke used in "changa" and vaporized DMT preparations.

**Downregulation**
The brain's adaptive response to chronic overstimulation of a receptor. When a receptor is activated too consistently — for example, by long-term *SSRI* use raising serotonin levels — the brain reduces the number of available receptors and lowers their sensitivity. The clinical consequence for psychedelic work: people on long-term antidepressants often find their psychedelic experience significantly blunted or absent, because the *5-HT2A* receptors the medicine needs to activate have been systematically reduced. Contrast with *Upregulation.*

**Drug**
In strict pharmacological usage, a neutral term for any substance that produces a biological effect. In common usage, the word carries connotations that range from "prescription medication" to "illicit substance" depending on context — a range wide enough to obscure more than it clarifies. This book uses "drug" in its precise pharmacological sense when discussing mechanisms and interactions (as in "drug-drug interaction" or "drug class"), and avoids it as a primary descriptor for plant medicines, where the cultural baggage of the word works against rather than toward understanding. See also: *Medicine, Pharmaceutical.*

**Entheogen**
Literally "generating the divine within." A term coined in 1979 by scholars including Gordon Wasson and Carl Ruck to describe psychoactive substances used in sacred or spiritual contexts. Preferred by some practitioners and researchers over "psychedelic" for its cultural and intentional specificity. Used interchangeably with "psychedelic" in many contemporary contexts.

**Harmine**
A *beta-carboline* alkaloid and the primary *MAOI* compound in *Banisteriopsis caapi*. A reversible inhibitor of *MAO-A*. Half-life of approximately two hours. The MAOI activity of harmine is what makes oral *DMT* bioavailable in ayahuasca preparations. Pharmacologically similar to pharmaceutical *RIMAs* (reversible inhibitors of MAO-A).

**Harmaline**
A *beta-carboline* alkaloid found in *Banisteriopsis caapi* alongside *harmine*. Also a reversible *MAO-A* inhibitor, though more potent than harmine at equivalent doses and associated with more pronounced sedative and visionary effects. Present in lower concentrations than harmine in most vine preparations.

**Facilitator**
The person who serves the medicine and holds the *container* during ceremony. Responsible for the physical and emotional safety of all participants, for creating and maintaining the held space, and for responding if something goes wrong. The role carries no single credential or lineage — facilitators may come from indigenous traditions with decades of apprenticeship, from contemporary therapeutic training, or from some combination of the two. What the role requires in every case is the capacity to stay present, to manage what arises, and to know the limits of their own competence. Used throughout this book in its broadest sense: the person in the room who is responsible. See also: *Curandero / Ayahuascero, Container, Guide.*

**Guide**
A facilitator's assistant in ceremony — a trusted person who supports the work without holding primary responsibility for the *container*. Guides help manage the room, sit with individual participants when needed, meet emergency responders if called, and free the facilitator to remain present with the group as a whole. Not the same role as the facilitator, and the distinction matters when it counts: the person who meets the ambulance outside, the person who stays with the group, the person who holds a participant through a difficult passage while the facilitator attends to something else — these are guide roles. See also: *Facilitator, Container.*

**Half-life**
The time it takes for the concentration of a substance in the body to reduce by half. Relevant for calculating *washout periods* before ceremony. A drug with a long half-life (e.g., fluoxetine, with a half-life of one to six days for its active metabolite) requires a much longer washout than one with a short half-life.

**Huachuma**
The traditional Andean name for the San Pedro cactus (*Trichocereus pachanoi*), which contains *mescaline* along with other alkaloids. Used ceremonially in Andean traditions, often referred to as a masculine medicine in contrast to the feminine attribution of ayahuasca. See also: *San Pedro, Mescaline.*

**Integration**
The process by which insights, emotions, and experiences from a psychedelic session are processed, understood, and woven into daily life. Not incidental to the therapeutic effect — the period following a session may be when much of the lasting change occurs, particularly given the temporary reopening of *critical periods* that psychedelics appear to facilitate. Integration is a phase, not an event, and may extend weeks to months following a session.

**Lithium**
A mood stabilizer prescribed primarily for bipolar disorder. Hard contraindication with all classical psychedelics. A Johns Hopkins analysis found that nearly half of reported combinations of lithium with classical psychedelics involved seizures. The mechanism is believed to involve convergence of two independently excitatory effects on cortical activity. Requires washout before any psychedelic work; the timing should be managed by the prescribing physician.

**MAOI (Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitor)**
A substance — pharmaceutical or natural — that inhibits the monoamine oxidase enzyme, slowing the breakdown of serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. In the context of ayahuasca, the *MAOI* component (*harmine*, *harmaline*) is not an incidental drug interaction — it is the mechanism that makes the preparation work by preventing gut breakdown of *DMT*. Pharmaceutical MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine) are irreversible; natural MAOIs from the vine are reversible. Both require dietary restrictions (*tyramine*) and carry interaction risks with serotonergic medications. See also: *RIMA, Tyramine.*

**MAO-A (Monoamine Oxidase A)**
The specific subtype of the monoamine oxidase enzyme primarily responsible for breaking down serotonin, *DMT*, and related compounds in the gut and CNS. The target of the *beta-carboline* alkaloids in ayahuasca. Also participates in *psilocin* clearance, which is why MAO inhibitors can prolong and intensify psilocybin experiences.

**Medicine**
The term used throughout this book to refer to plant-based psychedelic preparations: psilocybin-containing mushrooms, ayahuasca, mescaline in its various forms, and related compounds used in ceremonial and healing contexts. The choice is intentional and considered. These substances have served as healing tools for centuries, within organized ceremonial traditions, carried by practitioners with deep lineages of knowledge. Calling them "medicine" is not a claim that they are without risk or appropriate for everyone — this book is largely about exactly where those risks live. It is an acknowledgment of their therapeutic intention, their cultural context, and their demonstrated capacity for healing when approached responsibly. Where pharmacological precision requires specificity, the compound name or the term "substance" is used instead. See also: *Drug, Pharmaceutical.*

**Mescaline**
The primary psychoactive alkaloid in peyote (*Lophophora williamsii*) and San Pedro/Huachuma (*Trichocereus pachanoi*). A *phenethylamine* and *5-HT2A agonist*. Does not inhibit *SERT* (unlike psilocin). Also has activity at *5-HT1A* receptors and adrenergic α2A receptors. Key clinical differentiator from psilocybin: duration averages 11 hours in controlled trials, compared to 5 hours for psilocybin. No dietary restrictions of the kind required by *MAOI*-containing preparations. Plant sources (peyote, San Pedro) contain dozens of additional alkaloids with incompletely characterized effects.

**Neuroplasticity**
The brain's capacity to change its structure and function in response to experience. Highest during developmental *critical periods*; present but lower in adult brains. Psychedelics appear to temporarily increase neuroplasticity, a finding demonstrated by Gül Dölen's research on critical period reopening and supported by evidence of increased *BDNF* (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) following psychedelic sessions.

**Peyote (Lophophora williamsii)**
A small, spineless cactus native to Mexico and the southwestern United States, used ceremonially by indigenous peoples for centuries. Contains *mescaline* as its primary psychoactive compound, along with dozens of other alkaloids with incompletely characterized effects. The full alkaloid profile of peyote is pharmacologically distinct from isolated mescaline, making interaction data based on mescaline alone incomplete when applied to peyote ceremony.

**Pharmaceutical**
A medication produced through regulated industrial synthesis or extraction, dispensed through a licensed pharmacy, and prescribed or recommended by a licensed clinician. Defined by regulatory category and delivery system rather than by therapeutic intent. Pharmaceuticals and plant medicines frequently act on the same neurotransmitter systems — which is the entire reason Part 4 exists. The word carries no pejorative meaning in this book. Pharmaceuticals extend and save lives, manage conditions that plant medicines cannot touch, and represent the system of care that most of the people seeking this work are already embedded in. The challenge this book addresses is not that pharmaceuticals are bad. It is that the two worlds have not yet learned to speak to each other. See also: *Drug, Medicine.*

**Pharmacodynamics**
What a drug does to the body — its mechanism of action at the receptor or molecular level. The serotonin syndrome risk with psychedelics is a pharmacodynamic interaction: two compounds acting on the same receptor system simultaneously. Contrast with *Pharmacokinetics.*

**Pharmacokinetics**
What the body does to a drug — how it is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and eliminated. The *half-life* and *washout period* calculations used throughout Part 4 are pharmacokinetic considerations. Psilocin is metabolized primarily by *MAO-A* and glucuronidation enzymes, not the *CYP450* system — a pharmacokinetic fact that limits certain drug interactions that would otherwise be expected. Contrast with *Pharmacodynamics.*

**Phenethylamine**
A structural class of compounds that includes dopamine, norepinephrine, and *mescaline*, as well as the amphetamines. The shared backbone does not imply shared pharmacological effects. Mescaline does not cause monoamine release the way amphetamines do; its psychedelic effects are *5-HT2A*-mediated. The structural classification is real; the clinical equivalence is not.

**Psilocin**
The active metabolite of *psilocybin*. Psilocybin is a prodrug; phosphatase enzymes convert it to psilocin in the gut and liver. Psilocin is the compound that crosses the *blood-brain barrier* and activates *5-HT2A* receptors. Also weakly inhibits *SERT* (the serotonin transporter), adding a mild reuptake-inhibiting effect on top of the receptor agonism.

**Psilocybin**
A naturally occurring compound found in over 200 species of fungi, commonly called "magic mushrooms." A *prodrug* — inactive until converted to *psilocin* by the body. The most clinically studied classical psychedelic; FDA Breakthrough Therapy designation for treatment-resistant depression. Duration 4–6 hours. Relatively focused pharmacological profile — primarily *5-HT2A* agonism — compared to the three-mechanism serotonergic activity of *ayahuasca*.

**Psychosis**
A mental state characterized by a loss of contact with shared reality, typically involving hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thinking. Can be temporary (a "psychotic break") or chronic (as in *schizophrenia*). A contraindication for psychedelic work because psychedelics work by loosening the ordinary boundaries of perception — a mechanism that is therapeutic when the underlying tether to reality is strong, and dangerous when it is already compromised.

**Psychotomimetic**
Literally "psychosis-mimicking." The term used for psychedelic compounds before Humphry Osmond coined "psychedelic" in 1957. Psychiatrists in the 1950s used mescaline and LSD to experience states they believed would give them insight into their schizophrenic patients' experience. The term accurately captures the phenomenological overlap between psychedelic experience and psychotic experience — which is also the pharmacological rationale for the *schizophrenia* contraindication.

**RIMA (Reversible Inhibitor of MAO-A)**
A subtype of *MAOI* that inhibits the *MAO-A* enzyme reversibly — the inhibition wears off as the compound clears the body, rather than persisting until new enzyme is synthesized. *Harmine* and *harmaline* are RIMAs; this is what makes their dietary interaction window hours rather than weeks. Pharmaceutical RIMAs include moclobemide. Contrast with irreversible MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine), whose effects last two or more weeks.

**San Pedro (Trichocereus pachanoi)**
A columnar cactus native to the Andean mountains, containing *mescaline* and other alkaloids. Used ceremonially in South American traditions, often called *Huachuma* in traditional Andean contexts. Tends to produce less nausea than peyote, likely reflecting a different alkaloid profile. The additional alkaloids beyond mescaline have incompletely characterized pharmacological effects.

**Schizophrenia**
A chronic psychiatric condition characterized by hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking, and significant impairment in functioning. A contraindication for psychedelic work. The overlap between schizophrenic experience and psychedelic experience is pharmacologically real — both involve alterations in *5-HT2A* signaling and related systems. Psychedelics carry documented risk of triggering or worsening psychotic episodes in this population. Family history of schizophrenia is also a risk factor warranting caution.

**SERT (Serotonin Transporter)**
The protein responsible for removing serotonin from the synaptic gap and returning it to the neuron that released it. *SSRIs* work by blocking SERT, raising synaptic serotonin levels. *Psilocin* weakly inhibits SERT in addition to its *5-HT2A* agonism. *Mescaline* does not inhibit SERT. *Tetrahydroharmine* (THH) weakly inhibits SERT, contributing a third serotonergic mechanism to *ayahuasca's* pharmacological profile.

**Serotonin Syndrome**
A potentially serious condition caused by excessive serotonergic activity, typically from the combination of multiple serotonin-affecting substances. Symptoms range from mild (agitation, tremor, diarrhea) to severe (hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, seizures). Risk is highest with ayahuasca combined with serotonergic antidepressants, given the multiple converging serotonergic mechanisms. Risk with psilocybin and SSRIs is real but lower than pharmacology alone would suggest. Not the same as *neuroleptic malignant syndrome*, which involves dopamine rather than serotonin.

**Set and Setting**
A concept introduced by Timothy Leary referring to the mindset (set) and physical/social environment (setting) in which a psychedelic experience occurs. Now widely accepted in both research and ceremonial contexts as one of the most important determinants of psychedelic experience quality and outcomes. A well-prepared set (intention, psychological readiness) and a supportive setting (safe physical space, trusted guides) are foundational to beneficial outcomes.

**SSRI (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitor)**
A class of antidepressants that block *SERT*, raising synaptic serotonin levels. The most commonly prescribed antidepressants globally. Two interaction concerns with psychedelics: chronic use causes *downregulation* of *5-HT2A* receptors, blunting or eliminating the psychedelic experience; and the combination with *MAOIs* (as in ayahuasca) carries meaningful serotonin syndrome risk. Discontinuation requires medical supervision — see *Discontinuation Syndrome.*

**Synaptic Cleft**
The narrow gap between two neurons where chemical signals are transmitted. The space where *neurotransmitters* like serotonin are released, bind to receptors on the receiving neuron, and are subsequently cleared — either by *SERT* (reuptake) or by *MAO* (breakdown). The pharmacological action of most psychiatric medications and many psychoactive substances occurs in or around the synaptic cleft.

**Tetrahydroharmine (THH)**
A *beta-carboline* alkaloid found in *Banisteriopsis caapi*. Distinct from *harmine* and *harmaline* in that it functions primarily as a weak *SERT* inhibitor rather than a *MAO-A* inhibitor. This gives ayahuasca a third serotonergic mechanism — on top of MAO inhibition and *DMT's 5-HT2A* agonism — making it pharmacologically more complex than the other classical psychedelics. Half-life approximately 11 hours, longer than harmine.

**Tramadol**
A pain medication commonly classified as a mild opioid, but pharmacologically more complex. Also inhibits *SERT* and has weak *MAOI* properties. This places it in the serotonergic antidepressant interaction category for purposes of psychedelic screening — a fact not recognized by most participants or facilitators who think of it only as a pain medication. Documented serotonin syndrome cases with MAOIs in the medical literature.

**Tryptamine**
A structural class of compounds that includes *serotonin*, *DMT*, *psilocin*, and *LSD*. The classical psychedelics derived from tryptamine structures (psilocybin, DMT, LSD) contrast with the *phenethylamine*-derived mescaline. Both classes produce their psychedelic effects primarily through *5-HT2A agonism* despite their structural differences.

**Tyramine**
An amino acid derivative found in fermented, aged, and protein-rich foods. Normally metabolized by *MAO-A* in the gut before reaching systemic circulation. When MAO-A is inhibited (as by ayahuasca's *harmine* and *harmaline*), tyramine bypasses gut metabolism, enters the bloodstream, and triggers norepinephrine release — potentially causing a hypertensive crisis. The pharmacological basis for the traditional *dieta* restriction of aged cheeses, cured meats, fermented soy, and similar foods. Not relevant to psilocybin or mescaline, which do not involve MAOI mechanisms.

**Upregulation**
The brain's adaptive response to chronic understimulation of a receptor. When a receptor is chronically blocked or receives too little signal, the brain increases receptor density and sensitivity in an attempt to capture available signal. The opposite of *downregulation.* May occur during *SSRI* discontinuation as the brain adapts to reduced serotonin availability.

**Washout Period**
The time required after stopping a medication before the body has cleared it sufficiently to reduce interaction risk. Calculated based on *half-life* — typically five half-lives for most drugs to reach negligible levels. For SSRIs: two weeks for most; five weeks for fluoxetine (Prozac) due to its long-acting active metabolite. For irreversible *MAOIs*: minimum two weeks, often three recommended. Tapering under physician supervision precedes washout for most psychiatric medications.
