# Cooking the Medicine

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

I played the trumpet in high school. Poorly, and largely under duress.

I could read music. I understood time signatures and key changes. I knew where my fingers were supposed to go and, most of the time, I could get them there. By any technical measure, I was a competent music reader. What I was not, and what I understood with complete clarity the first time I found myself sitting in a jazz ensemble, was a musician.

Everyone around me was improvising. They were in a conversation with each other, responding in real time to what the room was doing, to what the music itself seemed to want to become. I was following a script. And when the script ran out, I had nothing. I could reproduce what was written on the page. I had no idea what the music was actually saying.

I think about that moment a lot when people ask me about cooking medicine.

## The Cookbook Problem

There is a version of ayahuasca preparation that looks, from the outside, like a recipe. You take this vine and these leaves. You add water. You cook it down through several wash cycles until what remains is dark, viscous, and concentrated. The instructions are not complicated. They can be written out. They have been written out, in varying forms, in books and on the internet and in the fieldnotes of anthropologists going back decades.

Following those instructions will produce a liquid that contains the relevant compounds.

It will not produce medicine.

The difference between the two is the same difference that separates me, the trumpet player faithfully executing the sheet music, from the musicians on either side of me who were playing the same song I was playing and doing something I couldn't come close to.

I can become fluent in a language without understanding its essence. I can memorize vocabulary, nail the grammar, produce sentences that are technically correct and entirely dead. Fluency is not comprehension. Following a recipe is not cooking. Reproducing the notes is not music.

A chef does not understand ingredients the way a recipe writer does. A chef understands them the way you understand a person you have known for years: their personality, their tendencies, who they play well with, who brings out something unexpected in them, when to push and when to leave them alone. A chef walking into an unfamiliar kitchen can open the refrigerator and produce something remarkable not because they have memorized more recipes but because they actually know the ingredients.

That is the relationship I am trying to describe. And in the context of cooking medicine, it is not a metaphor. It is a prerequisite.

## Two Plants, One Conversation

At its foundation, ayahuasca is a brew made from two plants. Banisteriopsis caapi, the vine called the Mother Vine or Abuela. And Psychotria viridis, known as Chacruna.

The vine contains beta-carboline alkaloids that act as MAO inhibitors. The leaf contains DMT, a powerful psychedelic that is ordinarily broken down in the gut before it can reach the brain. Together they solve each other's problem. The MAO inhibitors allow the DMT to survive digestion and cross into the bloodstream. The DMT activates through the vine's chemistry. Neither plant does what it does without the other.

That much is biology. It can be written in a textbook.

What cannot be written in a textbook is the conversation.

I have spent years with these plants. Years of harvest, cooking, sitting in ceremony, listening. I know Abuela the way I know old friends: her moods, her insistence, the particular quality of her communication on a dry morning versus after heavy rain. I know which sections of vine are ready and which need more time, not from a measurement but from attention. She shows me. This is not a figure of speech. It is a description of a working relationship that took years to develop and that I could not have shortcut.

The Chacruna is a family. Different cultivars, scattered across the jungle, each with its own emphasis, its own energy. Some are expansive and luminous. Others quieter, more introspective. I have come to know them individually, the way you come to know the members of any family you have been close to long enough. Each one recognized at a glance. Each one bringing something particular to what is being assembled.

## The Vine I Harvest Is Never the Same Vine Twice

This is the thing that most people do not understand about ayahuasca, and it matters enormously.

The vine responds to its environment, continuously and specifically. The season, the rainfall, how long this stretch of jungle has been undisturbed, what this particular growing cycle has been like. A vine harvested during a dry stretch carries something different than vine harvested after weeks of heavy rain. The leaves I gather this morning carry something of this morning in them. The plants are not static repositories of fixed compounds. They are living organisms, always changing, always becoming slightly different versions of themselves.

Which means every batch of medicine begins with materials that have never been assembled in exactly this combination before. And never will be again.

This variability is not a flaw in the process. It is the nature of the process. The jazz ensemble does not play the same song the same way twice. That is not a quality control problem. That is what makes it alive.

## What Else Goes into the Pot

Here is the part that people unfamiliar with plant medicine traditions find hardest to accept.

The cook goes in too.

The maestro's psychological state, their energetic condition, the quality of their presence throughout the days and nights of this work: these are not peripheral influences on the medicine. They are ingredients. In every tradition that has worked with this medicine across thousands of years, this is understood without debate. The emerging science of set, setting, and the interpersonal transmission of psychological states is beginning to give this ancient knowledge a language that Western medicine can recognize.

A cook who brings unresolved anger into the jungle produces medicine that carries it. A cook who is distracted, compromised, going through the motions: the medicine reflects that. This is not metaphor. It is something you can taste.

I approach the cooking with the understanding that what I produce will be drunk by people in one of the most open, vulnerable states a human being can enter. That understanding shapes everything about how I prepare. There is a dieta: a period of dietary and behavioral restriction that precedes the cooking, not only for the participants preparing to drink, but for me. The dieta is not ritual for its own sake. It is how I come to the work clean.

When I am cooking, I am singing. The icaros, the sacred medicine songs of the curandero tradition, are learned directly from the plants in deep ceremony. They are not decoration. They are part of what the medicine becomes. When I sing to the pot over the hours it reduces, I am not performing. I am participating in the creation of something I will later serve. What I bring to that participation matters.

I think of the parallel physician. We have data, good data, showing that the quality of a doctor's presence, their genuine attentiveness, their care for the specific person in front of them, affects outcomes in ways the mechanistic model of medicine consistently underestimates. We know this. We have just been slow to take it seriously.

In the jungle, this is not a debate. It is simply how it works.

## The Process

The cooking is labor. One to five days, depending on what the medicine needs.

The vine is shredded by hand. Each Chacruna leaf is washed individually. The plant material is packed into the vessel in alternating layers, vine then leaf then vine then leaf, covered with water and set over fire. Multiple wash cycles follow, each one drawing something from the plants that the previous wash left behind. The liquids combine and reduce over a second fire, slowly concentrated over hours until the broth becomes something else entirely. The Shipibo-Conibo call it Pae. Not ayahuasca. Not a brew. A living presence with its own intelligence and its own personality.

Additional plants may join the conversation. Bobinsana, known in the Amazon as Sirenita, is a master plant of the heart. She carries teachings on connection, on empathy, on the capacity to feel without being overwhelmed. Mapacho, the sacred tobacco, is grounding and protective: a masculine energy that provides containment. The decision about what to include is not made from a recipe. It is made in relationship, guided by what I sense the medicine needs to be for the people who will drink it.

There is no timer. There is no formula. There is only attention, and time, and fire.

## What You Are Actually Drinking

The medicine you drink is a once-occurring event.

The specific vine harvested at this point in the season, after this particular stretch of weather, from this section of jungle. The leaves gathered on this morning. The quality of presence I brought to the cooking. The songs I sang over the pot during the hours it reduced. The other plants I chose to include, and why. Every variable is alive. Every variable changes. The convergence of forces that produced this batch will never happen again in exactly this way.

And the person drinking it is also a once-occurring event. You have never been precisely who you are today before. The questions, griefs, hopes, and histories you carry into the ceremony are as unique as the medicine you are about to receive.

This is why the experience cannot be scripted. It can only be entered.

The musician across from me in that jazz ensemble was not reading music. He was listening: to the room, to the other musicians, to what the song wanted to do next. He knew the language well enough to stop following it and start speaking it.

Two unique things, meeting for the first time.

That is what is in the bottle.
