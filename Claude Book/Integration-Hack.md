# Dr. Evans' Integration Hack

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

A day is the sum of countless decisions. The vast majority of those decisions occur without your input.

You are an active participant in very few of the choices that shape your experience. You are rarely part of the conversation, much less driving the bus. And from your brain's perspective, this is not a problem. It is the whole point.

Active decision-making is metabolically expensive. It requires bandwidth pulled from other tasks, consumes resources the brain would rather allocate elsewhere, and introduces something the survival optimizer finds genuinely threatening: the unknown. A known solution to a familiar problem, even an imperfect one, is almost always preferable to an uncertain solution to a precisely defined one. The brain does not optimize for best. It optimizes for reliable.

So it bundles decisions.

Your first commute to a new job requires continuous, effortful navigation. Every turn, every timing decision, every contingency: active processing. But with each repetition, the decisions get refined and consolidated until the whole suite has been certified as optimized. At that point it gets packaged, labeled, and filed. The brain does not want your input on the morning commute anymore. You had your chance. It has this.

I know this mechanism intimately. After my first thirty-six hour overnight call as an intern in Boston, I got completely lost trying to ride the T home. I had not yet developed the skill of deciphering a Boston accent delivered through failing speakers over the sound of trains and people who had places to be. It did not take many repetitions before I could navigate home without ever being fully conscious of doing it. The T had been packaged and filed.

Most people spend most of their lives in this mode. Same morning routine. Same commute. Same people at work. Same conversations with those people. Before you make fun of the sheeple, recognize what you are actually looking at: a walking advertisement for what success looks like from an efficiency standpoint. The system is doing exactly what it was built to do.

## The Window

A well-supported psychedelic experience disrupts this. Substantially.

After ceremony, the packages are open. The bundled decisions that run your days are temporarily up for review in a way they are not under ordinary conditions. Every habitual response, every automated reaction, every default setting you have been running without examination: suddenly available for revision. This is not incidental to what the medicine does. It is the mechanism. It is why these experiences can produce changes that years of talk therapy could not reach.

Your brain knows this. And it does not like it.

There will be an immensely powerful pull back toward the familiar. Toward the known solutions, the optimized packages, the comfortable automated patterns. This pull is not weakness. It is the system doing what systems do when destabilized: seeking the most recent stable state. It is efficient. It is reliable. And if you do not actively work against it, it will win. The default state will reassert itself, the window will close, and the experience will become a memory rather than a transformation.

The window is finite. What you do inside it determines what the experience actually meant.

## The Hack

You cannot make different decisions if you do not show up to make decisions.

This is the integration hack, and it is both simpler and more demanding than most people expect: force yourself to make decisions. Deliberately. Continuously. Not because the specific decisions matter, but because the act of deciding keeps you present and keeps the autopilot from reclaiming the wheel.

What time will I wake up tomorrow? Shower before coffee or after? Which hand brushes the teeth? Same route to work or a different one? Same lunch, or something else? Same response to that familiar irritation, or a different one?

The content of these decisions is almost beside the point. The point is that you are actively engaged in shaping your experience rather than passively watching it happen to you. Every micro-decision you make is a moment you have not handed back to the system. And in those moments, real choices become available: a different relationship with the morning. A different quality of attention at work. A different way to navigate the relationships that usually navigate you.

Maybe you choose to start the day with five minutes of genuine gratitude rather than immediately surrendering to whatever the algorithm on your phone has decided you should think about first. That is not a small choice. That is the whole choice, made small enough to actually make.

This window will close. It closes for everyone. The brain is patient and it is very good at what it does, and eventually the packages get re-filed. But the packages that get re-filed do not have to be identical to the ones that got opened. The window is not an invitation to rebuild your entire life. It is an invitation to make different decisions long enough for different decisions to start feeling like options.

Show up to make the decisions.

Decide to decide.

Everything else follows from that.

What you discover, if you stay awake long enough, is that the choice was always there.
