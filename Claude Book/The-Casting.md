# The Casting

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

Every person in your life is carrying a version of you.

Not you. A character they have assembled from the accumulated history of every interaction you have had with them, weighted toward the most memorable moments and the most recent ones, filtered through their own fears and needs and unresolved business. That character lives in their story, plays a role in their narrative, and behaves in ways that are consistent with what the story requires.

Most of the time you have no idea this is happening. You walk into a room and the other person is already in a scene with a version of you that you did not agree to play, and the pull to simply become that character, to take your assigned place in their story, is stronger than most people realize. It is easier. It resolves the tension. It confirms what they expected and gets everyone back to familiar ground.

This is how old stories survive long past their expiration date. Not because they are accurate. Because both parties keep showing up to perform them.

## The Wife

Here is the daily version of this.

On the days when my wife is not carrying the best version of me, when she has assembled her picture of me from the last difficult interaction, or the accumulated weight of a hard week, and she begins to interpret my actions through that lens, she is casting me. Giving me a role. Handing me a script.

And I feel it. The pull to either defend myself against the character she is describing, which means accepting that the character is real enough to be worth defending against, or to simply become him because arguing takes energy and the path of least resistance is always available.

I don't want to play that character.

Not because my wife is doing something wrong. She is doing exactly what every human nervous system does: running its most recent stable model of the people it is in relationship with, because that is efficient and familiar and feels like accuracy even when it isn't. She is not trying to miscast me. She is working with the data she has, filtered through the mood she is in, and producing a version of events that makes sense to her.

But the character she is casting me in is not me. And I have a choice about whether to perform him.

The third option, the one that is easy to miss because the other two are so loud, is to simply not accept the role. To show up as myself rather than as her current model of me. To respond from where I actually am rather than from where her story has placed me. This does not require a confrontation. It does not require announcing that her version is wrong. It only requires declining, quietly and consistently, to become the character she is expecting.

What happens then is the same thing that happened with my sister. The old story loses its co-author. A story written for two people cannot sustain itself when one of them stops performing their part. The script still exists, but it has no one to speak the lines, and eventually it goes quiet.

## The Two Moves

The work that these medicines make possible is, at its core, about authorship.

The first move is the one my sister story illustrates: you rewrite your own character. You stop performing the role that your history, your wounds, your accumulated defenses have been playing, and you show up as something closer to who you actually are. This disrupts every story that was written around the old character. People who were in relationship with that version of you will feel the change. Some will update their story. Some will not. That is their work to do.

The second move is this one: you recognize when someone else is doing the casting, and you decline the role. Not with drama. Not with a speech about how they are seeing you wrong. Simply by being a different person than the one they expected, consistently enough that their old script stops fitting the scenes.

Both moves require the same underlying capacity: the ability to see the story while you are inside it. To notice that a casting is happening before you have already taken the stage. This is the thing that ordinary consciousness makes almost impossible and that the medicine, for a window of time, makes available. You cannot unsee what you have seen in ceremony. You cannot unknow that the stories were stories. And once you know that, you have a choice about how to participate in them.

We are always being cast in each other's stories.

The work is learning you can put the script down.
