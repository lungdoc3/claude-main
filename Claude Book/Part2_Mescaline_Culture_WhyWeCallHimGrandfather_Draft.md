Grandmother Ayahuasca earns her name through a kind of standing that gets built across generations before you ever arrive in the room, an elder's love decided long before you showed up and held steady no matter what you do once you're there. Grandfather Huachuma earns his a different way, and the difference is written into how the plant itself actually grows.

San Pedro spends almost all of its energy on one project, going up. Most of the cactus's body is just an ever-extending green column, photosynthetic surface stacked on photosynthetic surface, climbing toward whatever light it can reach. Eventually an arm gets too tall for its own structure, catches the wind, and breaks under its own weight. It falls. The grounded end takes root where it lands and starts growing new pups from the wreckage. Watch an Andean hillside over years instead of minutes and what you'd actually see is a slow, repeating cycle of building and toppling, towers going up, towers coming down, new towers rising from the rubble of the last ones.

Ayahuasca climbs a tower that's already standing, the canopy, the existing architecture of the jungle around her. Huachuma builds his own tower from nothing and lets it fall and builds again. That difference in the plant is the same difference you feel in the medicine. Where grandmother can take you out past the edge of ordinary reality entirely, the phenethylamine in this cactus works differently, pro-social, body-aware, integrative rather than dissolving. It does its real work close to the ground, reconnecting a person to their own body and their own environment rather than removing them from both. Grandfather doesn't usually take you out of the world. He keeps you standing inside it while something in you gets torn down and rebuilt sturdier than it was. Falling, for him, is just what it costs to grow the next ten feet.

Even his mutations carry that same blunt physicality. Every so often a Trichocereus throws off a monstrose variant, and it comes in two distinct forms. The masculine version loses its spines and ribs almost entirely and puts everything into height and raw energy production. The feminine version does the opposite, gives up height, spreads low and wide, and swells outward with extra areoles that double as both armor and additional sites for flowering and pupping. Even when the plant breaks its own rules, it breaks them in a shape you'd recognize.

The plant has its own case to make for why people keep coming back to it, separate from anything mescaline does to the nervous system. The spines on the ceremonial varieties sit far enough apart to let a hand close around the trunk comfortably, a spacing usually only seen in cacti that have already been shaped by generations of human handling. Cuttings taken from it rarely cause it harm and often help it spread, the opposite of what taking a piece of most plants does to them. Its flowers open at night in clusters a foot across, fragrant enough to be impossible to miss and beautiful enough to draw bees and hummingbirds across long distances to pollinate them. A plant built this thoroughly for cross-species cooperation has been inviting a relationship with us for a long time, well before anyone wrote a name for it.

That name, Wachuma, is Quechua, and in that language it just means cactus, used for roughly eight of the more than forty Trichocereus varieties native to the Andes. Quechua-speaking peoples across Peru, Ecuador, and Bolivia have gathered to drink it for at least eight and a half thousand years, dated from preserved cactus skin found in a cave in northern Peru. *[Randy: I have the cave name as Cuaterero in the source material, please confirm the correct site name before this goes to print, I want to get this right.]* Ancient temple complexes built around this sacrament once drew thousands of travelers from across South and Central America for ceremony, prayer, and ecstatic dance.

The name San Pedro is newer, and the story behind it is one of my favorites in this entire tradition. During the period when Spanish authorities were actively suppressing indigenous plant sacraments, one community invited Spanish missionaries to sit in ceremony with them, hoping to show that the cactus was a genuine gift from God rather than the devil's work the church assumed it to be. The missionaries drank, and by their own account, agreed. They named it after Saint Peter, the apostle who holds the keys to the gates of heaven, because what they experienced in that ceremony felt like exactly that, a gate opening. It helps that the most common ceremonial variety carries six ribs, and when you slice it in cross section, what looks back at you is unmistakably a Star of David, the same star shape that turns into a sky of floating points the moment those slices hit boiling water.

Grandmother's authority comes from a lineage, a maestro, years of apprenticeship standing between a person and the medicine before they're trusted to serve it to anyone else. Grandfather's authority gets built differently, the same way his towers do. Most Wachumeros work from a mesa, a portable altar laid out on a blanket or cloth, covered in power objects that belong to that practitioner alone. No two mesas look alike, because no two practitioners built theirs the same way, and a ceremony centered on the mesa, a mesada, ends up just as singular as the altar driving it. The modern Wachumero reads that mesa the way a maestro reads a lineage, except the mesa was assembled by hand, piece by piece, by the person standing over it. Even his authority gets built the way everything else about him does, one tower at a time, by the one doing the building.
