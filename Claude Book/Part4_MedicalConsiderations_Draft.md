# Medical Considerations
## Part 4 — Clinical Safety Section

*[Note: Covers the primary medical conditions requiring risk stratification before psychedelic work. Organized around the principle that these are clinical conversations, not automatic disqualifications. First-pass draft in Randy's voice. Randy POV placeholder at end.]*

---

**Risk, Not Prohibition**

The conditions in this section share one important feature: none of them are automatic disqualifications. Each one raises the question of how much physiological reserve someone has, and how well their condition is currently managed. That is the clinical conversation worth having. Not "do you have this diagnosis" but "where are you with it right now, and who is helping you manage it."

All classical psychedelics produce a predictable, temporary increase in heart rate and blood pressure. In a healthy person with no underlying cardiovascular vulnerability, this is well-tolerated and self-limiting. It is the same physiological response the body mounts under excitement, exertion, or stress. The question for every condition below is not whether this response will occur. It will. The question is what it meets when it arrives.

---

**High Blood Pressure**

Hypertension is the most common cardiovascular condition in the western world, and it is the one most likely to appear on a pre-ceremony screening form without anyone having thought carefully about it. The concern is not subtle: elevated baseline blood pressure plus the temporary additional elevation that accompanies a psychedelic experience adds up. How much that matters depends entirely on how controlled the hypertension is going in.

Well-controlled hypertension, managed with appropriate medication and verified by recent blood pressure readings, is a manageable risk. The key word is verified. Not "I take my medication" but "I have checked my blood pressure recently and it is in range." A home blood pressure cuff is a reasonable investment for anyone with hypertension who is preparing for ceremony. Check it several times in the days before, at different times of day. A single reading in a clinical setting tells you less than a pattern. If readings are consistently elevated, that is a conversation to have with a physician before proceeding.

The medication management issue connects directly to the earlier discussion: stopping blood pressure medications before ceremony to clear the pharmacological slate is precisely the wrong approach. The rebound hypertension that follows abrupt discontinuation adds risk rather than removing it.

---

**Stroke and TIA**

A stroke or transient ischemic attack is, at its core, a vascular event. Something went wrong with blood flow to the brain. The cerebrovascular disease that produced that event does not disappear after recovery. The vessels involved are the same vessels that will experience the temporary blood pressure elevation of a psychedelic experience.

This is not a prohibition. People recover from strokes. Cerebrovascular risk can be managed. But it requires a physician who knows the specific history to make an informed judgment about whether the cardiovascular stress of a psychedelic experience is appropriate. A facilitator cannot make that call. A screening form cannot make that call. This is a conversation that requires the participant's medical provider.

Timing matters. A stroke or TIA in the recent past represents a different risk profile than one that occurred a decade ago in someone who has since achieved excellent risk factor control. The recency and the current management are both relevant.

---

**The Cardiologist Question**

Heart attack, arrhythmia, structural heart disease, heart failure, implanted devices. These conditions are different from each other in important ways, but they share a common screening approach. The most clinically useful question is not a diagnosis inventory. It is this: are you currently, or have you ever been, under the care of a cardiologist?

That question catches the person who had a stent placed and considers themselves fixed. It catches the person managing an arrhythmia who does not think of themselves as having heart disease. It catches the person whose heart failure is well-compensated and easy to minimize. The word "cardiologist" signals specialist-level care, which signals the condition was considered serious enough to require it. That is the relevant threshold.

For anyone who answers yes, the next conversation is with that cardiologist or a physician with access to their cardiac history. A well-revascularized MI several years out with preserved ejection fraction and controlled risk factors is a different clinical picture than a recent event with ongoing instability. An arrhythmia that is rate-controlled and anticoagulated is different from one that is intermittent and unmanaged. The cardiovascular stimulation of a psychedelic experience places a demand on the cardiac system, and someone who knows that system needs to weigh in on whether it can meet that demand.

---

**Insulin-Dependent Diabetes**

Insulin-dependent diabetes belongs on this list, but for a different reason than the cardiovascular conditions above. The concern is not primarily hemodynamic. It is metabolic.

Psychedelic experiences alter appetite, food intake, and the body's stress response, all of which influence blood sugar. Ceremony typically involves eating less than usual on the day of the experience. The physiological stress response that accompanies an intense psychedelic experience can affect glucose regulation unpredictably. For someone managing insulin dosing in a normal daily routine, ceremony day represents a meaningful departure from that routine.

This is manageable. It requires preparation, not avoidance. The insulin management plan for ceremony day is not something to work out on arrival. It requires a conversation with the prescribing physician before the retreat, accounting for the specific circumstances of that day. The facilitator also needs to know about the diabetes and be prepared to recognize and respond to hypoglycemia.

---

**Seizure History**

Psychedelics lower the seizure threshold. This is a documented pharmacological effect, not a theoretical concern, and it operates independently of whatever caused the original seizures. For someone with a history of seizures, the combination of a lower baseline seizure threshold and a medicine that lowers it further deserves explicit clinical attention before any ceremony.

This conversation belongs with a neurologist or the physician managing the seizure disorder. The relevant questions are whether the condition is currently controlled, what medications are managing it, and whether those medications carry their own interaction concerns. Lithium carries a specific and well-documented seizure risk in combination with classical psychedelics, covered in the pharmaceutical section. Certain anticonvulsant medications may also interact with the psychedelic experience in ways that are not fully characterized. The seizure history is the starting point. The full medication picture is the context.

A history of febrile seizures in childhood, with no recurrence and no ongoing medication, is a different clinical picture than active epilepsy managed with multiple anticonvulsants. The current status and current management are what matter most.
