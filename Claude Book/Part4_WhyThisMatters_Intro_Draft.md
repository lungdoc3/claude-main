**Two Cosmologies Walk Into a Ceremony**

Go back one generation of healers. The curanderos, the maestros, the ayahuasceros who trained the teachers who trained the facilitators who are working today — these were people who had spent their lives learning an extraordinarily sophisticated tradition of healing. Their knowledge of plant medicines, of ceremony, of the human psyche and spirit was deep and genuine and hard-won. It still is.

But their patients were not carrying medication lists.

The conditions we think of as the defining challenges of modern western life — hypertension, depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, treatment-resistant PTSD, the slow metabolic wreckage of processed food and sedentary work — these did not exist in the cosmology of traditional Amazonian healing, because they did not need to. Their patients did not have them, or did not have them in the way we do, or understood and addressed them through an entirely different framework. When indigenous teachers passed their knowledge forward, they taught everything they knew. They were not withholding a chapter on pharmaceutical interactions. That chapter simply did not exist. It did not need to.

Then Marge from Ohio packed her bags.

Marge has hypertension, anxiety, seasonal affective disorder, a toenail fungal infection she has been meaning to address, and a genuine spiritual hunger that conventional medicine has not touched. She has heard that ayahuasca changes people. She has done her research, found a ceremony, and arrived. She is sitting across from a healer who has spent decades becoming fluent in a tradition that was never designed to account for her lisinopril, her sertraline, or the four supplements on her nightstand.

Two very different cosmologies just walked into the same room. And neither of them fully knows it.

---

**The Gap Has Three Edges**

The gap in this picture is real, and it cuts in three directions.

The indigenous healer carries the deepest lineage and the least familiarity with the western pharmacological world. This is not a criticism. It is an accurate description of a knowledge system that was complete for the world it was built in, encountering a world it was not built for. Expecting an ayahuascero trained in the Shipibo tradition to evaluate a patient's MAOI washout timeline is not a reasonable expectation. It was never part of the transmission.

The non-indigenous facilitator — trained by indigenous healers, often with genuine depth of experience — typically has more. They know the language of western conditions. They understand in a general sense that high blood pressure means there is probably a medication, that depression means there is probably an antidepressant, that anxiety disorders get treated with a range of compounds. What they are usually missing is a working clinical understanding of what those medications actually do at the level of neurotransmitters and receptors — and therefore what it means to add a serotonin-active plant medicine on top of a serotonin-active pharmaceutical. We would not expect them to have that knowledge. It is not part of their training and it is not a failure. But the gap is real.

And then there is the participant — carrying genuine intention, carrying real need, and carrying pharmacological baggage they may not even recognize as baggage. No one told them their sleep supplement has MAO-inhibiting properties. No one told them their antidepressant will likely blunt the experience they traveled this far to have. Their prescribing physician, who does know about the medications, does not know about the ceremony. Their facilitator, who knows about the ceremony, does not fully know about the medications. The information that would close the loop exists — it just has not been in the same room at the same time.

---

**Preventable**

Here is what matters most, and I want to say it plainly: the vast majority of adverse outcomes from the intersection of plant medicines and western pharmacology are preventable.

Not rare. Not unusual. Preventable.

The information needed to prevent them is increasingly available. The pharmacological mechanisms are understood. The clinical interactions have been studied, at least in the foundational cases. The specific compounds that warrant hard contraindications are known. The categories of risk — the serotonin interactions, the MAOI dangers, the lithium-seizure story, the cardiovascular concerns — none of these are obscure. They are documented. They are in the literature. They are in this book.

That word — preventable — carries weight. It is not an accusation aimed at any tradition or any teacher. It is a statement of clinical and ethical reality. If harm can be prevented and the means to prevent it are available, then proceeding without taking those means is a choice. Every healer, indigenous or otherwise, operates under the oldest principle in medicine: first, do no harm. Harm that is preventable and not prevented is not an accident. It is a gap that someone chose not to close.

The gap is closable. That is the reason this section exists.

---

**What Follows**

The medical and pharmacological discussions that follow are not designed to create gatekeeping, to pathologize the people who seek this work, or to impose a western clinical framework onto traditions that predate it by centuries. They are designed to give every person in the room — the healer, the facilitator, the participant — enough information to make responsible decisions.

Some interactions are dangerous and warrant genuine caution. Some are manageable with timing and preparation. Some are uncertain enough that they require a physician familiar with this territory. And some carry more fear than the actual pharmacology justifies. Knowing the difference is the work.

Marge from Ohio deserves the full benefit of what these medicines offer. Getting her there safely is everyone's job.
