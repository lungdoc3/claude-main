# The Diagnosis Trap
## Part 1: The Framework

---

*[Note: Rewrite of the diagnosis trap section. Three moves: the meaning-making brain as a feature not a bug, the medical school effect as primary illustration, the identity surrender after diagnosis. Closes with the connection to psychedelic healing. Randy's voice. First-pass draft.]*

---

Your brain does not tolerate randomness well. It was not built to. Randomness is threatening — it means you do not know what is coming, which means you cannot prepare, which means you might not survive. So the brain does what it was designed to do. It assembles patterns. It converts ambiguous, fragmented experience into coherent narrative as fast as possible. It finds the category and files the experience into it. This is not a malfunction. It is the primary feature.

It is also the setup for the diagnosis trap.

---

**The Medical School Effect**

No physician makes it through medical school without diagnosing themselves with at least a handful of fatal diseases and a considerable number of severely disabling ones. This is not a joke. It is a documented phenomenon and every physician you have ever met has lived it.

Here is how it works. You are a second-year medical student. You open the textbook to hemiballismus — a neurological condition involving uncontrollable flinging movements of the limbs. You read four symptoms. You recognize three of them in yourself. Before the chapter is finished, you are fairly certain you have hemiballismus. By the end of the week, you have also contracted two tropical parasitic infections, a rare connective tissue disorder, and at least one thing that has not been named yet.

The meaning-making machine does not wait for complete information. It pattern-matches on partial data and produces a conclusion. The more you know about a disease, the more surface area you have to match against your own experience. Medical students are drowning in surface area.

Now here is the important part. This effect is strongest for disorders of the psyche. Because there is no objective measure. With hemiballismus, you either have the involuntary flinging movements or you do not. With depression, with anxiety, with trauma — there is no such threshold. The DSM criteria for any psychological diagnosis will describe experiences you have had, because they describe experiences that exist on a spectrum everyone sits somewhere on. It is only pathology when the frequency or intensity or impairment crosses a line. Whatever that line is. Nobody can tell you precisely where it falls. Population studies can give you distributions. They cannot tell you where you sit in the distribution.

So you read the criteria. You recognize yourself. The meaning-making machine does its work. And now you have a diagnosis.

---

**The Bus Driver**

Here is what changes when a physician confirms it.

The patient who leaves my office with a diagnosis leaves with more than a name. They leave with an identity, a set of expectations, a community of fellow sufferers, a literature, a prognosis, and — this is the part that matters most — a story about where the problem lives.

The problem lives in the diagnosis. The diagnosis is an entity, external to the self, that arrived and took up residence. It is something that happened to you. You are the patient. The diagnosis is the bus driver.

I watched this play out across thirty-five years of clinical medicine. "You know I can't do that, Dr. Evans. I have COPD." This from patients whose pulmonary function had normalized, whose capacity had returned, whose physiology was telling a completely different story than their self-concept was. The diagnosis had outlasted the disease. The story was running the body.

The relief this produces is real, and it is worth understanding rather than dismissing. A diagnosis means you are not making it up. It means your suffering has a name and therefore a reality that other people can acknowledge. It means you are not failing at being a person — you are a person with a condition. That is genuinely comforting when you have been struggling without explanation.

But the relief comes with a cost that most people do not see until later.

If the diagnosis is the cause, you are not the cause. And if you are not the cause, you cannot be part of the cure. The agency goes out with the ambiguity. You have traded the discomfort of not knowing for the comfort of a story that removes you from the equation.

---

**Inquiry Stops**

Once a diagnosis has been made, everything that follows gets filtered through it. Every new symptom is first tested against the diagnosis. Every change in experience is interpreted in its light. Confirmation bias is a feature of every human brain, including mine, and it operates below the level of conscious reasoning. The brain has found its category. It will protect that category.

This is where the trap fully closes. Not just because the story shapes how the patient understands their experience going forward, but because inquiry stops. The question has been answered. We know what this is. The curiosity that might have led somewhere useful — what is actually happening here, what drove this, what is this experience trying to communicate — that curiosity gets filed as unnecessary once the label is in place.

I have been asked more times than I can count how long someone had to live. My answer was always the same: I do not know, and any answer I give you will be wrong. What I can tell you is that if I put a hundred people with your exact situation in a room, fifty of them will be dead in five years. I cannot tell you which fifty. Population statistics describe distributions. They do not describe you. The diagnosis tells you which room you are in. It does not tell you where in the room you are standing, and it cannot.

Finding the broken part is not the same as solving the problem. The diagnosis is not the destination. In many cases it is the beginning of a detour.

---

**Why This Matters Here**

Psychedelic medicines do not treat diagnoses. They do not know what depression is, and they do not care. What they contact is the experience underneath the label — the actual texture of what someone is living in, stripped of the story that has been organized around it.

The person who arrives in ceremony as a depressed person, fully identified with that story, will have a different experience than the person who arrives curious about the patterns running underneath their suffering. The first person is asking the medicine to fix what the diagnosis named. The second is asking the medicine to illuminate what the diagnosis covered.

The distinction is everything.

Understanding the diagnosis trap is not an argument against clinical medicine or against the genuine utility of diagnostic frameworks. It is an argument for holding them loosely. The label is a map. The territory is always more complicated than the map, and always more open.
