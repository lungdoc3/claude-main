# The Trojan Horse

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

There is a version of this conversation that started sixty years ago and never finished.

In 1966, a researcher named Willis Harman and a psychologist named James Fadiman sat a group of professionals down in a room in Menlo Park, California. Engineers. Architects. Mathematicians. Each one had brought a real problem, something they had been stuck on for months, something that conventional thinking had not cracked. They were given a low dose of mescaline or LSD. They were asked to work.

The results were not what a skeptic would predict. Problems that had resisted sustained effort yielded. Some of the solutions were later patented. The researchers concluded, carefully, that these substances seemed to do something to the way the mind approached a problem. They loosened something. They opened a door that ordinarily stays shut.

That study never got a follow-up. The results were solid. The conversation got shut down.

By the end of the 1960s, psychedelics had become culturally radioactive. The research was real but it got buried under everything else that was happening: the politics, the panic, the association with a counterculture that the establishment had decided was a threat. In 1970, the Controlled Substances Act placed LSD and psilocybin in Schedule I, the category reserved for substances with no accepted medical use and high potential for abuse. The conversation did not slow down. It stopped. Abruptly. For decades.

What happened next is worth understanding, because it shapes everything about where we are now.

## The Only Play Available

When researchers began trying to restart the work in the 1990s, the front door was still locked. The stigma had not lifted. The Schedule I classification had not changed. So they found a back door.

The back door was this: if a substance could be shown to treat a specific, diagnosable, treatment-resistant condition (something that conventional medicine had genuinely failed), it could be studied. The research could be funded and published and taken seriously, because it was dressed in the language conventional medicine already spoke. It looked like a drug trial. It had a control group. It had endpoints. It fit the framework.

This was not a failure of imagination. It was a recognition of reality. The only way back into the conversation was to speak medicine's language. The Trojan horse was not a compromise. It was the only play available.

The study that mattered most came from Robin Carhart-Harris and his colleagues at Imperial College London. In 2016, they published results showing that psilocybin could produce significant, lasting relief in patients with treatment-resistant depression, people who had failed multiple rounds of conventional treatment. Then in 2020, Roland Griffiths and his team at Johns Hopkins published a rigorously controlled trial confirming that psilocybin-assisted therapy could effectively treat major depressive disorder. These were not small findings. Treatment-resistant depression affects tens of millions of people and costs the healthcare system hundreds of billions of dollars. Conventional medicine had no good answer for it. These studies suggested that something stigmatized and Schedule I might do what a century of pharmaceutical development had not.

That got attention.

The horse was inside the gates.

## The Price of the Agreement

Here is where it gets complicated.

The medical framework that allowed these substances back into the conversation is also, quietly, putting them in boxes. Psilocybin is the depression drug. MDMA is the PTSD drug. Ibogaine is the addiction drug. Each substance gets assigned to a room. The room has a diagnosis on the door. Access requires that diagnosis. Everything outside the room is, officially, still off the table.

The research is real. The clinical applications are important. But the framing is incomplete in ways that have specific, concrete consequences.

Every one of those trials was organized around a diagnostic category. A DSM code. A defined condition with measurable endpoints that the existing research infrastructure knows how to evaluate. This is how clinical research works, and it works this way for reasons that are structural rather than conspiratorial. Research requires funding. Funding follows conditions that the existing system recognizes, reimburses, and cares about addressing. The diagnostic category is the unit of currency.

This produces rigorous science inside a particular set of boundaries. What it does not produce is a complete picture of what this medicine can do or who it can serve.

The population that could benefit from this work is not the population that shows up in clinical trial inclusion criteria. The person who does not meet threshold for any particular diagnosis (not depressed enough, not traumatized enough to qualify, carrying no DSM code but also not living the life they are capable of living) does not exist in this research framework. In my clinical experience, this is a large population. It is also exactly the population this medicine is often most consistently useful for. We are not generating data on them. And the absence of data will shape what becomes accessible, to whom, and under what conditions.

There is a longer consequence worth thinking about.

When FDA approval arrives (and based on the current trajectory of the evidence, it will arrive), it will come with an indication, a label, a standardized protocol, and a clinical infrastructure designed to deliver the treatment in a form the approval process can evaluate and certify. What that infrastructure can certify is safety and reproducibility. What it cannot certify is the relational container. The quality of preparation. The depth of integration support. The music, the setting, the permission to go wherever the medicine needs to go. Those variables cannot be standardized without being lost.

In the trials, they are controlled for. Held constant. The point is to isolate what the molecule does. The medicine that gets approved will be the medicine the trials measured: the molecule, delivered in a defined protocol, by a trained clinician, in a setting designed for reproducibility rather than depth.

Some of those sessions will still produce meaningful outcomes. The medicine is too potent to be entirely contained by a thin protocol. But the outcomes will be smaller than what becomes possible when the container is right, and the people who needed more than symptom relief will not always get it.

They will conclude the medicine failed.

The medicine will not have failed. The frame will have failed. But the headline will say psychedelics, and the next funding cycle will reflect it, and the opening we are in right now will be a little narrower than it was before.

The researchers doing this work understand these tensions. Many of them are working as carefully as the system allows. The constraints are not of their making. The system that funds and approves medical treatments was not designed to evaluate what makes psychedelic healing work, and it will not expand to accommodate it without sustained pressure, time, and probably some instructive failures.

## The More Interesting Question

What Harman and Fadiman were looking at in that room in Menlo Park in 1966 was not a diagnosis. It was a door in the mind that these substances seem to be able to open. The question they were asking was not "what condition does this fix?" The question was "what does this make possible?"

That is still the more interesting question.

The Trojan horse was necessary. It got the work back inside the gates. The clinical trials are generating data that will protect access, build credibility, and help people who have been failed by everything else. None of that should be dismissed.

And the horse was still a horse. What it carried inside is now operating according to rules that were written for a different kind of medicine, and those rules will not capture everything that matters about this work.

This book is an attempt to hold both. To honor the research and to refuse the reduction. To take the clinical evidence seriously without allowing it to define the ceiling.

The door Harman and Fadiman found sixty years ago is open again. What we do next, and how honestly we reckon with the terms of the agreement that got us here, will determine how long it stays that way.
