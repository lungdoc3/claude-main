**Where We Start**

Psilocybin is the most clinically studied classical psychedelic. That is not a coincidence. It has a relatively short duration, a manageable side-effect profile, and a pharmacological fingerprint clean enough that researchers could design controlled trials around it. The FDA granted it Breakthrough Therapy designation for treatment-resistant depression. Phase III trials are ongoing. The body of interaction data, while still incomplete in places, is better than what exists for mescaline and substantially more systematized than what exists for ayahuasca.

That relative clarity makes psilocybin the right place to start. The concepts introduced here — how it works, what interacts with it and why, where the real risks are and where the concern is overstated — form the foundation for everything that follows in the substance-specific sections. Mescaline is discussed as a differential from this baseline. Ayahuasca is a departure from it significant enough to require its own complete account.

---

**The Pharmacological Foundation**

Psilocybin is a prodrug. When you swallow it, the compound itself is inactive — it does nothing until the body's phosphatase enzymes remove a phosphate group, converting it into psilocin. Psilocin is the active compound. The conversion happens quickly, primarily in the small intestine and liver, which is why effects typically begin within thirty to sixty minutes of ingestion.

Psilocin's primary mechanism is agonism at the 5-HT2A serotonin receptor. These receptors are concentrated in the prefrontal cortex — the region responsible for executive function, meaning-making, and the default mode network that governs our ordinary sense of self. When psilocin activates 5-HT2A receptors in sufficient numbers, that network's ordinary dominance loosens. Perception shifts. The usual boundaries between self and world become more permeable. The qualities of experience that make psilocybin therapeutically and spiritually significant become available.

Psilocin also has modest activity at other serotonin receptor subtypes — 5-HT1A among them — and weakly inhibits the serotonin transporter (SERT), the protein responsible for removing serotonin from the synaptic gap. This SERT inhibition is mild compared to what an antidepressant does, but it is not zero. It contributes a small reuptake-inhibiting effect on top of the receptor agonism, which is relevant when calculating total serotonergic load in the context of drug interactions.

What psilocin does not do: it does not substantially affect dopamine receptors. It does not trigger monoamine release the way stimulants do. It does not inhibit MAO enzymes. It has no meaningful adrenergic receptor activity. Its pharmacological profile is comparatively focused — primarily serotonergic, primarily through one receptor subtype — which is one of the reasons its interaction profile is more navigable than some of the other medicines in this section.

Psilocin is metabolized primarily by MAO-A and by glucuronidation enzymes (UGTs). It is not significantly processed by the CYP450 liver enzymes that govern most pharmaceutical drug interactions. This matters: many of the drug-drug interactions people worry about with psilocybin do not operate through the usual liver-metabolism pathway. The relevant interactions are pharmacodynamic — they occur at the receptor and neurotransmitter level, not in the liver.

Duration of effect at a full psychoactive dose is typically four to six hours, with significant individual variability. The subacute period is generally clean — most people feel largely returned to baseline by twelve hours, with some residual afterglow that is typically mild and positively valenced.

**The Interaction Landscape**

**Serotonergic antidepressants — blunting and the serotonin syndrome question.**

The most common interaction in clinical practice is not a dangerous one. It is a frustrating one.

People on SSRIs or SNRIs — the most commonly prescribed antidepressants — frequently find that psilocybin produces little or nothing. The experience is blunted, attenuated, or in some cases entirely absent. The mechanism is the 5-HT2A downregulation discussed in the general pharmaceutical section: chronic antidepressant use raises synaptic serotonin, the brain adapts by reducing receptor density and sensitivity, and psilocybin arrives to find fewer windows open. The medicine cannot do its work through a system that has been systematically dialed back.

This is the most clinically important antidepressant interaction for most people, because most people seeking psychedelic work are not asking about dangerous combinations — they are trying to understand why the medicine is not working, or whether they need to taper their antidepressant before ceremony.

The washout question is real and requires individual clinical attention. SSRIs have varying half-lives. Stopping them requires appropriate medical supervision, particularly for anyone who has been on them for an extended period and may experience discontinuation symptoms. The timing of washout before a psilocybin experience, and the timing of restarting afterward, is a clinical conversation that belongs between a patient and a physician who understands both sides of it.

The second antidepressant concern — serotonin syndrome — is real in theory and documented in the broader serotonergic drug literature, but the specific evidence with psilocybin is less alarming than the pharmacology alone would suggest. Documented cases of serotonin syndrome from psilocybin combined with an SSRI are uncommon in the published literature, likely because psilocybin's weak SERT inhibition adds relatively little to the overall serotonergic load when an SSRI is already present. The theoretical risk is not zero and warrants acknowledgment, particularly as the number of converging serotonergic inputs increases. The practical risk, for most psilocybin-SSRI combinations, appears to be primarily experiential — a blunted or absent journey — rather than medically dangerous. That framing does not eliminate clinical caution. It calibrates it.

SNRIs carry the same general profile as SSRIs, with the addition of norepinephrine reuptake inhibition, which adds a modest adrenergic layer to the interaction picture.

**MAO inhibitors — caution, with a caveat.**

MAO-A participates in psilocin's metabolic clearance. When MAO-A is inhibited — by pharmaceutical MAOIs, or by the natural MAO-inhibiting supplements covered in the supplement section — psilocin clears more slowly and its effects may be prolonged and intensified. Combined with the elevated serotonin that results from MAO inhibition, the serotonin syndrome risk becomes more mechanistically direct.

Pharmaceutical MAOIs warrant a full washout before psilocybin work — two weeks minimum for most irreversible MAOIs, following the same principle as their contraindication with serotonergic antidepressants. The natural MAO inhibitors — passionflower, rhodiola rosea, methylene blue — deserve the same category of caution even though their mechanisms are less potent, because they add to the serotonergic environment without most people recognizing them as pharmacologically relevant. The supplement section covers these in full.

**Antipsychotics — the experience blocker.**

Antipsychotic medications block 5-HT2A receptors. That is not a side effect of their action — it is a central mechanism of how they work. When 5-HT2A is blocked, psilocin has nowhere to bind. The result, demonstrated in controlled clinical trials, is a dose-dependent attenuation or complete elimination of the psychedelic experience.

This is clinically relevant in two directions. Before ceremony, antipsychotics on board mean the therapeutic potential of the medicine is compromised or absent. In the context of a very difficult experience that requires intervention, certain antipsychotics — specifically those with significant 5-HT2A blocking activity — can reduce the intensity of the experience. That is a tool in emergency management, not a routine use.

The older antipsychotic haloperidol is worth a specific note: it is primarily a dopamine antagonist rather than a 5-HT2A antagonist. In a controlled psilocybin study, haloperidol reduced some components of the experience while paradoxically increasing anxiety, without affecting visual hallucinations. It is not a reliable rescue medication for a difficult psilocybin experience. Knowing which antipsychotic does what, at the receptor level, matters more than knowing the drug class in general.

Mirtazapine, an antidepressant that functions as a potent 5-HT2A antagonist as a secondary mechanism, blunts psilocybin for the same reason antipsychotics do. This is not widely known and catches people off guard.

**Lithium — the hard stop.**

Lithium and classical psychedelics do not belong together. A Johns Hopkins analysis of reported combinations found that nearly half involved seizures, and roughly a third of psilocybin-specific reports involved a seizure requiring medical attention. The leading mechanism involves convergence of two independently excitatory effects on cortical activity crossing a threshold for seizure. Whatever the full mechanism, the clinical conclusion is unambiguous: lithium is a hard contraindication with psilocybin and all classical psychedelics.

This matters particularly because lithium is prescribed for bipolar disorder — a condition for which psychedelic therapy is being actively explored as a potential treatment. The interaction does not close that door. It is a reason any clinical protocol working at that intersection must account for it explicitly, including appropriate washout.

**Benzodiazepines — a double-edged consideration.**

Benzodiazepines blunt the psilocybin experience through general CNS sedation. They do not interact with the serotonin system directly — the effect is a GABAergic dimmer switch, not a receptor-level antagonism. Routine use before ceremony compromises the therapeutic value of the experience and is not clinically advisable.

The other side of that equation: in the context of a very difficult experience that is not resolving and where someone needs support, benzodiazepines are among the cleaner emergency tools available. They reduce intensity without the receptor-blocking complications of antipsychotics. Their availability as a contingency in ceremony settings has a reasonable clinical rationale. Pre-ceremony routine use does not.

**Tricyclics — cardiac conduction plus serotonin.**

Older tricyclic antidepressants carry two concerns stacked on top of each other. Their serotonergic activity creates the same class of interaction risk as SSRIs. Their effect on the heart's electrical conduction system — which psilocybin does not share — adds a cardiac concern that does not exist with the newer antidepressants. The combination of a psychedelic experience's cardiovascular stimulation with a drug that affects the QT interval deserves real clinical attention. Tricyclics are not commonly prescribed now, but they are still out there.

**Tramadol — the one people miss.**

Most people think of tramadol as a pain medication and stop there. It is also a serotonin reuptake inhibitor with weak MAO-inhibiting properties — a pharmacological profile that puts it in the same interaction category as antidepressants for purposes of psilocybin screening. Documented serotonin syndrome cases involving tramadol and serotonergic medications exist in the literature. It should appear on every pre-ceremony medication screen, not just the psychiatric medication section.

**Triptans — blocking effects and vasoconstriction.**

Triptans, used for acute migraine management, are serotonin receptor agonists at subtypes including 5-HT1B and 5-HT1D. They also produce vasoconstriction as their mechanism for migraine relief. Combined with psilocybin, the concerns are two: additive serotonergic activity across receptor subtypes, and overlapping vasoconstrictive effects with psilocybin's own cardiovascular influence. Community experience among people who use psychedelics for cluster headache management is consistent: triptans taken close to a psychedelic experience reliably diminish or block it. Timing matters.

**Cardiovascular medications.**

Psilocybin produces reliable cardiovascular stimulation — elevated heart rate and blood pressure — that is well-tolerated in healthy individuals and generally transient within the four-to-six-hour window. For people on cardiac medications, the picture is more nuanced.

Beta-blockers dampen the adrenergic response and may reduce peak cardiovascular stimulation, which is generally protective rather than harmful. The exception is carvedilol, which carries documented 5-HT2A activity and may attenuate the psychedelic experience through that mechanism on top of its cardiac effects.

The general principle for any cardiac medication — antihypertensives, antiarrhythmics, anticoagulants — is that the existing cardiovascular picture needs to be fully understood before adding the cardiovascular stimulation of a psychedelic experience. This is a clinical conversation, not a blanket contraindication.

**Natural MAO inhibitors and serotonergic supplements.**

Passionflower, rhodiola rosea, and methylene blue all carry documented MAO-inhibiting activity. St. John's Wort, 5-HTP, L-tryptophan, and SAMe all add to the serotonergic environment through various mechanisms. None of them are prescription medications. All of them are relevant to a pre-psilocybin screening conversation. The supplement section covers each in detail, including the regulatory context that makes supplement interactions harder to assess than pharmaceutical ones.

**What Makes Psilocybin's Profile Relatively Navigable**

It is worth saying plainly, because it gets lost in the complexity of interaction lists: psilocybin's pharmacological profile is among the cleaner ones in this section.

No built-in MAOI. No dietary restrictions of the kind ayahuasca requires. No adrenergic receptor activity of the kind mescaline has. A focused mechanism — primarily one receptor subtype — that produces a relatively predictable interaction fingerprint. A duration that, while immersive, is not the eleven-hour commitment mescaline can require. And a growing body of controlled clinical research that makes the interaction data more reliable than what exists for the other medicines.

None of that makes psilocybin interaction-free. The lithium contraindication is hard. The antidepressant blunting is real and affects a substantial portion of the people seeking this work. The natural supplement interactions are underappreciated and genuinely relevant. But the overall interaction landscape is more navigable than what follows in the ayahuasca section, and the data is more solid than what exists for mescaline.

This is the baseline. What changes from here is the subject of the sections that follow.

**Summary: Key Interaction Categories**

| Compound / Class | Primary Risk | Mechanism | Clinical Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| SSRIs / SNRIs | Blunted or absent experience; theoretical SS risk | 5-HT2A downregulation from chronic use; additive serotonergic load | Washout under medical supervision; timing is a clinical conversation |
| Irreversible MAOIs | Serotonin syndrome risk; prolonged / intensified effects | MAO-A participates in psilocin clearance; inhibition slows metabolism and raises serotonin | Minimum 2-week washout |
| Tricyclic antidepressants | Cardiac conduction + serotonergic risk | QT interval effects + serotonin reuptake inhibition | Treat as higher-risk than SSRIs; medical review required |
| Tramadol | Serotonin syndrome risk | Serotonin reuptake inhibition + weak MAOI; often missed in screening | Flag as serotonergic drug, not just a pain medication |
| Lithium | Seizure risk | Cortical excitability convergence; documented in case series | Hard contraindication; do not combine |
| Antipsychotics | Blocked experience | 5-HT2A antagonism; receptor-level opposition | Experience compromised; available as emergency tool for difficult sessions |
| Mirtazapine | Blocked experience | Potent 5-HT2A antagonism as secondary mechanism | Same as antipsychotics; often not recognized as a blocker |
| Triptans | Blunted experience; additive vasoconstriction | Serotonin receptor agonism at different subtypes; overlapping cardiovascular effects | Avoid in close temporal proximity to ceremony |
| Benzodiazepines | Blunted experience (routine); useful emergency tool | GABAergic sedation; not receptor-level antagonism | Avoid routine pre-ceremony use; keep available for emergency support |
| Beta-blockers (general) | Dampened cardiovascular response | Adrenergic receptor blockade | Generally not a hard stop; carvedilol has 5-HT2A activity — note which agent |
| Passionflower / rhodiola / methylene blue | MAO inhibition; elevated serotonergic load | MAO-inhibiting compounds in supplement form | Treat as pharmacological MAOI concern; see supplement section |
| SSRIs in supplement form (SJW, 5-HTP, L-tryptophan) | Additive serotonergic load | Serotonin precursors and reuptake effects | Disclose and review; not pharmacologically neutral |
