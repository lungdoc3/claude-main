# Safety Is Relational

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

In pharmaceutical medicine, safety is primarily a property of the molecule.

You establish it through trials. You identify the dose range, the drug interactions, the contraindications, the adverse event profile. You build that information into the label. The prescribing physician screens the patient, accounts for known risk factors, and delivers the molecule. Safety is, in large part, a function of knowing the drug well enough to use it carefully. This works because the mechanism is molecular and the primary risks are molecular.

The medical risks of psychedelics are also, in the main, molecular. Cardiovascular considerations, drug interactions, contraindications for certain psychiatric histories: real, known, and manageable with appropriate screening. They belong on any responsible list and they deserve attention.

They are not, however, where the significant risk lives.

The molecule opens a window. The person on the other side of that window is more open, more impressionable, and more trusting than they ordinarily are. Their usual filters are loosened. Their capacity to be shaped by what happens in that room, by what is said and not said, by the quality of presence around them, by whether the space feels safe enough to stop resisting: is at its highest. That is not a side effect of the medicine. It is how the medicine works. And it means that everything happening in that room is part of the active intervention, not the backdrop to it.

The molecule isn't the danger. The container is.

A guide who is inadequately trained or who has lost their way is a safety issue, not a quality-of-service issue. Preparation that is cursory or absent is a risk factor, not a missed opportunity for enrichment. Integration support is not optional aftercare offered to the motivated. It is where the psychological material that surfaced in ceremony either gets worked with properly or compounds into something harder to carry.

The same substrate that makes profound healing possible makes genuine harm possible. The window that opens onto something extraordinary can also open onto something overwhelming. What determines which it becomes is not the molecule. It is the quality of everything surrounding the molecule: the preparation, the container, the guide, the integration. In pharmaceutical medicine, the relationship between prescriber and patient matters, but it is not typically the mechanism of the treatment. In psychedelic medicine, the relationship is load-bearing. It is part of how the medicine works. Which means it is also part of how things go wrong.

This is why guide selection is a clinical decision. Why preparation is the foundation of what becomes possible, not an administrative formality. Why integration is built into the intervention rather than offered as an add-on for those who want it.

The ceremonial traditions that have worked with these medicines for centuries did not develop elaborate preparation practices and sustained integration support for aesthetic reasons. They developed them because working with people in these states, over a very long time, made it obvious that the container was not separate from the medicine. The two were one thing. And the safety of the whole depended on the integrity of the whole.

The pharmaceutical safety question is: is this molecule appropriate for this patient?

The psychedelic safety question is: is this container adequate for what this medicine is about to make possible in this person?

Both questions matter. The first one alone is not enough.
