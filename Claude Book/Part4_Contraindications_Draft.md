**Who Should Not Sit**

Psychedelics work, in part, by loosening the ordinary boundaries of perception and selfhood. That loosening is the point. For someone with a stable relationship to reality, it creates the conditions for insight, healing, and transformation. For someone whose relationship to reality is already compromised, it creates something else entirely.

That is the organizing principle behind the contraindications in this section. It is not that these individuals have nothing to heal or nothing to gain. It is that the specific mechanism by which these medicines work is not safe when the underlying architecture is already fragile.

---

**Schizophrenia and Active Psychosis**

Psychosis, at its core, is a temporary or persistent disconnect from shared reality. Hallucinations, delusions, and distorted perception are not metaphors in schizophrenia. They are the experience. A psychedelic experience and a psychotic episode share enough phenomenological territory that clinicians have long noted the resemblance. The difference, for most participants, is that the psychedelic experience is time-limited and reversible. They return.

For someone with schizophrenia or a history of active psychosis, that return is not guaranteed. The phenomenological overlap between psychedelic experience and psychotic experience was not lost on the early psychiatric researchers who first studied these compounds. Before Humphry Osmond coined the term "psychedelic" in 1957, the prevailing clinical term was "psychotomimetic." Psychosis-mimicking. Psychiatrists took mescaline and LSD specifically to gain first-hand insight into what their schizophrenic patients experienced. The experiment was illuminating. What it inadvertently confirmed is the clinical argument against using these medicines with people who already live in that territory. The experience is close enough to psychosis to serve as a model for it. For someone with schizophrenia, that is not a therapeutic entry point. It is a risk.

The neurological architecture that would normally allow someone to re-integrate the experience and find their way back is already compromised. Psychedelics in this population carry documented risk of triggering psychotic episodes, worsening existing symptoms, and producing states that do not resolve cleanly when the medicine does. This is not a judgment about the person. It is a statement about the mechanism. The tool is wrong for the substrate.

Personal history of psychosis, and a first-degree family history of psychotic disorders, both warrant serious caution. The genetic loading for psychotic vulnerability is real and relevant.

---

**Bipolar Disorder**

The pharmacological case against psychedelics in bipolar disorder is less precisely characterized than it is for schizophrenia, but the clinical concern is no less real.

People with bipolar disorder are not continuously symptomatic. Many maintain stability through medication, lifestyle, and careful management of what their nervous system is exposed to. The risk with psychedelics is destabilization. The medicine does not respect equilibrium that has been carefully constructed. It introduces an intensity of experience that can precipitate a manic or depressive episode. In bipolar disorder, each episode can lower the threshold for the next. The concern is not that the experience will be unpleasant. The concern is that it may tip someone out of a balance they have worked hard to maintain, and that tipping has consequences that do not stay within the bounds of the ceremony.

This is a case-by-case clinical conversation. It requires a mental health professional who knows the patient well, understands the medicine, and can make an informed judgment. Not a retreat center waiver.
