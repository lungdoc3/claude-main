## Part 4 — Food Interactions Reference

**Two Kinds of Restriction**

The dieta is one of the oldest safety protocols in plant medicine work. Different lineages have different versions of it, but most traditions converge on some variation of the same core list: avoid pork, alcohol, fermented foods, sex, salt, and sugar in the period before ceremony. Generations of healers have passed this guidance forward, and it has generally served people well.

What the tradition does not always specify — because it did not need to — is why.

Some of what the dieta prohibits will hurt you if you ignore it. The tyramine-rich foods on the list are genuinely dangerous in the context of active MAO inhibition: the pharmacology that makes ayahuasca work also removes the body's first line of defense against a compound that, in sufficient quantity, can cause a hypertensive crisis. That is not a subtle risk or a theoretical concern. It is a documented mechanism. Those restrictions belong on the list for the same reason a physician would give them to a patient starting phenelzine.

Other restrictions on the list have nothing to do with pharmacology. They are about intentionality. About preparing a body that is ready to receive, rather than one still processing last night's feast. About entering a sacred space with attention and care rather than on autopilot. These are real reasons. They just are not pharmacological ones. Treating them as equivalent — either by overclaiming the science or by dismissing the tradition because the science is thin — misses the point of both.

The table below tries to hold both honestly. The verdict column is not meant to invalidate anything. It is meant to give you enough information to make informed decisions, and to have an honest conversation with the people in your care.

**A Note on Fresh vs. Aged**

Not all cheese is the same. Not all soy is the same. Not all alcohol is the same. One of the most common distortions in how the dieta gets communicated is collapsing whole food categories when the actual risk is specific to a subset. Fresh mozzarella has negligible tyramine. Aged parmesan has a lot. Fresh tofu is low. Fermented miso is high. Bottled beer has less tyramine than homebrew. These distinctions matter clinically, even if they are harder to communicate in a ceremony context than a blanket restriction.

The table notes where the restriction applies to a whole category and where it applies to specific forms within that category.

**What the Table Does Not Cover**

Sexual abstinence is a near-universal element of traditional dieta and is worth acknowledging, even though it is not a food restriction. The pharmacological basis for sexual abstinence during dieta is essentially absent. The traditional basis is consistent and deeply held across lineages. It belongs in the same category as salt: no documented mechanism, real ceremonial meaning, worth respecting on its own terms.

Fasting entirely before ceremony is sometimes presented as required. It is not universal across traditions, and the rationale that exists — reducing nausea, entering ceremony with a clean system — supports a light meal approach rather than full fasting. Full pre-ceremony fasting has its own risks, particularly for people with blood sugar regulation issues or those on medications affected by food intake. Blanket fasting instructions should be individualized, not applied uniformly.
