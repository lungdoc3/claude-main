# The Five Themes: Introduction Paragraphs

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

---

## I. We Treat What We Can Fix, Not What Truly Ails Us

Modern medicine is extraordinarily good at treating the wrong thing. Not out of malice, and not entirely out of negligence, but out of a structural bias built into the system: we grant "disease" status to conditions we can intervene on, and we stop digging precisely where our tools run out. Coronary artery disease is not a disease. It is the final, measurable expression of decades of upstream causes: chronic stress, inflammation, poor nutrition, unresolved emotional injury, a life lived in a body that was never given what it needed. The plaque is the symptom. We stent the symptom and call it treatment. This is not healing. It is a sophisticated form of looking away, and this book is an argument for looking further upstream, past the interventionally convenient, toward what is actually driving the suffering we are trying to address.

---

## II. The Pharmaceutical Model Is a Dead End

Drug development used to be like early bitcoin mining: relatively accessible, rewarding, worth the effort. That era is over. The returns have diminished, the costs have become staggering, and the gap between what gets developed and what people actually need has never been wider. The response from the industry has been, in many cases, to redefine the need to fit the product: to medicalize ordinary human experience until the market for symptom management becomes essentially unlimited. Into this landscape arrive psychedelics, which the pharmaceutical model spent decades ignoring because they could not be neatly packaged and repeatedly purchased. They are, from any honest assessment, a previously overlooked pile of extraordinary value, compounds with millions of years of human trials behind them that require something the pharmaceutical model is not designed to accommodate: genuine engagement from the person being treated. That is not a bug. It is the whole point.

---

## III. Psychedelics Don't Fix You

This may be the most important thing I say in this book: psychedelics are not medicine in the way that antibiotics are medicine. They do not act on you. They act with you, or more precisely, they create conditions under which you can act on yourself in ways that are not available under ordinary consciousness. They are mirrors. Catalysts. Initiators. The insight that surfaces in a well-held ceremony, the emotional release, the reconsolidation of a traumatic memory, the dissolution of a story that has been running your life without your consent: none of that is delivered by the compound. The compound opens the door. You are the one who has to walk through it, and what you find on the other side is yours. If we allow the pharmaceutical narrative to dominate psychedelic medicine, we will lose the very essence of what makes these substances powerful. We will turn them into sterile, symptom-suppressing treatments that fit neatly into diagnostic codes and miss the point entirely. Forcing these substances into a framework designed to produce passive patients who consume products until their symptoms quiet down does not just limit their potential. It destroys it.

---

## IV. The Brain Is a Tool, Not the Truth

The brain is a prediction machine. Its primary job is not to perceive reality accurately but to generate the most efficient model of reality it can, based on prior experience, and to present that model to you as if it were simply what is. It is extraordinarily good at this, and it will defend its predictions against incoming evidence with a tenacity that looks, from the inside, exactly like certainty. The phantom limb is the most elegant proof: the brain will generate vivid, painful sensation in an arm that no longer exists because the model demands it. We are all, to varying degrees, living inside models that were built from experiences we did not choose and that we have not meaningfully updated since. The work of psychedelic therapy is, at its core, the interruption of that predictive loop. Long enough for something genuinely new to become possible. Long enough for the person to discover that the model is not the territory, and that the territory is larger than they had been allowed to believe.

---

## V. Safety Is Sacred, and Strategic

I believe in psychedelic safety for two reasons, and the second one is as important as the first. The first is simple: people deserve not to be harmed. The second is this: every adverse event in this space is a political event. Every ethical failure, every poorly held ceremony, every preventable injury becomes ammunition for the forces that would shut this work down entirely, and those forces are paying attention. Safety is not just how we protect individuals. It is how we protect access. It is how we ensure that these medicines remain available to the people who need them, in settings that honor what they actually are, rather than being surrendered to regulatory frameworks designed to contain them. The psychedelic renaissance is still fragile. What we do now, how seriously we take the responsibility of working with these medicines, will determine whether this moment becomes a permanent opening or a window that closes again. Safety is not a constraint on the work. It is the foundation that makes the work possible.
