# Ayahuasca: The Full Picture
## Part 4 — Substance-Specific Interaction Document

---

*[Note: This is not a differential. Ayahuasca does not get a "what changes from the general discussion" treatment because the pharmacological foundation is different enough to require its own complete account. The reason mescaline needed a differential was that its core mechanism — 5-HT2A agonism — is shared with psilocybin. Ayahuasca contains a 5-HT2A agonist too, but it also contains a built-in monoamine oxidase inhibitor that is not incidental to the preparation — it is the mechanism by which the preparation works at all. You cannot understand the interactions without understanding the MAOI first. So we start there. First-pass draft in Randy's voice.]*

---

**Grandmother Has a Different Pharmacology**

There is a reason experienced practitioners speak of ayahuasca with a reverence that feels different from how they speak of other medicines. Part of that reverence is cultural and ceremonial. Part of it is the nature of the experience itself. And part of it — the part this section addresses — is that ayahuasca is pharmacologically more complex than any other classical psychedelic.

It is not a single compound. It is a combination medicine. Two plants, brought together in a preparation that has been refined over centuries through empirical observation, produce an effect that neither plant can produce alone. Understanding why that is the case — at the level of neurotransmitters and enzymes — is the prerequisite for understanding every interaction that follows.

---

**The Pharmacological Foundation: Why It Works at All**

DMT — N,N-dimethyltryptamine — is the primary psychoactive compound in ayahuasca, contributed by the admixture plant, most commonly Psychotria viridis (chacruna) or Diplopterys cabrerana (chaliponga). DMT is a potent 5-HT2A agonist. It binds the same receptor psilocin does, which is why its effects share the perceptual and psychological character of other classical psychedelics.

Here is the catch: DMT is almost completely inactive when taken orally. The enzyme monoamine oxidase, concentrated in the gut wall and the liver, breaks it down so efficiently that essentially none of the compound reaches the brain. Taken orally without intervention, a DMT-containing plant produces nothing of significance. Shamans in the Amazon discovered, through centuries of trial and working knowledge, that combining the DMT-containing plant with Banisteriopsis caapi — the ayahuasca vine — changes everything.

The vine contains three primary beta-carboline alkaloids: harmine, harmaline, and tetrahydroharmine (THH). Harmine and harmaline are potent, reversible inhibitors of monoamine oxidase A — the enzyme that would otherwise destroy the DMT before it reaches systemic circulation. When MAO-A is inhibited, DMT survives the gut and liver, enters the bloodstream, crosses the blood-brain barrier, and reaches the 5-HT2A receptors it binds.

This is not a drug interaction in the conventional sense. This is intentional pharmacological engineering. The MAOI is not contaminating the preparation or creating an unintended risk. It is the preparation. Without the MAOI, there is no ayahuasca. There is only plant tea.

The clinical implication of this fact runs through every interaction in this section. The question is never "what happens if someone taking ayahuasca is also exposed to an MAOI?" The question is always "what happens when someone adds something to a preparation that already has an active MAOI built in?" That is a different question, and it leads to different clinical conclusions.

---

**Three Layers of Serotonergic Activity**

Psilocybin produces serotonergic effects through two mechanisms: 5-HT2A agonism, and weak inhibition of the serotonin transporter by psilocin. Mescaline produces effects through 5-HT2A agonism without the SERT component. Ayahuasca produces effects through three converging serotonergic mechanisms simultaneously.

The first is 5-HT2A agonism from DMT. This is the primary psychedelic mechanism, shared with the other classical psychedelics.

The second is MAO-A inhibition from harmine and harmaline. MAO-A is the primary enzyme responsible for breaking down serotonin in the gut and, to a degree, in the CNS. When MAO-A is inhibited, serotonin is not cleared at its normal rate. Synaptic serotonin levels rise. This is not just a pharmacokinetic convenience for getting DMT into the brain — it is an active pharmacodynamic effect on serotonin itself.

The third is serotonin reuptake inhibition from THH. Tetrahydroharmine, the third major alkaloid in B. caapi, functions as a weak selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor — a mechanism pharmacologically similar to the SSRI antidepressants. It slows the removal of serotonin from the synapse through a second, distinct pathway.

Three mechanisms converging on elevated serotonin: reduced breakdown, reduced reuptake, and direct receptor activation. This is why every serotonergic drug interaction that is a moderate concern with psilocybin becomes a serious concern with ayahuasca. The baseline serotonergic load of the medicine itself is substantially higher.

**Hard Contraindications**

**Serotonergic antidepressants — SSRIs and SNRIs.**

With psilocybin, the concern about serotonergic antidepressants has two components: the blunting of the psychedelic experience through 5-HT2A downregulation, and a theoretical but uncommon serotonin syndrome risk. With ayahuasca, the risk calculation changes fundamentally.

SSRIs work by blocking the serotonin transporter — raising synaptic serotonin by preventing its reuptake. An ayahuasca preparation already contains harmine and harmaline (blocking serotonin breakdown) and THH (blocking serotonin reuptake). Adding an SSRI adds a fourth mechanism driving serotonin up, on top of a preparation that is already converging three mechanisms in that direction.

The theoretical risk of serotonin syndrome in this combination is high, for mechanistic reasons that are straightforward. Documented case reports in the published literature are uncommon — Callaway and Grob described one case of what appeared to be a benign serotonin syndrome reaction in 1998. But the rarity of published cases should not be confused with clinical safety. People combining ayahuasca with SSRIs rarely present to academic medical centers and write up their outcomes. The pharmacology is what it is.

SSRIs and SNRIs are a hard contraindication with ayahuasca. Not a caution, not a "discuss with your physician," not a "it depends on the dose." The pharmacological mechanism for serious serotonin toxicity is direct and additive. Standard washout guidance for SSRIs before pharmaceutical MAOIs applies: two weeks for most SSRIs (five half-lives), five weeks for fluoxetine given its long-acting active metabolite. Those washout principles apply here.

**Tricyclic antidepressants.**

Tricyclics carry their own serotonin reuptake inhibition in addition to their cardiac conduction effects. The combination of a tricyclic with an MAOI — pharmaceutical or otherwise — has a documented history of producing severe serotonin syndrome and hypertensive crisis in the psychiatric pharmacology literature. This is not a theoretical concern. It is a well-characterized interaction with serious clinical outcomes. Hard contraindication.

**Tramadol.**

Tramadol, discussed in the general pharmaceutical section as an underappreciated serotonergic drug, carries a documented history of serotonin syndrome in combination with MAO inhibitors. The cases in the published literature involve pharmaceutical MAOIs, but the mechanism is the same: tramadol's serotonin reuptake inhibition plus its weak MAOI properties, stacked on top of active MAO inhibition, creates a situation where serotonin toxicity has an established pathway. This is not a pain medication that can be screened past. It is a serotonergic drug in the same risk class as the antidepressants for purposes of ayahuasca screening.

**Meperidine (pethidine).**

Among opioid medications, meperidine is uniquely contraindicated with MAOIs — a combination associated with severe, occasionally fatal serotonin syndrome in the anesthesia and emergency medicine literature. The mechanism involves meperidine's serotonin reuptake inhibition combined with MAOI-driven serotonin accumulation. Other opioids carry lower risk through this specific mechanism, but meperidine is in a category of its own. Absolute contraindication.

**Stimulants — amphetamines, cocaine.**

MAO inhibitors combined with sympathomimetic stimulants produce hypertensive crisis through excessive release of norepinephrine. The standard clinical teaching around pharmaceutical MAOIs prohibits amphetamines for this reason. The harmine-class MAOIs in ayahuasca are reversible, which makes their sympathomimetic interaction profile somewhat less severe than irreversible pharmaceutical MAOIs in certain contexts — but the acute MAO inhibition during and around the ceremony is pharmacologically active and the interaction risk during that window is real. Any stimulant drug use in close proximity to ayahuasca is contraindicated.

**Lithium.**

The seizure risk with lithium and classical psychedelics, documented in the Johns Hopkins analysis, applies to ayahuasca as it does to psilocybin. The additional serotonergic load from the MAOI component does not change the primary mechanism of concern (cortical excitability convergence), but does add to the overall pharmacological complexity. Hard contraindication, same as with other classical psychedelics.

**The Tyramine Question: What the Dieta Is Actually For**

Every ayahuasca tradition that has been practiced long enough has its own version of the dieta — a period of dietary restriction before ceremony. Different traditions vary in their specifics. But the convergence on avoiding fermented foods, aged cheeses, alcohol, and cured meats is not coincidental. It is empirical safety pharmacology, encoded in ceremonial practice before the pharmacology was understood.

MAO-A in the gut normally handles tyramine — an amino acid derivative found in fermented, aged, and protein-rich foods. It metabolizes tyramine so efficiently that most tyramine in the diet is neutralized before it reaches systemic circulation. When MAO-A is inhibited, that protection is removed. Tyramine enters the bloodstream, triggers norepinephrine release, and in sufficient quantity can cause hypertensive crisis — rapid, severe elevation of blood pressure that carries risk of stroke and cardiac events.

With pharmaceutical irreversible MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine), the tyramine restriction is extensive and the risks are well-documented. Harmine and harmaline are reversible — the MAO-A inhibition resolves over hours rather than weeks — which means the dietary restriction applies primarily to the active window around ceremony, not to an extended post-dose period.

What this means clinically: the traditional dieta recommendation to avoid tyramine-rich foods in the period around ceremony is pharmacologically sound. The specific concern is during the active window of MAO inhibition. Foods with high tyramine load — aged cheeses, cured and fermented meats, fermented soy products (tempeh, miso, soy sauce), tap and homebrew beer, wine, overripe or dried fruits, broad beans — should be avoided on ceremony day and reasonably avoided in the day before. The restriction lifts as harmine clears — generally within 24 hours of the last dose.

This is one of the areas where indigenous ceremonial practice and western pharmacology arrived at the same answer through completely different routes. The dieta is not purely spiritual preparation. It is also harm reduction.

---

**Pharmaceutical MAOIs: The Two-MAOI Problem**

If ayahuasca already contains a built-in MAO inhibitor, what happens when a participant is also taking a pharmaceutical MAO inhibitor?

Pharmaceutical MAOIs fall into two categories. Reversible inhibitors of MAO-A (RIMAs) — such as moclobemide, used in some countries as an antidepressant — are pharmacologically similar in class to harmine and harmaline. Irreversible MAOIs — phenelzine, tranylcypromine, selegiline at higher doses — bind permanently to the MAO enzyme. Their effects last until the body synthesizes new enzyme, which takes approximately two weeks.

A participant on phenelzine is walking into ceremony with MAO-A inhibited at near-total levels before the brew is consumed. The harmine in the ayahuasca provides no additional MAO inhibition beyond what is already present, but the combined serotonergic load is at maximum: zero MAO-A activity, plus DMT's 5-HT2A agonism, plus THH's serotonin reuptake inhibition. Every mechanism driving serotonin up is fully active. The risk of serotonin syndrome in this configuration is not theoretical.

The washout required for irreversible MAOIs before working with any serotonergic medicine is a minimum of two weeks, often recommended as three in conservative clinical practice. This applies with full force to ayahuasca. There is no clinical framework in which an irreversible MAOI and ayahuasca are compatible in close temporal proximity.

For moclobemide and other RIMAs, the washout is shorter given reversibility — typically 24 hours. The concern is not eliminated by reversibility; it is modified. The pharmacological principle stands.

**Natural MAOIs With Ayahuasca: Potentiation of the MAOI Component**

The general supplement section flagged passionflower, rhodiola rosea, and Syrian rue as natural sources of MAO-inhibiting compounds. With psilocybin, these compounds add a serotonergic interaction layer that is serious enough to warrant caution.

With ayahuasca, the calculation changes again. Ayahuasca already contains beta-carboline MAOIs. Adding an additional source of MAO inhibition — passionflower's harmine and harman, rhodiola's MAO-inhibiting compounds, or most significantly Syrian rue (Peganum harmala, which contains high concentrations of harmine and harmaline) — does not simply add serotonergic concern. It potentiates the MAOI component of the preparation itself. The MAO inhibition becomes more complete, lasts longer, and produces a higher serotonergic load for a longer window.

Syrian rue deserves particular mention. It is sometimes used as a substitute for B. caapi in ayahuasca preparations, and is occasionally taken by participants who believe it will enhance the ceremony. What it actually does is stack a high-dose harmaline preparation on top of another harmaline preparation, with unpredictable effects on the intensity and duration of MAOI activity. This is not enhancement. It is pharmacological compounding of a mechanism that is already clinically significant.

The take: any natural source of beta-carboline alkaloids is contraindicated around an ayahuasca ceremony. Syrian rue, wild rue, passionflower taken in therapeutic doses, and rhodiola rosea all belong in this category.

---

**Antipsychotics: Blocking Grandmother**

Antipsychotic medications block the 5-HT2A receptor — the same receptor DMT activates. This is the pharmacological mechanism that makes antipsychotics effective at reducing psychotic symptoms. It is also why they reliably attenuate or eliminate the effects of all classical psychedelics.

This applies to ayahuasca through the same mechanism it applies to psilocybin. A participant on an antipsychotic will find the DMT component of the medicine blocked at the receptor level. The MAOI component remains fully active — harmine and harmaline will still inhibit MAO-A, still allow tyramine to enter circulation, still elevate serotonin through decreased breakdown. What the antipsychotic eliminates is the psychedelic effect.

The clinical implication is not just "the experience will be blunted." It is that the MAOI is still running, the serotonergic load is still elevated, and the protective 5-HT2A agonism that defines the therapeutic experience is absent. Antipsychotics do not make ayahuasca safe for someone who should not be taking it. They make it pharmacologically active in the wrong direction.

---

**Cardiovascular Medications**

The cardiovascular profile of ayahuasca, like psilocybin, includes elevated heart rate and blood pressure during the active experience. The MAOI component introduces an additional cardiovascular variable: tyramine sensitivity, which if triggered produces sympathetic nervous system activation and acute hypertensive response.

For participants on antihypertensive medications, antiarrhythmics, or other cardiac drugs, the same principle applies as in the general pharmaceutical discussion: the sustained cardiovascular demand requires clinical attention, particularly for anyone with existing cardiac vulnerability. Duration of the ayahuasca experience is typically 4 to 6 hours, similar to psilocybin, so this concern is not as prolonged as with mescaline.

Beta-blockers deserve specific note. These medications dampen the adrenergic response to the experience, which may reduce cardiovascular stress. But as noted in the general discussion, carvedilol (a commonly prescribed beta-blocker with alpha-blocking properties) has documented 5-HT2A activity and may attenuate the psychedelic effects through that mechanism. A participant on carvedilol arriving at ceremony may have a meaningfully different experience than expected, independent of set and setting.

Antihypertensive medications that work through alpha-2 adrenergic pathways (clonidine, methyldopa) interact with the adrenergic component of the ayahuasca experience in ways that are not well-characterized clinically. The general principle applies: existing cardiovascular pharmacology needs to be known and considered, not assumed to be neutral.

---

**Sedating Medications and Opioids**

The role of benzodiazepines as both a liability before ceremony and a potential emergency tool is the same with ayahuasca as with psilocybin. Benzodiazepines blunt the experience through GABAergic sedation without directly addressing the MAOI mechanism. If a benzodiazepine intervention is used to manage a difficult experience, the MAOI and its consequences persist. Dietary and drug interaction risks from the harmine/harmaline component do not disappear because the 5-HT2A-mediated experience has been attenuated.

Opioids in general carry more serotonergic risk in the context of active MAO inhibition than in other contexts. Meperidine is the hard stop, as noted. Other opioids vary: fentanyl, morphine, and buprenorphine carry lower serotonergic risk through this pathway than meperidine, but the MAOI context elevates concern across the opioid class. Any participant on opioid pain management should be flagged for careful clinical review before an ayahuasca context, not only for the serotonergic considerations but for the interaction between opioid sedation and a multi-hour immersive psychedelic experience.

---

**The Admixture Question**

Traditional ayahuasca preparations include two core plants: the B. caapi vine and the DMT-containing admixture plant. Many preparations include additional plants — admixtures that vary by tradition, by region, and by the curandero's specific practice. These may include Brugmansia (which contains anticholinergic tropane alkaloids — scopolamine, hyoscyamine, atropine), tobacco (nicotine plus other alkaloids), various Datura-related plants, and others.

The interaction profile of a preparation that includes Brugmansia is fundamentally different from a preparation that does not. Anticholinergic alkaloids produce tachycardia, urinary retention, confusion, and hyperthermia — an effect profile that overlaps with, and can be confused with, serotonin syndrome. Participants and facilitators who do not know what additional plants are in a preparation cannot fully anticipate the pharmacological environment.

This is not an argument against traditional preparations with admixtures. It is an argument for knowing what is in the cup, and for facilitators to be explicit about the preparation they are working with so that appropriate clinical considerations can be applied.

---

**Duration and the Day After**

Ayahuasca's active experience typically runs 4 to 6 hours, closer to psilocybin than to mescaline in duration. The acute MAOI window — during which tyramine restriction and drug interaction precautions apply — extends through harmine's clearance, generally within 12 to 24 hours of the last dose. Harmine's half-life is approximately 2 hours; tetrahydroharmine's is longer, around 11 hours.

The practical guidance: dietary restrictions and avoidance of serotonergic or sympathomimetic substances should be maintained through the day following ceremony. This is not paranoia. It is appropriate caution during the tail end of an active MAOI window.

The day after an ayahuasca ceremony is also not a clean pharmacological slate for other reasons. Integration is pharmacologically active, not just psychologically active. The return of normal MAO function and normal serotonin homeostasis takes time. Introducing medications, supplements, or substances that affect serotonin in the 24 hours following ceremony is not the same as introducing them a week later.

---

**The Reinstatement Problem**

One specific clinical situation merits explicit attention: a participant who has stopped their SSRI or antidepressant in preparation for ceremony.

Stopping SSRIs eliminates the pre-ceremony blunting effect, and some practitioners recommend discontinuation before psychedelic work. But the pharmacological picture is more complex for ayahuasca specifically. The 5-HT2A receptor upregulation that occurs after SSRI discontinuation — the receptor's rebound response after chronic downregulation — may actually increase sensitivity to DMT in ways that are unpredictable and difficult to dose for. And reinstatement of the SSRI after ceremony, before the ayahuasca experience has fully cleared and MAOI activity has resolved, reintroduces serotonergic activity into a system that has not yet returned to baseline.

Anyone who has stopped an SSRI for an ayahuasca ceremony and plans to restart should do so only after allowing sufficient time for the MAOI component to fully clear. This is not a detail that should be left to the participant to figure out on their own. It is a clinical conversation that requires the same precision as the pre-ceremony washout.

**Summary: Interaction Categories**

| Category | Risk Level | Mechanism | Clinical Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| SSRIs / SNRIs | Hard contraindication | Additive serotonergic mechanisms; SS risk is direct and mechanistic | Full washout required (2 wks most SSRIs; 5 wks fluoxetine) |
| Tricyclic antidepressants | Hard contraindication | MAOI + TCA = documented SS and hypertensive crisis | Full washout required |
| Pharmaceutical MAOIs (irreversible) | Hard contraindication | Two-MAOI system; complete MAO-A inhibition | Minimum 2–3 week washout |
| Pharmaceutical MAOIs (reversible/RIMAs) | High concern | Additive MAOI effect | 24–48h washout minimum |
| Tramadol | Hard contraindication | SRI + weak MAOI + active MAOI = SS pathway | Treat as serotonergic antidepressant |
| Meperidine (pethidine) | Hard contraindication | Documented severe SS with MAOIs | Absolute contraindication |
| Amphetamines / stimulants | Hard contraindication | MAOI + sympathomimetic = hypertensive crisis | No use around ceremony window |
| Lithium | Hard contraindication | Seizure risk (same as other classical psychedelics) | No combination |
| Antipsychotics | Major concern | Block DMT's 5-HT2A mechanism; MAOI still active | Blunts experience; does not neutralize MAOI risks |
| Mirtazapine | Major concern | 5-HT2A antagonism blocks experience | Same as antipsychotics re: receptor blockade |
| Other opioids (non-meperidine) | Moderate concern | Variable serotonergic properties; sedation risk | Case-by-case clinical review |
| Beta-blockers | Moderate concern | Dampens cardiovascular response; carvedilol has 5-HT2A activity | Know which one; generally not a hard stop |
| Benzodiazepines (routine) | Moderate concern | Blunts experience through sedation; MAOI still active | Avoid routine use; keep available as emergency tool |
| Syrian rue / passionflower / rhodiola | High concern | Potentiates MAOI component of preparation | Avoid in full window around ceremony |
| Tyramine-rich foods | Moderate–high concern | MAOI removes first-pass tyramine metabolism | Dietary restriction on ceremony day + day before |
| Natural SSRI supplements (SJW, 5-HTP, L-tryptophan) | High concern | Additive serotonergic load on active MAOI | Avoid; treat as serotonergic medications |
| Carvedilol specifically | Moderate concern | Alpha/beta blocker + 5-HT2A activity | May significantly attenuate experience |
