# Psilocybin Drug Interactions: Research Reference
## Part 4 Working Document

*Sources: Halman et al. (2023) systematic review, J Psychopharmacol [PMC10851641]; Nayak et al. (2021), Pharmacopsychiatry; multiple peer-reviewed supporting studies. Evidence tiers noted throughout.*

---

## Pharmacological Foundation (Why These Interactions Happen)

Understanding the interaction profile requires knowing how psilocybin actually works in the body.

**Psilocybin itself is pharmacologically inert.** After ingestion, intestinal alkaline phosphatase rapidly dephosphorylates it to **psilocin**, the active compound. Everything that follows is psilocin pharmacology.

**Psilocin's primary mechanism:** Agonism at 5-HT2A serotonin receptors, predominantly in cortical pyramidal neurons. This is the receptor responsible for the psychedelic experience. Secondary activity at 5-HT2C, 5-HT1A, and other serotonin receptor subtypes.

**Critical distinction from LSD:** Psilocin has weak serotonin reuptake inhibition (i.e., it mildly raises synaptic serotonin). LSD does not. This matters for serotonin syndrome risk.

**Psilocin does NOT bind dopamine receptors.** (LSD does. This is why the interaction profiles differ.)

**Metabolism:** Psilocin is metabolized primarily by aldehyde dehydrogenase, monoamine oxidase (MAO-A), and UGT glucuronidation enzymes (UGT1A9, UGT1A10, UGT1A6-1A8). CYP enzyme involvement is minor compared to LSD, which is heavily CYP-dependent. This narrows the CYP-based drug interaction profile significantly.

**Cardiovascular baseline:** Psilocybin reliably increases systolic blood pressure (~19 mmHg) and diastolic BP (~8.7 mmHg), and elevates heart rate. This is relevant for any medication affecting cardiovascular function.

## PRESCRIPTION MEDICATIONS

### HARD CONTRAINDICATIONS

---

#### Lithium

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Lithium carbonate, lithium citrate |
| **Mechanism** | Not fully elucidated. Proposed: (1) Both compounds independently increase cortical excitability — combined, this may cross the seizure threshold. (2) Chronic lithium reduces serotonin levels; in that low-serotonin state, psilocin as a 5-HT2A agonist may produce exaggerated receptor activation. (3) Possible pharmacokinetic interaction altering plasma levels of one or both compounds. |
| **Clinical effects** | Seizures (documented). Cardiac complications including cardiac arrest (case reports). Nayak et al. (2021, Johns Hopkins): in an analysis of online psychedelic experience reports, 47% of lithium + classic psychedelic combinations were associated with seizures; 39% required medical attention. Of 6 psilocybin-specific lithium reports, 2 (33%) involved seizures. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 2–3 (case series + internet report analysis; no controlled RCT — ethically impossible) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Hard contraindication. Do not combine.** |

---

#### MAOIs (Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Phenelzine (Nardil), Tranylcypromine (Parnate), Isocarboxazid (Marplan), Selegiline (Eldepryl/Emsam), Moclobemide (reversible MAO-A inhibitor) |
| **Mechanism** | MAO-A is involved in psilocin degradation. MAO inhibition slows psilocin clearance and elevates synaptic serotonin systemically. Combined with psilocin's own weak serotonin reuptake inhibition and direct 5-HT2A agonism, this creates conditions for excessive serotonergic activation. **Important nuance:** Human studies of MAOIs + LSD (a related compound) paradoxically showed MAOIs *attenuating* LSD effects — likely because elevated endogenous serotonin competes at 5-HT2A receptors. However, serious serotonin toxicity has been documented with 5-MeO-DMT + β-carboline MAOIs (one fatal case). The picture is not simple. |
| **Clinical effects** | Theoretical serotonin syndrome: agitation, tachycardia, hyperthermia, diaphoresis, hypertension, muscle rigidity — potentially life-threatening. No direct controlled psilocybin + MAOI human studies exist. Note: ayahuasca *is* a MAOI + DMT combination by design; it is a different pharmacological situation from pharmaceutical MAOIs + psilocybin. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (mechanistic + indirect evidence from related compounds; case reports from 5-MeO-DMT) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Contraindicated. Washout period required — 2 weeks minimum for irreversible MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine); 24 hours for moclobemide.** |

### MAJOR INTERACTIONS — CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

---

#### SSRIs (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Fluoxetine (Prozac), Sertraline (Zoloft), Escitalopram (Lexapro), Citalopram (Celexa), Paroxetine (Paxil), Fluvoxamine (Luvox) |
| **Mechanism** | **Primary concern:** Chronic SSRI use causes downregulation and desensitization of 5-HT2A receptors — psilocin's primary target. Blunted receptor density means blunted psychedelic response. **Secondary concern:** SSRIs block serotonin reuptake; psilocin has its own weak reuptake inhibition; theoretically, the combined serotonergic load could produce serotonin syndrome, though this appears rare in practice. |
| **Clinical effects** | Attenuation or blunting of psychedelic effects in approximately 50% of users (retrospective survey, Gukasyan et al. 2023). Becker et al. (2022) RCT: 14-day escitalopram pretreatment reduced *negative* effects (anxiety, ego dissolution anxiety) without eliminating therapeutic effects — but 14 days does not reflect years of clinical SSRI use. The Goodwin et al. (2023) study of treatment-resistant depression patients on chronic SSRIs found 42% showed depression improvement with psilocybin; two serious cardiovascular events (hypertension) requiring clonidine occurred. Serotonin syndrome from psilocybin + SSRI: appears rare but has been reported in mushroom poisoning case reports. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 1–2 (RCT for escitalopram; open-label trial; retrospective survey) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Significant interaction. Primary risk is therapeutic attenuation. Serotonin syndrome risk is real but appears lower than theoretical concern suggests. Clinical decisions about SSRI tapering before psilocybin use should involve the prescribing physician.** |

---

#### SNRIs (Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Venlafaxine (Effexor), Duloxetine (Cymbalta), Desvenlafaxine (Pristiq), Milnacipran (Savella) |
| **Mechanism** | Dual reuptake inhibition of serotonin and norepinephrine. Similar 5-HT2A downregulation effect as SSRIs, with additional noradrenergic load. Psilocybin's cardiovascular stimulation (elevated BP, HR) may be amplified by noradrenergic potentiation. |
| **Clinical effects** | Expected to be similar to SSRIs (blunting of effects) with potentially greater cardiovascular concern due to noradrenergic component. No direct psilocybin + SNRI human trials. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (mechanistic extrapolation from SSRI data) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Interaction expected; manage similarly to SSRIs with added cardiovascular caution.** |

---

#### TCAs (Tricyclic Antidepressants)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Amitriptyline, Imipramine, Clomipramine, Nortriptyline, Doxepin, and others |
| **Mechanism** | Block serotonin AND norepinephrine reuptake (similar to SNRIs) but also antagonize histamine H1, muscarinic acetylcholine, and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors — and critically, **block cardiac sodium channels** causing QT prolongation and arrhythmia risk. Psilocybin's cardiovascular stimulation on top of a drug with cardiac conduction effects is the central concern. |
| **Clinical effects** | Broader and less predictable side effect profile than SSRIs. Cardiovascular risk (QT prolongation, arrhythmia). Potential serotonin excess. No direct psilocybin + TCA controlled studies. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (mechanistic) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Contraindicated — particularly given cardiac risk. Do not combine.** |

---

#### Tramadol

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Tramadol (Ultram) |
| **Mechanism** | Weak opioid agonist + serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor + weak MAOI-like properties. Already associated with serotonin syndrome in combination with SSRIs alone. When added to psilocin (5-HT2A agonist with weak reuptake inhibition), the combined serotonergic burden increases the theoretical risk substantially. Also a CYP2D6 substrate, which may affect psilocin metabolism. |
| **Clinical effects** | Serotonin syndrome risk. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (tramadol's independent serotonin syndrome risk is Tier 1; application to psilocybin is mechanistic) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid. Unusually high serotonin syndrome risk profile for an OTC-adjacent analgesic.** |

---

#### Antipsychotics (5-HT2A Antagonists)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs (effect-blocking)** | Risperidone (Risperdal), Olanzapine (Zyprexa), Chlorpromazine, Quetiapine (Seroquel), Pipamperone |
| **Mechanism** | These drugs block 5-HT2A receptors — psilocin's primary target. They essentially occupy the receptor before psilocin can. |
| **Clinical effects** | Dose-dependent attenuation or complete blockade of psychedelic effects. Risperidone: RCT demonstrated dose-dependent attenuation (Vollenweider 1998). Chlorpromazine: blocked pupil dilation and visual distortion (RCT). Clinically relevant in two directions: (1) these drugs will prevent or reduce psychedelic experiences; (2) they are therefore useful in emergency management. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 1 for risperidone and chlorpromazine; Tier 3 for olanzapine/quetiapine (mechanistic extrapolation) |

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs (paradoxical effects)** | Haloperidol (Haldol) — D2 antagonist, weak 5-HT2A activity |
| **Mechanism** | Haloperidol primarily blocks D2 receptors; psilocin does not bind dopamine receptors. The interaction is indirect via dopaminergic modulation of psilocybin's cortical effects. |
| **Clinical effects** | RCT (Vollenweider 1998, N=15): haloperidol *reduced* oceanic boundlessness and ego dissolution — but *increased anxiety* — without affecting visual hallucinations. This is clinically important: haloperidol is not a reliable rescue medication and may worsen the experiential component people are most distressed by. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 1 |

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Antipsychotics in the 5-HT2A antagonist class (risperidone, olanzapine) will blunt or block psilocybin effects. Haloperidol is an unreliable emergency intervention and may worsen anxiety. Avoid routine combination; know this for emergency management context.** |

---

#### Mirtazapine (NaSSA Antidepressant)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Mirtazapine (Remeron) |
| **Mechanism** | Potent 5-HT2A and 5-HT2C antagonist, plus H1 antagonism. Named explicitly in the Halman et al. systematic review as an agent predicted to "diminish the effects of psychedelics" via 5-HT2A blockade. |
| **Clinical effects** | Would be expected to blunt or block psilocybin effects. However: mirtazapine has been used intentionally post-ceremony to abort difficult experiences — this is community practice, not controlled evidence. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (mechanistic; named in systematic review) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid before ceremony. Potentially useful as post-experience intervention if difficulty arises — but this is not medical advice, it is a clinical discussion point.** |

---

#### Triptans (5-HT1B/1D Agonists — Migraine Medications)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Sumatriptan (Imitrex), Zolmitriptan (Zomig), Rizatriptan (Maxalt), and other "-triptans" |
| **Mechanism** | Serotonin receptor agonists at 5-HT1B/1D (and some activity at 5-HT1A). Psilocin has broad serotonin receptor activity beyond 5-HT2A. Theoretical serotonin syndrome risk from combined serotonergic agonism. Additionally, triptans are vasoconstrictive; psilocybin also causes vasoconstriction. |
| **Clinical effects** | Community experience (cluster headache patients) consistently reports triptans blocking psychedelic effects. Theoretical serotonin syndrome risk. Combined vasoconstriction. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 4 (community reports; no controlled studies) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid. Likely to block effects; possible serotonin and cardiovascular concerns.** |

---

#### Mood Stabilizers / Anticonvulsants

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Valproate (Depakote), Carbamazepine (Tegretol), Lamotrigine (Lamictal) |
| **Mechanism** | These agents reduce cortical excitability (GABA enhancement, sodium channel blockade). Psilocybin enhances cortical excitability as part of its mechanism. Expected pharmacodynamic antagonism. Note: **Lamotrigine specifically was studied by Nayak et al. (2021) and did NOT show the same seizure association as lithium** — suggesting it may have a safer interaction profile than lithium, despite also being a mood stabilizer. |
| **Clinical effects** | Likely attenuation of psychedelic effects. Seizure risk much lower than with lithium (evidence-based). |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 for valproate/carbamazepine; Tier 2 for lamotrigine (Nayak 2021 analysis) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Expect blunted effects. Lamotrigine appears meaningfully safer than lithium for seizure risk.** |

---

#### Beta-Blockers

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Propranolol, Metoprolol, Atenolol, Bisoprolol; **Carvedilol** (special case) |
| **Mechanism** | Beta-adrenergic receptor blockade attenuates the cardiovascular response to psilocybin (HR, BP elevation). **Carvedilol specifically** has documented 5-HT2A antagonist activity in vitro and in vivo — it would be expected to reduce psychedelic effects via 5-HT2A blockade, not just cardiovascular effects. |
| **Clinical effects** | May blunt cardiovascular response (potentially protective in some patients). Carvedilol may reduce therapeutic psychedelic effects. Theoretically could cause paradoxical hypertension (conflicting cardiovascular signals) in some scenarios. No controlled psilocybin + beta-blocker studies. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3; Carvedilol mechanism is Tier 2 (in vitro/in vivo receptor data) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Standard beta-blockers: uncertain but likely lower risk. Carvedilol: additional concern for effect blunting via 5-HT2A. Medical monitoring of BP/HR recommended.** |

---

#### Benzodiazepines

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Diazepam (Valium), Lorazepam (Ativan), Alprazolam (Xanax), Clonazepam (Klonopin) |
| **Mechanism** | Positive allosteric modulators at GABA-A receptors. CNS depression. No direct pharmacodynamic interaction with serotonergic system; mechanism of psychedelic attenuation is likely general sedation and anxiolysis rather than receptor-specific antagonism. |
| **Clinical effects** | Blunting and potential termination of psychedelic experience. Community experience is consistent: benzodiazepines can reduce intensity of difficult experiences. Clinical use as emergency intervention is established in psychedelic-assisted therapy protocols. Regular use before ceremony likely reduces therapeutic benefit. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3–4 (community experience; protocol guidance; no blinded RCT) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid routine pre-ceremony use. Recognized as emergency intervention tool in clinical protocols.** |

---

#### NRI/NDRIs

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Bupropion (Wellbutrin), Methylphenidate (Ritalin/Concerta) |
| **Mechanism** | Norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibition. Psilocin does not bind dopamine receptors, so direct dopaminergic interaction is unlikely. However, bupropion lowers the seizure threshold; combined with psilocybin's cortical excitability effects, this is a theoretical concern. Methylphenidate adds stimulant cardiovascular effects to psilocybin's existing BP/HR elevation. |
| **Clinical effects** | No direct studies. Bupropion: seizure threshold concern. Methylphenidate: additive cardiovascular stimulation. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Insufficient data. Use caution; consult prescribing physician.** |

---

#### Ondansetron vs. Metoclopramide (Anti-nausea — Important Distinction)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Ondansetron (Zofran)** | 5-HT3 receptor antagonist. Acceptable for nausea management during psilocybin; no significant interaction with psilocin's 5-HT2A activity. |
| **Metoclopramide (Reglan)** | Dopamine antagonist with antipsychotic-class properties. Should be avoided — it shares enough receptor activity with antipsychotics to risk blunting effects or producing extrapyramidal effects. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 2–3 |

## OTC MEDICATIONS

---

#### First-Generation Antihistamines

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Diphenhydramine (Benadryl), Chlorpheniramine (Chlor-Trimeton) |
| **Mechanism** | H1 receptor antagonism; significant anticholinergic effects; blood-brain barrier penetration producing CNS depression and delirium-like states at higher doses. Adding CNS anticholinergic effects to a psychedelic experience creates an unpredictable, potentially distressing altered state. |
| **Clinical effects** | Increased dizziness, confusion, worsened anxiety and paranoia. Unpredictable experiential effects. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3–4 |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid. No clear benefit; meaningful risk of worsening experience quality.** |

---

#### Dextromethorphan (DXM — cough syrups, NyQuil)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Found in Robitussin DM, NyQuil, and many OTC cough preparations |
| **Mechanism** | NMDA receptor antagonist (dissociative) + weak serotonin reuptake inhibitor + CYP2D6 substrate. The CYP2D6 overlap with psilocin metabolism could alter plasma levels of one or both. The serotonin reuptake inhibition adds to psilocin's own weak reuptake activity. NMDA antagonism adds a dissociative layer to a serotonergic psychedelic experience. |
| **Clinical effects** | Combined dissociative + psychedelic effects (unpredictable and potentially distressing). Theoretical serotonin syndrome risk. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (mechanistic) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid.** |

---

#### Pseudoephedrine / Phenylephrine (Decongestants)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Sudafed, DayQuil, many cold/sinus preparations |
| **Mechanism** | Sympathomimetic agents; adrenergic stimulation increasing heart rate and blood pressure. Additive to psilocybin's known cardiovascular stimulation (~19 mmHg systolic elevation). |
| **Clinical effects** | Risk of amplified hypertension, tachycardia. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid, especially in anyone with cardiovascular risk factors.** |

---

#### NSAIDs (Ibuprofen, Naproxen, Aspirin)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | COX inhibition; no significant interaction with serotonergic or dopaminergic systems. Limited pharmacokinetic overlap with psilocin. |
| **Clinical effects** | No significant pharmacodynamic interaction with psilocybin expected. Aspirin's antiplatelet effects are theoretically irrelevant to the interaction profile. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 (absence of expected interaction) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Low concern. Not a meaningful contraindication.** |

---

#### Omeprazole and PPIs (Proton Pump Inhibitors)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Drugs** | Omeprazole (Prilosec), Pantoprazole, Esomeprazole |
| **Mechanism** | Chronic PPI use can cause hypomagnesemia. Low magnesium increases arterial reactivity to vasoconstriction. Psilocybin causes vasoconstriction. The theoretical concern is amplified vasoconstrictive effects in chronically magnesium-depleted patients. |
| **Clinical effects** | Theoretical and indirect. Clinically relevant mainly in patients on long-term PPI therapy. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 4 (very indirect mechanistic chain) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Low direct concern; worth noting for patients on long-term PPI therapy.** |

## SUPPLEMENTS

---

#### St. John's Wort (Hypericum perforatum)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Active compounds hyperforin and hypericin inhibit serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine reuptake (SSRI-like activity). Additionally, St. John's Wort is a potent inducer of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and P-glycoprotein. Given psilocin's metabolism involves MAO and UGT enzymes (not primarily CYPs), the CYP induction concern is less acute than for many other drugs — but the serotonergic additive effect is the primary concern. Hyperforin has a ~5-day half-life; adequate washout requires 5+ days. |
| **Clinical effects** | Theoretical serotonin syndrome risk (additive serotonergic load). Possible alteration of psilocin metabolism (minor). |
| **Evidence** | Tier 2–3 (SJW's SSRI-like pharmacology is well-established; application to psilocybin is mechanistic) |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Discontinue at least 5–7 days before. Treat similarly to an SSRI.** |

---

#### 5-HTP (5-Hydroxytryptophan)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Direct serotonin precursor; increases serotonin synthesis. Raises the serotonergic floor before adding psilocin's 5-HT2A agonism and weak reuptake inhibition on top. |
| **Clinical effects** | Theoretical serotonin excess; could produce serotonin syndrome or paradoxically blunt effects by flooding receptors with endogenous serotonin. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid.** |

---

#### Kava Kava

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Kavalactones modulate GABA-A receptors and voltage-gated sodium channels; sedative/anxiolytic. Inhibits CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP3A4. Additionally hepatotoxic in some individuals; psilocybin also increases liver enzyme activity. Combined hepatotoxic load is the primary concern beyond CNS sedation. |
| **Clinical effects** | CNS sedation may blunt experience. Hepatotoxic risk in combination. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3 |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid.** |

---

#### Cannabis

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | CB1/CB2 receptor agonism; multiple CNS effects. Psilocybin and cannabis share the ability to amplify anxiety and paranoia, and the combination is widely reported to intensify and destabilize the psychedelic experience. Cannabis can also trigger psychotic-spectrum symptoms in vulnerable individuals; combined with psilocybin, this risk compounds. Additionally, chronic cannabis use has been identified as a risk factor for HPPD. |
| **Clinical effects** | Intensified and less predictable experience; increased anxiety/paranoia; higher risk in those with psychotic vulnerability. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 3–4 |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid for at least 72 hours before. Particularly important for those with personal or family history of psychotic illness.** |

---

#### Valerian Root, Passionflower, Other Sedating Supplements

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | GABAergic sedation; CNS depression. |
| **Clinical effects** | May blunt experience. Unpredictable CNS combination. |
| **Evidence** | Tier 4 |
| **Clinical bottom line** | **Avoid near ceremony time.** |

## Key Clinical Notes for Chapter Writing

**1. Psilocybin's interaction profile is narrower than ayahuasca's.** Because psilocin is not primarily CYP-metabolized (unlike LSD or ayahuasca's harmine compounds), many of the dramatic pharmacokinetic drug interactions that apply to those compounds are less relevant here. The dominant concerns are pharmacodynamic (receptor-level) rather than metabolic.

**2. The dangerous interactions cluster around a short list.** Lithium is the hardest contraindication with the clearest evidence. MAOIs and TCAs are next — theoretical but mechanistically compelling and not worth testing. For the rest, the primary risk is usually *effect attenuation* rather than physical harm.

**3. Serotonin syndrome from psilocybin alone or with SSRIs appears to be rare.** The theoretical risk is real and should be disclosed. The clinical evidence, however, does not support treating every SSRI combination as acutely dangerous. The risk exists on a spectrum; the combination of multiple serotonergic agents simultaneously is where the risk becomes material.

**4. Evidence quality is generally low.** The honest disclosure: most of what we know about psilocybin drug interactions comes from controlled studies of *other* psychedelics (LSD, DMT, ayahuasca), small trials, case reports, and community surveys. This is not a gap in rigor — it reflects that until recently, clinical trials of any kind were legally impossible. The field is moving fast.

**5. "No interaction" does not mean "no effect."** NSAIDs and standard beta-blockers have no strong evidence of dangerous pharmacodynamic interaction — but the cardiovascular stimulation of psilocybin, combined with any agent affecting circulation, warrants noting.
