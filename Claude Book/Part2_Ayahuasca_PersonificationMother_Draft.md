# Why We Call Her Mother

*Part 2: The Substances — Ayahuasca chapter, aside*

Most people who sit with this medicine long enough start talking about her like she's a person. Grandmother Ayahuasca. Madre. She showed me this and knew exactly what I needed before I did. I used to hear that language and assume it was ceremonial color, something people say in the maloca that doesn't survive translation into a clinic. Fifteen years in, I take it more literally than that.

In the indigenous healing traditions this medicine comes from, grandmother is a specific position in a matriarchal social structure, earned the same way every elder earns it in that culture. She's the elder whose love for you was decided generations before you arrived and stays exactly where it started no matter what you do. That kind of standing is rare, and it's exactly what gives her the authority to say the things nobody else in your life has enough credit with you to say. Unconditional love and zero tolerance for your excuses come from that same authority, working in two directions at once.

The personification carries that same structure. She responds to what a person actually needs, regardless of what they walked in asking for. She tracks state in a way no plant compound should be able to on its own. Calling that "her," and specifically grandmother, is plain language for a pattern too consistent to be coincidence.

The maestros I trained under call it stern compassion. "I love you too much to let you keep lying." That line only works coming from someone with grandmother's standing. After fifteen years of watching people purge their own excuses in front of me, it's the most accurate clinical description I have for what happens in that room.

The pattern shows up most clearly around intention. People arrive wanting clarity on a marriage, a decision about a job, or permission to grieve someone properly. A grandmother who has walked three generations through the same mistakes already knows which question actually needs answering, and she starts there instead of wherever you started. Most people don't recognize the detour until they're standing in the middle of it. They come out having learned something they didn't know they needed before they ever get to the thing they came for. I have watched this run too many times across too many people to file it under coincidence.

It connects back to the spoon. Both the spoon and the intention sheet are plans handed to her. She has her own.

Call her Mother, or Grandmother, whichever is the more honest word for what happened in that room. You don't have to believe in her to stop arguing with it. Grandfather Huachuma earns a different kind of authority, and that's a conversation for his own chapter. The family resemblance is worth holding onto when we get there.
