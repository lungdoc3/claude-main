# Psilocybin

*Working Draft | Healing with Psychedelics: A Physician's Perspective*

Before she is a medicine, she is something else entirely.

She is infrastructure.

The fungal networks running beneath every forest floor on earth are doing something that no other living system does: delivering nutrients to the cellular level, removing waste, connecting life forms across distances that have no other bridge, and facilitating communication between organisms that have no other language. The trees are talking to each other. The fungi are the network. Not a metaphor for a network. The actual network, biological and ancient and operating with a sophistication that makes the internet look like a rough draft.

I know of no other life form that exists solely in service and support. Not partly in service. Not incidentally. Solely. The fungi take nothing for themselves that is not immediately returned. They are the most selfless organisms on earth, and they have been at this for longer than anything else currently alive.

This is who she is before she has anything to do with healing your depression or opening your heart or showing you the thing you have been avoiding for thirty years.

She has earned some respect.

## Many Faces

As a medicine, psilocybin wears more faces than any other plant teacher I have worked with.

Ayahuasca is ayahuasca. She does not negotiate her essential character. Wachuma arrives as Wachuma and leaves as Wachuma. There is range within each medicine, but there is also a recognizable identity, a core quality you learn to know the way you learn to know a person.

Psilocybin is different. She is the same medicine at every dose and an almost entirely different experience depending on how much of her you bring. The doorways of perception to which she holds the keys are numerous, varied, and genuinely accessible. Not a single door she opens reluctantly. A whole building of doors, each one leading somewhere real.

She also does not trigger the reflex response that the word psychedelic has trained most people to associate with the category. The anxiety, the brace-for-impact that someone brings to their first conversation with a powerful medicine: she does not call that forward the way the others can. There is a comfort about her. A quality of welcome. People arrive softer, more open, less defended. And that quality of arrival matters enormously to what becomes available.

## The Dial

Here is the thing that makes psilocybin unique among everything I have worked with, and the thing that makes her so important therapeutically.

She is titratable.

Every other medicine in this conversation operates in a register that is largely fixed once you have stepped into it. Ayahuasca drives the bus. You can make choices about how you prepared, what intention you carry, whether you resist or surrender. But the medicine determines the territory. The dosing is almost beside the point: as one of my teachers told me, we just open the door. Doesn't matter if it's a crack or wide open. She's coming in.

Wachuma is not a medicine that invites control. She is too generous for that. Too expansive. You can walk around in her experience and find things, but she is not asking for your input on what the experience will be. She does not do request lines.

Psilocybin hands you a dial.

At a low dose, she shows up much like Wachuma: gentle, open, present, unhurried. Laughing. She invites you to look at what is actually around you with new attention. She softens the edges of things without removing them. She is good company.

At a high dose, she moves into territory that is recognizably Ayahuasca's: immersive, interior, capable of profound revelation and genuine confrontation with what you have been carrying. She will take you somewhere. You will not be steering.

And in between: everything. Every floor of the building is accessible. You can work at whatever level matches what the person needs, what the moment calls for, what the therapeutic relationship can hold. That is not something any other medicine offers. It is not a small distinction. In a therapeutic context, it is the difference between having one tool and having a full set.

The magic, if you are genuinely curious, lives in the middle floors. The places between the comfortable and the overwhelming, where the real conversation happens and where something can shift that doesn't shift anywhere else.

## What She Knows

If Ayahuasca is my main teacher, psilocybin is the one who knows where everything in the school is stored. And how to get it.

That is a specific kind of intelligence. Not the teacher's authority. Not the grand curriculum with its demands and its revelations delivered on schedule. Something more intimate than that. The person who has been in every room in the building. Who knows which doors are unlocked, which archive holds what, who can get you where you actually need to go with a minimum of ceremony and a certain amount of laughter.

She has been human's partner in this work longer than almost anything else. Over two hundred species of psilocybin-containing mushrooms, distributed across every inhabited continent, finding their way into the hands and the ceremonies and the consciousness of people who had no contact with each other and arrived at the same understanding independently: this one matters. This one knows things.

There is a reason she appears in the oldest artistic record of human experience. There is a reason she was woven into the foundations of cultures that had never met. She did not wait to be discovered. She was already present, already doing what she does, already holding open the doors that most of human experience keeps firmly closed.

She does not need our validation.

## The Smoke

I had heard for years about people who claimed to know someone who sat with ayahuasca and felt nothing. I always dismissed it. Until I watched a woman stay completely sober through two full nights of good medicine. She broke open the next day with breathwork. I filed it away without understanding what it was telling me.

Then a medicine carrier referred a woman to me. The story was that she had sat with ayahuasca before and nothing had happened. She had subsequently worked with psilocybin, had a profound experience, felt like she had moved what needed to move, and was now ready to revisit ayahuasca. Everything checked out. I made certain her preparation was right, the medicine was right, the container was right. There was no reason this wouldn't work.

She was ninety-eight pounds. She drank two generous cups.

Nothing.

So I asked Grandmother what was happening.

She explained it to me the way you explain something to a child who should have known better than to ask. She showed me the walls this woman had built around herself. Not a fence. A fortress. Moats, drawbridges, boiling oil. The full architecture of someone who had learned that survival required keeping everything out. The walls were not a symptom. The walls were the person. Lose the walls, lose everything. There was no identity waiting on the other side of them.

Ayahuasca could bring those walls down, Grandmother told me. She could do it. But what was on the other side would not survive the demolition.

Fair play, I told her. Subtle you are not.

But then I asked the question that had been forming: why had psilocybin been able to reach her when ayahuasca could not?

And Grandmother showed me something I have not forgotten.

Psilocybin can be ephemeral. Smoky. She does not arrive at the gate demanding entry. She finds the places where the mortar is thin, where there is the smallest gap between stones, and she moves through them without disturbing what holds them together. She reconstitutes herself on the other side. Inside the walls.

And from inside, she can begin to do what no force from outside could accomplish.

It is always easier to open doors from within than to break them down from the outside.

This is old wisdom. Not the playful best-friend energy I associate with working with her myself, but something considerably older and more patient. A knowledge of human architecture, of how people defend themselves, of where the access is that they do not know they have left.

Ayahuasca arrives with power and announces herself. She is not wrong to do so. But not every person, at every moment in their life, can survive that announcement.

Psilocybin knows this. She has always known this. She has been finding her way through human defenses, gently, without force, for longer than we have had words for what she does.

The fungi built the network that feeds every root in the forest. They did not build it by force. They built it by finding, over millions of years of patient work, exactly where the pathways were.

She brings that same intelligence to the work of healing.

## Going on an Adventure

I want to be honest about what this medicine feels like to me personally, because I think it matters that a physician who works in this field is willing to say it plainly.

Working with psilocybin, at the doses and in the contexts I use her, feels like sneaking out of the house to go on an adventure with my best friend who makes me laugh.

Not the solemnity of the night ceremony. Not the sustained, luminous opening of a Wachuma day. Something more mischievous than either. A shared delight. A quality of knowing that I am going somewhere interesting with someone who knows the way and is enjoying the fact that I do not yet know what we are about to find.

The laughter is real. Not the nervous laughter of someone uncomfortable with where they are. The laughter of genuine recognition, of ideas meeting each other unexpectedly and producing something neither had access to alone.

But underneath the mischief, the old wisdom is always there. The intelligence that has been navigating human consciousness since before we had language for it. The patience of something that has never needed to force a door because it has always known where the doors are.

She is playful and she is ancient and she holds both of those things simultaneously without any apparent tension between them.

The service network beneath every forest floor on earth has been feeding the roots, clearing the waste, carrying the messages for hundreds of millions of years without asking for anything in return.

And when she turns her attention toward a human nervous system, she brings that same quality of care.

She already knows what you need.

She has always known where it is stored.

## How They Work Together

I have worked with all three of these medicines long enough to understand how they relate to each other, and I want to say something about that from my own experience rather than from theory.

Grandmother Ayahuasca opens big, heavy doors. Behind those doors lie vast, largely unexplored landscapes. The work is big and the lessons are big, and they are not always personal in the ordinary sense. She does not limit herself to your individual story. She will take you to places that feel much larger than one person's biography, and she will show you things that have nothing immediately to do with the life you went in hoping to sort out. She operates at whatever scale she decides the moment requires. You do not negotiate that with her.

Wachuma comes in after. Especially after an intense run with Grandmother. He does what he does: he warms what got cold, opens what got armored, reminds the body that it is safe to feel things again. He is extraordinarily well suited to the particular tenderness that follows Aya's work. If she is the surgery, he is the recovery room, and he is very good at his job.

And then there is psilo.

My buddy and I go exploring the new landscape together. The doors Grandmother opened are still open. The territory is different than it was before. And the two of us, psilo and I, move through it the way you move through somewhere you have not been yet: carefully in some places, with total abandon in others. It can be spooky. It can be breathtaking. Occasionally both at the same time. It is always an adventure, and I mean that in the truest sense of the word.

We are, I have come to understand, something like the yard cleanup crew.

Not glamorous. Not always dramatic. But the work we do together in that territory, after the big doors have been opened and the heart has been warmed back open, is some of the most useful work I have ever done. The kind of work that looks, from the outside, like wandering, and feels, from the inside, like exactly what needed to happen next.
