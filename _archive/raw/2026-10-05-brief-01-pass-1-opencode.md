**1.**

**STATEMENT.** The human side of the relationship is limited to serial, symbolic, voluntary output because the keyboard was inherited from the typewriter, not chosen for coupling to a machine.

**ORIGIN.** Sholes's typewriter, 1874, established hand keying as the office's text interface. The Teletype ASR-33 (1963) fused that keyboard to telegraphy and became the standard time-sharing terminal. Computing adopted a device built for writing letters, not for conversing with a processor.

**STATUS.** Implicit. Typing already meant writing, so the machine adopted the office's existing tool without argument.

**COST.** It forecloses any human output that is not symbol production. Pressure, posture, gesture, and continuous movement carry no traffic because no inherited device encoded them.

**FALSIFICATION.** Sutherland's Sketchpad (1963) and Engelbart's chord keyset show that pointing and chording were live alternatives at the same moment. Had either become default, the keyboard would not read as natural.

**CONFIDENCE.** High. The inheritance chain is documented. Raised by a source showing the ASR-33 keyboard selected through explicit comparison.

**2.**

**STATEMENT.** The machine-to-human channel is a screen the human watches because the relationship was first built for radar operators supervising an automated system, not for partners exchanging information.

**ORIGIN.** The Semi-Automatic Ground Environment (SAGE) air-defense system, operational 1958, gave operators a cathode-ray-tube display and a light gun. This was the first mass human-computer interaction and it fixed the role: the machine displays a state, the human supervises and intervenes.

**STATUS.** Implicit. SAGE was built for threat interception; the operator-supervisor role entered the general interface as an unexamined default.

**COST.** It forecloses the machine as anything but a reporter of its own state. The human is positioned outside, watching, rather than coupled. The exchange is framed as supervision, not conversation.

**FALSIFICATION.** If the dominant early interaction model had been Licklider's symbiosis (1960) rather than the command-and-control terminal, watching a screen would not be the default posture.

**CONFIDENCE.** Medium. SAGE's influence is well documented; its propagation into civilian interfaces is inference.

**3.**

**STATEMENT.** The relationship is treated as a transmission channel whose task is to move symbols accurately, because Shannon excluded meaning from the founding theory and the industry inherited only the transmission layer.

**ORIGIN.** Claude Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948), declared semantic aspects "irrelevant to the engineering problem." Warren Weaver (1949) located semantics (Level B) and effect on conduct (Level C) as unsolved. Computing inherited Level A as if it were the whole problem.

**STATUS.** Explicit at the theory's origin; implicit in the industry, which never returned for B and C.

**COST.** It forecloses measuring whether meaning arrived or conduct changed. The architecture guarantees characters delivered and nothing else, so the relationship is engineered to a solved layer and blind to the open ones.

**FALSIFICATION.** If an interface standard ever required a measure of comprehension or effect rather than bit accuracy, the exclusion would be shown as a choice, not a boundary.

**CONFIDENCE.** High on the history; medium that the exclusion is why interfaces ignore B and C.

**4.**

**STATEMENT.** The human was framed as a "user" consuming a shared resource, which cast the relationship as provision rather than partnership.

**ORIGIN.** The Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS) at MIT, 1961 to 1965, and its Project MAC expansion introduced the "user" who dials into a shared machine for a slice of its time. The term fixed a role: the human requests service, the machine allots it.

**STATUS.** Implicit. "User" was operational convenience that hardened into identity.

**COST.** It forecloses the human as co-equal or co-evolving. A user is a customer of capability, not a counterpart, and the language still shapes what gets built.

**FALSIFICATION.** The term's persistence is observable; the claim that it constrains design would be shown false if "user"-centered systems still produced co-evolutionary outcomes.

**CONFIDENCE.** Medium. The term's history is certain; its causal weight is inference.

**5.**

**STATEMENT.** The human was made the fixed term of the pairing and the machine the only thing permitted to change, because the ease-of-use branch defeated Engelbart's co-evolution branch on training cost.

**ORIGIN.** Douglas Engelbart, "Augmenting Human Intellect" (1962), and the 1968 demo proposed the human changing too; his chord keyset took weeks to learn. The commercial branch that followed kept the human untrained and pushed adaptation onto the machine.

**STATUS.** Implicit. The fork was a commercial decision, defensible on cost, never restated as a claim about human nature.

**COST.** It forecloses the human as a developing side of the relationship. Every improvement since targets the machine because the other side was ruled out of scope.

**FALSIFICATION.** It would be false if the trained-human branch were shown viable at scale: a commercial system requiring human training would reveal "the human arrives unchanged" as a choice with a cost, not a boundary.

**CONFIDENCE.** High on the historical fork; low on whether it was the only viable branch.

**6.**

**STATEMENT.** The entire interface is organized around producing and manipulating paper documents, an inheritance from print and office work that made the page, not the person, the unit of the relationship.

**ORIGIN.** Five centuries of print plus the nineteenth-century office: the keyboard (typewriter), the printer, the "file" and "folder" (manila and cabinet), the "page," the "desktop" (Xerox PARC, 1973). Each term names a paper object, not a human capacity.

**STATUS.** Implicit. No one chose the document as the relationship's goal; it was the ambient environment the machine was built into.

**COST.** It forecloses the nervous system itself as the addressed party. The machine's output is a page, an artifact, rather than a response to the person.

**FALSIFICATION.** If the dominant metaphor had been conversation or coupling rather than documents, the page would not structure the interface. A culture that produced computing from telephony rather than print would show the difference.

**CONFIDENCE.** Medium. The vocabulary is evident; that it constrains the relationship is inference.

**7.**

**STATEMENT.** The relationship was built as one human paired with one machine, a dyad, which made the unit of analysis the individual at a desk and left collective and extended cognition out of the architecture.

**ORIGIN.** The personal computer, from the Xerox PARC Alto (1973) through the Apple Macintosh (1984), committed to one operator, one screen, one desk. Time-sharing had been many humans on one machine; the PC inverted to one human on one machine.

**STATUS.** Implicit. "Personal" was a marketing and design claim that became a structural assumption.

**COST.** It forecloses relationships of many to many, and the nervous system as embedded in others and in tools. The human is addressed as isolated.

**FALSIFICATION.** Licklider's 1960 symbiosis and Engelbart's 1962 augmentation both framed individuals plus instruments plus others as one system. If that framing had won, the dyad would not be the default.

**CONFIDENCE.** High that the PC fixed the dyad; medium that it forecloses collective coupling.

**A. THE MISS.**

The relationship is conducted in the imperative mood: the human commands, the machine executes. Its origin is the stored-program computer itself, whose instruction set is a list of orders, compounded by the military command chain of the early funders. No other lens will surface it. Structural will map channels and bandwidth, not the illocutionary force of the traffic. Biological will place the relationship on the mutualism-to-parasitism axis, not the master-to-subordinate grammatical axis. Economic will trace who profited, not the command grammar inherited from the instruction set and the Pentagon.

**B. UNCERTAINTY.**

I am least sure of my foreclosure claims: that these inheritance chains still constrain the relationship rather than merely describe its etymology. "Came from" is not proof of "still constrains." Touchscreens and voice now exist, so the foreclosures are not total. The leap from a historical origin to a present constraint is the weak point in assumptions 1, 2, 6, and 7, and my medium confidences reflect it. History shows where things came from; it does not by itself show that the origin still binds.

**C. DISAGREEMENT WITH THIS BRIEF.**

The brief speaks of "the architecture" as if it were designed once by identifiable actors whose commitments could have been examined. The record shows no single architecture and no designer: the keyboard is 1874, the screen is 1958, the GUI is 1973, the personal computer 1984. The load-bearing assumptions are inherited constraints from telegraphy, typewriting, radar, and print, accreted rather than decided. Accretion cannot be revisited the way a decision can, so the premise that the architecture "carries assumptions never subsequently argued for" presumes a moment of architecture that never occurred.

The drift-versus-shift periodization understates the 1968 to 1984 window. The GUI and the personal computer changed who the human is (a "user" of a "personal" machine) even while the underlying channels held. That is a shift in the relationship's unit and frame, not drift.

The "nervous system" framing is anachronistic. None of the historical actors (Shannon, Licklider, Engelbart, Sutherland, the SAGE engineers) designed for a nervous system. They designed for an operator, a user, a writer, a pilot. The question projects a present-day biological vocabulary backward onto people who had no such category, which risks finding assumptions no one ever actually held.
